A Teenager’s Moral Responsibility

Details “I have used the word ‘attention,’ which I borrow from Simone Weil, to express the idea of a just and loving gaze directed upon an individual reality.” — Iris Murdoch

Introduction

A man walks into a room full of people who are strangers to him. He is quiet, perhaps a little bit awkward — the normative discomfort of navigating unfamiliar social terrain. Then, someone in the room decides, based on absolutely no certain information at all, that he is strange. Perhaps even unsettling. This judgement was made in seconds, almost unconsciously, and from that moment onward, a framework is erected around him that he does not know exists, cannot see, and perhaps most damningly, cannot do anything about. When he finally tries to make a joke, it is “trying too hard” or “conforming.” When he is kind, he is “suspicious” and “manipulative.” When he eventually withdraws from the situation due to his conscience having noticed the distinct feeling of being uninvited or unliked from the group, his withdrawal becomes the final confirmation: See? There was always something wrong with that guy. The architecture is now complete, and it is, by design, unfalsifiable — because every piece of evidence, no matter how contradictory, is metabolized into the same conclusion.

This is no rare occurrence. In fact, it is one of the most common and destructive patterns in social life in general. A woman is labeled “difficult” in her workplace on the basis of a single disagreement; and from that point on, her confidence is now “aggression,” her thoroughness is “controlling,” and her eventual frustration at being perpetually misread is taken as a fact that the people viewing her have always been right. A young man is rumored — perhaps by someone with a grudge, perhaps by someone who simply dislikes him — to have done something terrible; the rumor metastasizes, and suddenly his entire history is rewritten through the lens of the accusation. His friendliness becomes “predatory.” His protest becomes “defensiveness.” His silence becomes “guilt.” His life, as he had it, is now socially over — just because someone decided what he was.

This raises a particular question that I believe is too frequently ignored and passed on despite how important it is in our everyday lives: do we bear moral responsibility for how we perceive other people?

My answer is an unequivocal yes. In the following paragraphs, I will substantiate my claim through arguing that the act of perceiving another person — of constructing a narrative about who they are, what they intend, and what they deserve — is not simply an amoral psychological process but holds moral responsibility in and of itself, and that it carries genuine obligations for which we can be held genuinely accountable. The argument will proceed in four stages: first, I will examine the psychological mechanisms underlying the phenomenon and how it is definitively cruel; second, I will argue, through the concept of culpable ignorance and Daniel Kahneman’s framework of dual-process cognition, that these mechanisms, though automatic, are not involuntary, and that their operation does not absolve us of moral responsibility but in fact deepens it; third, drawing on the work of Iris Murdoch, I will argue that moral attention — the effort that one exerts to be able to see and understand the other person — is not merely a peripheral virtue but a foundational ethic; and finally, I will contend that the failure to exercise this attention constitutes a distinct and undertheorized form of moral harm — one that I will describe, following Miranda Fricker, as a kind of epistemic injustice — before addressing the deeper question of why people so reliably abdicate moral responsibility for the perceptions they construct.

The Architecture of Bias

The psychological mechanisms that are involved in this context are particularly well-documented in social psychology but are insufficiently examined in their moral dimensions. But to understand how a single impression can calcify into an unshakeable belief about another person’s character, one must understand the very machinery that produces it — specifically, heuristics.

Heuristics, as known in social psychology, are cognitive shortcuts. They are fast, automatic, and designed by our minds to be useful as they allow us to deal with our daily complicated social positions without being paralyzed by deliberation at every single instance. The thing is, that is exactly why they fail in person perception — they are designed for efficiency, not accuracy.

The most relevant heuristic here is what Kahneman and Tversky term the “representativeness heuristic:” the tendency to judge something by how closely it resembles the example that we conjure in our minds rather than the individual features of it. When using this on an object, like something that looks like a chair, it is fair to assume, perhaps even necessary to do so. But in truly seeing a person, the implication is flipped upon its head due to the fundamental difference in these situations. When we encounter another human being, the representativeness heuristic prompts us to perceive the person through preexisting categories of people in our minds rather than as a living, breathing, unique individual. The man in the opening example is quiet and awkward; the heuristic matches him to the prototype of “strange” or “unsettling,” and from that point forward, the cognitive machinery does the rest.

What the heuristic initiates, confirmation bias completes. Once the initial categorization is done, a second mechanism takes over — one that blocks off all future information from making any meaningful difference to the perception of the person. Confirmation bias makes people search for, interpret, and recall information in ways that confirm their preexisting beliefs. Contradictory evidence may not only be ignored but wrongly interpreted. If the hypothesis is that someone is abusive/cold, then their warmth/care becomes “performative” or “manipulative.” If the hypothesis is that someone is dishonest, then their transparency becomes “calculated.” Every attempt that a person could make to demonstrate any truths is subsequently absorbed back into the framework to prove it is right.

Effectively, the person is trapped in an epistemic prison that they themselves cannot actually do anything about: a constructed reality in which the person being perceived is trapped inside a narrative they did not author, cannot access, and are structurally prevented from escaping. Not only that, the prison itself is fundamentally self-reinforcing, as every future situation only serves to contribute to its walls.

The crucial feature of this prison is its unfalsifiability. In the philosophy of science, Karl Popper argued that a theory which cannot, even in principle, be proven wrong is not a genuine theory at all — it is a dogma. We can apply the same logic here: the framework works upon the subjective interpretation of the person only, and so any evidence that counts against it is merely reinterpreted to support the hypothesis. It is no longer a framework but rather a conscious projection of a stereotype. And yet the observer experiences it as objective reality. As simply seeing what is there.

Culpable Ignorance and the Obligation to Think

It is possible now for one to anticipate a basic response: that because these mechanisms are automatic and uncontrollable in our minds, the person who is performing these perceptions cannot reasonably be held morally responsible for their consequences. You cannot blame someone for a process they did not choose and cannot control. This is, in essence, an appeal to involuntariness: heuristics are reflexes of the mind, and we do not hold people responsible for their reflexes.

The reply, however seductive it is, is inherently wrong. The objection is as follows: as heuristics are automatic, they are therefore beyond our control — that their automaticity places them in the same moral category as a sneeze or a flinch. But this obviously does not follow. The question was never about whether the heuristic was automatic or not, but rather about whether we are beings who are capable of becoming aware, of noticing that a snap categorization has occurred, of interrogating whether it is fair — and we are. That capacity for awareness is not incidental to our cognition. It is one of its defining features.

A heuristic judgement is merely the beginning of the process of understanding a person — yet we treat it as the endpoint and do not recognize the nature of choices that we have when performing it. The judgement lands in consciousness: this person is strange, this person is threatening, something is off. At that moment, two paths are available. One can accept the output uncritically, allow it to settle into conviction, and proceed to interpret all subsequent information through its lens. Or, one can pause, register the judgement as a judgement rather than a fact, and ask what evidence it is actually based on, holding it provisionally while attending to the person in front of them. The first path requires no effort; the second requires considerable effort. But it is precisely that moral effort that makes the paths a moral choice rather than a moral inevitability.

So, the point is, the automaticity of heuristics does not grant them authority. A process that we have the capacity to notice, question, and override is not something that gets to be treated as a fact just because it came easily and quickly. To treat it as though it does — to allow the heuristic output to harden into settled perception without ever subjecting it to scrutiny — is to make a decision, even if it does not feel like one. It is the decision to let a cognitive shortcut, designed for efficiency rather than accuracy, serve as the finality of who another human being is. And that is not a decision one gets to make without morally paying for it.

This is where the concept of culpable ignorance becomes decisive. In discussions of moral responsibility, culpable ignorance refers to the ignorance for which one is morally responsible — not because they chose to be ignorant, but because they could have known better and failed to take the reasonable steps to do so. A drunk driver is not condemned because he intended to kill a person, but because he decided to drink, decided to drive, and failed to account for the worst possible situation happening despite the risks of his prior actions. The incapacity he had at the critical moment does not absolve him from the responsibility because that incapacity was culpable.

The same logic applies to the situation mentioned above. A person who places a human into a category and never revisits their perception of the human being, allowing confirmation bias to harden an initial impression without ever stopping to test it and construct a more accurate interpretation of the person, is not just making a cognitive error but committing culpable ignorance. They could have paused. They could have questioned whether their initial categorization was fair. They could have sought out disconfirming evidence, or taken seriously the other person’s own testimony about who they are. They are capable adults who can use their System 2, as is the case for any neurotypical adult, and yet they did not. Not because they could not, but because it was easier not to. Because the heuristic judgement felt right, and interrogating it would have required a level of effort, humility, and the uncomfortable possibility of discovering that they had been unjust.

And so the argument is formed: heuristics may explain how the failure of perception happens, but they do not absolve anyone from the moral responsibility they bear, according to the principle of culpable ignorance. Because the mechanism, though automatic, is not uncontrollable, and the failure to control it, when another person’s selfhood, mental health, and social relationships are at stake, is a failure for which you can and should be held to account.

Murdoch and the Ethics of Attention

To further the framework, we must now consider Iris Murdoch’s moral philosophy, as Murdoch provides the positive account of what the moral obligation identified above actually looks like in practice.

Murdoch argued that morality is not primarily a matter of action and choice but also one of perception and attention. One’s moral life, as Murdoch would have it, begins not at the moment of decision but long before — in the quality of regard we direct toward the world and, above all, toward other people. After all, “I have used the word ‘attention,’ which I borrow from Simone Weil, to express the idea of a just and loving gaze directed upon an individual reality.”

The defining word in this quote is “just.” Murdoch is not speaking of a sentimental or uncritical gaze but one that is disciplined, honest, and oriented toward seeing what is actually there rather than whatever our ego, preconceptions, and anxieties want us to find. This fair perception is something that she considers to be the foundational moral act — not merely because it leads to good actions, though it does, but because it is itself a form of moral goodness. To make the effort to see a person clearly, to resist every single psychological pull that one feels and to know them for who they actually are — is what love fundamentally means. As she writes elsewhere: “Love is the extremely difficult realization that something other than oneself is real.”

Murdoch’s term here for this practice is unselfing: the deliberate turning of one’s consciousness away from the self’s concerns, projections, and habitual categorizations and toward the reality of the other. It is fundamentally the act of overriding heuristic perception to effortfully understand and attend to the person. She arrives at the same point that Kahneman does, that our default mode of perception is reductive and self-serving, and that genuine moral perception requires the sustained, often uncomfortable effort of moving beyond it.

Murdoch illustrates this principle with a famous example. She imagines a mother-in-law, M, who finds her daughter-in-law, D, pert and juvenile, “lacking in dignity and refinement.” M’s perception of D is colored by prejudice — perhaps class-based, perhaps temperamental. In the terms of this essay, M has applied the representativeness heuristic — she has matched D to a prototype (“vulgar,” “common”) and is now perceiving her through that category rather than as an individual. But M, recognizing that her perception may be unjust, undertakes the difficult inner work of revising it. She engages, in Kahneman’s terms, System 2. She does not simply change her behavior toward D; she changes her way of seeing D. Over time, through sustained moral effort — through unselfing — M comes to see D as “not vulgar but refreshingly simple, not undignified but spontaneous.” No outward action has changed. D has not changed. What has changed is the quality of M’s attention — and this, Murdoch insists, is a genuine and significant moral achievement.

And this therefore exemplifies that the reversal of this act is also a reversal of moral success — a moral failing. M had the capacity to revise her perception, and she did. If she had refused to use her abilities to reinterpret and see D for who they really were, then she would not merely have been mistaken about D, but also culpably mistaken.

The Epistemic Prison and Its Injuries

Finally, Miranda Fricker’s concept of epistemic injustice provides a framework for understanding the specific harm inflicted by the mechanisms described above. There are two key forms that she addresses: testimonial injustice, in which prejudice causes a hearer to give a deflated level of credibility to a speaker’s word; and hermeneutical injustice, in which a gap in collective interpretive resources puts someone at an unfair disadvantage when it comes to making sense of their own social experiences.

Of course, both of these concepts have been demonstrated in the cases I have presented. When a person has been imprisoned within a false narrative, any attempts they may make on their own behalf — to explain, to correct, to say that is not who I am — are systematically discredited. Any defenses they have conjured for themselves have already been accounted for: their protest is “defensiveness,” their explanation is “manipulation,” their frustration is “proof” that the original judgment was correct. Despite having every piece of information one could possibly need to evaluate who they are, the speaker is fundamentally disallowed from doing so because their reasoning and words are now treated as untrustworthy.

But epistemic injustice only actually accounts for constituting the harm. The actual pain comes from consistently being misperceived and denied the ability to defend oneself and to prove oneself. Or, in other words, from being denied the very dignity of being treated as a human being. Not a saint, not a paragon of virtue, just a person whose actions are granted the deserved presumption of good faith that most people receive without ever having to earn it. Most people move through the world with this presumption intact. When they smile at someone, it is taken as friendliness. When they offer help, it is taken as kindness. When they are quiet, it is taken as quietness. They are, in other words, allowed to simply be — to act and to have those actions received at something close to face value, with the charitable interpretation that social life depends upon but rarely acknowledges.

The imprisoned individual has been stripped of this presumption entirely. For them, no matter what they do, they will never be faithfully interpreted. Every single gesture and word passes through the distorting lens of the framework before it reaches the perceiver, and what arrives bears little resemblance to the input. They cannot be kind, because their kindness is “manipulation.” They cannot be honest, because their honesty is “a performance.” They cannot be hurt, because their pain is “guilt” or “self-pity.” They are, in every sense possible, not seen — not because they are physically invisible but metaphysically so; the very person standing in front of the observer and what the observer actually sees are fundamentally different human beings.

There is a particular cruelty that deserves to be named. The cruelty of being punished not for what you have done but for what someone has decided you are. Yet, they are forced to carry the weight and meaning of a version of themselves that they had no say in, that they do not recognize. The weight is not dramatic; the weight is not singular. It is the cumulative weight of a thousand small interactions between others. The psychological toll of this is not incidental. It compounds with the number of interactions; it makes people begin questioning their own actions, their own words, focusing on what went wrong and what made people think differently about them, not realizing that there is nothing happening there. Because the reaction was never to them. It was to the construct. Over time, this can lead the person to doubt themselves, and it would take extraordinary psychological resilience not to begin wondering whether they might be right. Whether perhaps you are the person they seem to see. Whether your own understanding of yourself — your intentions, your character, the reasons behind your actions — might be less reliable than the consensus of those around you. The epistemic prison does not merely trap you and deny you the understanding you deserve; it begins to make the person within it doubt the very truth of who they know themselves to be.

The damage does not stop at the individual, though; it radiates outward, into the social world around them, poisoning relationships that the person inside the prison had no hand in contaminating. Friends begin to hear things. Not accusations, but merely impressions. A raised eyebrow. A careful tone. I don’t know, there’s just something about him. And now the friend is placed in an impossible position. They know this person, deeply, and their own experience contradicts what the world around them is saying, yet they have to weigh that against the social weight of the narrative. They may risk being considered as an extension of the doubted, of the shunned; they also have to fight against the issue of conformity, risk losing other friends, even though they understand and know, from direct personal experience, that it is wrong.

Some friends may resist. They hold to what they know, they defend the person, they refuse to let a framework constructed by others override the reality of their own experience. But it does not come without cost — because to defend someone who has been seen as a risk is, in and of itself, a risk. Why are you defending him? What does that say about you? The social penalty for dissenting from a consensus — even an unjust one — is real and significant, and not everyone is willing to pay it. And so, gradually, the circle contracts. People who once felt warmly toward the person begin to pull away — not because their own experience has changed, but because the social cost is too high. This comes back to bite the subject as well, as they cannot comprehend why their friends would suddenly do so, which adds to all the other mechanisms contributing to their self-doubt.

But just because these injuries are abstract, are unseen, are not physical does not mean they are justifiable in any sense at all. And so, treating them as though they are not harmful only because they are not recognized as such is a foundational error.

Conclusion

None of this is to say that first impressions are always wrong, or that every single negative judgement of another person is a projection. Some people are dishonest. Some are dangerous. Some are evil. The capacity that allows us to make assessments of character has obvious adaptive and social value. Exercising them is not, in itself, morally problematic.

What is problematic, however, is the refusal to hold said assessments accountable — to treat them as settled truths, to close oneself off from disconfirming evidence, to deny every piece of information that goes against their narrative. What is morally problematic, above all, is the abdication of moral responsibility for the act of perception itself — the comfortable fiction that we are merely seeing what is there, when in truth we are, at every moment, choosing what to see.

Murdoch understood that the moral life is a matter of the quality of attention we direct toward the world in the ordinary course of living — the slow, unglamorous, largely invisible work of seeing other people for who they truly are. It is difficult; it requires humility; it demands that we acknowledge our own limitations, which requires, perhaps, courage of the highest level.

But that is not to say that it is supererogatory; it is still what we owe to one another. Not as charity, not as a virtue, but as a basic requirement of moral life. To be a moral agent is to both act rightly and see rightly — or, at the very least, not act upon incomplete and interpretive information. The philosophical frameworks to understand why we must are well-established. What remains is the willingness — the moral willingness — to use them. To reject those premises, to reject those frameworks and settle into distorted perception is not merely a cognitive error. It is a moral failing at the most fundamental level. It is a failure of attention, a failure of justice, and, in Murdoch’s deepest sense, a failure of love.

The people we misperceive do not disappear. They continue to exist and endure every single narrative from every single person around them, something that they cannot escape. And so, the least we owe them, and to our own morals, is the willingness to look again.
