The Comprehensive Guide to DBT

Details The structuring of session time is the primary factor that distinguishes Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT) skills training from DBT individual psychotherapy. In individual therapy, the agenda is open and set by the client’s behavior since the last session. In skills training, the agenda is pre-set by the behavioral skill to be taught, creating a structured, classroom-like environment.

Pretreatment DBT

The Core Four-Part Session Structure

Every skills training session, regardless of length, adheres to a consistent four-part structure:

  1. Beginning Ritual (Mindfulness and logistics)

  2. Homework Review (Sharing of skills practice) Managing Homework

  3. Presentation of New Material (Teaching new skills) Skills training Procedures

  4. Closing “Wind-Down” (Emotional regulation and session closure)

Session duration varies by context. For adults with severe disorders (like BPD), sessions are typically 2.5 hours with a 15-minute break. This length is considered necessary; 1.5 hours is often insufficient, as it provides inadequate time for both homework review (50-60 mins) and new material (50-60 mins). Adolescent groups or groups for less severe disorders may last 90 minutes to 2 hours. Individual skills training is typically 45-60 minutes.

1. The Beginning Ritual

The session formally begins with a mindfulness practice led by the leader, often using a bell (three rings to start, one to end). The type of practice is varied weekly to cover all mindfulness skills. After the practice, each participant (including leaders) shares their experience, which provides an opportunity for the leader to offer corrective feedback.

This time is also used for logistics. Members who missed previous sessions can briefly share where they have been. Any group-level issues (announcements, lateness, not calling when absent) are addressed. This brief attention to therapy-interfering behaviors (no more than 5 minutes) is a crucial part of the session and should not be dropped.

2. The Homework Review

This phase is essential for skills acquisition. The leader goes around the circle and asks each member to share their efforts to practice skills during the week. This is not voluntary; waiting for volunteers wastes time. The “homework” (a term that should be discussed, as some clients find it demeaning) review is a powerful motivator. The sure knowledge that each client will be asked about their practice, and that not practicing will be analyzed, encourages effort. This part of the session takes precedence over any other task.

3. The Presentation of New Material

Following a 10-15 minute break, the next hour is devoted to teaching. The break is an important, unstructured time for clients to interact and fosters group cohesion independent of the leaders. Leaders should be alert during the break, as clients having a hard time may be at risk of leaving.

When a new module begins, the first 30 minutes of this teaching block are spent on the rationale for that module. The leader’s task is to convince clients that the skills are relevant, that learning them will improve their lives, and that they are capable of learning them.

A variation occurs in the final session of a module. No new material is presented. Instead, this time is used to:

  • Review all skills from the current module.

  • Review skills from previous modules.

  • Discuss pros, cons, and generalization of skills.

  • If clients are “graduating,” a positive sendoff ritual is held, which includes certificates, a card with personal notes from leaders, and an opportunity for each member to say goodbye.

4. The Closing “Wind-Down”

The final 5-15 minutes are critical for clients with emotion dysregulation. Skills sessions can be emotionally activating, and this period helps clients regulate their affect and “close up” the session before leaving. It also allows any clients who dissociated to “come back” to the room.

  • “Observe and Describe” Wind-Down: The most popular method. Each member shares one observation from the session. Leaders coach clients to describe just the facts (“I noticed the thought that I was angry”) rather than interpretations (“Bill was angry”).

  • “Process-Observing” Wind-Down: A more advanced method where members observe the group process itself. This is risky, as it can lead to critical comments, conflict, and members storming out. It is especially problematic when mixing new and advanced clients.

In both wind-downs, the leaders go last to repair any damage and pull the group together.
