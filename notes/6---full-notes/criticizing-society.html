Criticizing Society

Details The collectivist society has always been marked by a particular, quieter counterpart to the nationalism of more individualistic ones: compliance. In this sense of compliance, I am not necessarily criticizing the broader architecture of political and social structures that allow us to function — that rests on a multitude of fields I am simply unqualified to synthesize or speak on with any real authority. Though, as someone whose academic interests sit firmly in psychology and philosophy, I believe the claim still stands: compliancy is arguably the defining characteristic of how certain collectivist societies (like my own) operate, and more importantly, how they self-perpetuate.

Now, why am I bringing this up? What does compliancy have to do with my critique of society? After all, I am not a sociologist, I have not personally experienced any society meaningfully different from my own, and I would not claim accuracy in perceiving their benefits and flaws from the outside. I largely concede that. But I do think I am entitled to an opinion on how our sociological structures bear down on our psyche and cultural perceptions — and crucially, I am speaking from inside the society I am critiquing, which grants a different kind of authority than an academic one. What I lack in comparative breadth, I make up for in proximity. Addressing the claim of criticizing society: I will be focusing primarily on academic environments, which are a miniaturized version of said society and are shaped by it through practically every available means.

Perhaps, as the means, conditions have been stated, I will no longer stall this essay for any longer and finally get to my arguments. The first argument I want to make about the current state of society is its overemphasis on community. Now, I am not arguing that we should abandon this emphasis; it is inherently what holds our society together. The reason that we are not a governed state or a part of another country is because of how communal relations have been emphasized throughout our history. Nonetheless, it is a new age, and I contend that we would be a fool to continue this tradition so harshly. Though there are arguments for its preservation, it has recently proven impractical, perhaps even self-defeating in our own society. To signify this claim, I want to bring forth stigma. Stigmas are inherently a sociological concept, and it is strongly determined by the bonds of a society as they cast out outsiders depending on their differences to the main group; I believe that because of such a strong bond in our society, we strengthen stigma even further, effectively constraining our ideologies that are strictly outdated, unscientific, illogical, and of course harmful (to ourselves!). Research on mental illness stigma in Pacific Rim collectivist societies, including those with strong Confucian and Vietnamese cultural influences, shows that psychological distress is frequently moralized as personal weakness, poor character, or a threat to family honor and “face,” leading to widespread avoidance of acknowledgment and professional help (Ran et al., 2021; Do et al., 2020). People inherently become blind or bias when having to engage in this problem, having to maintain their compliancy and relationship to the community and society. They are willing to condemn their own blood and people they care about to maintain their own framework of society that they have built for their whole lives, which society has cultivated throughout their lifetime. I can mention more mechanisms about how stigma restrains more than just society or the individual but relatives, friends,… but this is not a psychology course so I digress. Now, to the next point, how does this translate to academic environments?

Adult societies are consisted of people who have fully developed themselves and such, not necessarily their identity but the abilities that allow them to live and socialize in said society. For teenagers and children? Not so much, of course, in this society, we have learnt multiple social skills, or survival skills from a young age to survive scrutiny from our caretakers and adult relatives, it is still not enough for us to be prepared for the academic environment. The reason for this is that… well, they are teenagers, too. Now, that does not necessarily explain what I am getting at, but it provides us with a natural sense of safety or framework that would encourage us to interact and keep touching the fire despite how it may hurt us — through bullying, isolating, verbal/emotional abuse and so on. This is a real issue in academic environments. Because of how undeveloped every single individual in an academic environment is, with unresolved trauma, familial problems, or such, they are likely to be volatile as a “mob” or “group,” weaponizing the trauma and personal problems to other students that are…weaker socially. As an outcast, or just a radically different individual myself, I have experienced countless experiences of people outright violating my boundaries, criticizing my moralities without understanding it fully, failing their own morals, and so on and so forth. The life of an outcast is inherently made difficult in a society alike this, and is even harder in one where the main “goal” is to fit in and be content. Again, this is only my own testimony, and should be taken with a grain of salt, but this is consistent with what research on adolescent social dynamics actually shows in dozens of psychological analyses—and with ethnographic work on Vietnamese lower secondary schools, where bullying is deeply embedded in institutional power relations and disciplinary frameworks (Horton, 2011). Now, one may use the developmental failings mentioned above to rationalize the actions of these individuals, but doing so would be ignorant of the unnecessary, unaccountable, and immoral harm that it produces for other innocent individuals. Additionally, the actions are also poor alternatives to what actually resolves said problems (therapy, counseling, mentalizing to name a few). To not further ruminate on one singular point, we will move on to the next argument.

This second argument addresses the cultural factors. Citing research on stigma in Southeast Asian countries, specifically Confucian values, we can further understand the cultural context that produces the trend and how we act towards actions and people. Confucian thought heavily emphasizes the character and moral values of a person and how it is connected to their actions; society integrates said idea to its core, judging a person’s identity based on a singular negative action rather than logically deducing from the amounts of evidence that would more accurately conjure who they are (Hu et al., 2024; Berkson, 2021). Now, because of how rooted this phenomenon is in previous generations, it is much more prevalent in teachers and adults in most environments rather than in the current generations. Nonetheless, it is still somewhat prevalent, only passively as people judge and assess someone’s identity behind their backs when discussing with someone without inadequate evidence. This phenomenon is inherently psychologically harmful and wrong, as it is considered more as a shaming tool for adults to discipline and educate their students and children rather than a defense mechanism for the perpetrator’s psyche and as an outdated and impractical value. But due to how embedded and respected Confucian is as a philosophy, people continue to ingrain it into their lives without actually justifying the benefits and worthiness of doing so. Because of how human functions, when faced with questions against this inherent incoherence, people often attempt to rationalize their position with rhetoric, or if push comes to shove, become defensive and attacking the values of the other person - the very thing they are being critiqued of doing.

There are additional concepts that strengthen these phenomena even further. I will not mention every single heuristic or psychological mechanism that contributes to these phenomena, as I have also exemplified the Fundamental Attribution Error in the previous paragraph, but I will proceed to list the most devastating ones which actually have a substantial amount of effect on the environment and individuals residing in it. The first mechanism in line is the bystander effect, it practically defines the diffusion of moral responsibility that people possesses when in a group. This effect effectively expands and compounds for every person that is in the group. The reason this is so devastating is due to how it encourages teenagers to act against what they may morally conceive as the right thing in exchange for social aptitude. Furthermore, it allows others to chime in on the matter, bringing in another psychological mechanism - conformity. Morally righteous individuals, or individuals who never actually participate in bullying, can certainly be compelled by the group to engage in it as to not be judged or separated from it. After all, it is in our basic evolutionary-based mind that we adhere to the group and society as to not be casted aside. The argument is a goalpost-moving move itself, as the institutions that govern these teenagers develop rules and moral conduct system to inherently train the teenagers to act morally, yet adults can still rationalize for their immoral actions just cause those actions do not correlate to the rules ? Point in case, our inherent psychological heuristics further strengthens the injustice that our culture and societal allows to happen, and there is no justification that can account for the immorality of the actions of these teenagers.

This is not the endpoint of the mechanisms and moral implications of a collectivist society — it is, at best, a first cross-section. What I have tried to show is that the cultural, sociological, and psychological factors at play in these environments do not operate in isolation; they compound, and the compounding is what makes the injustice so difficult to name and resist. That difficulty is itself worth examining — and worth continuing.

Supporting Content Though I will not philosophically establish a framework here, I will mention about the concept of moral responsibility. In the situations mentioned above, people have adequate epistemic conditions about what was happening, they had control of themselves (on a baseline level due to how every single human operates with psychological mechanisms yet can still make moral decisions), yet they acted immorally nonetheless, so one cannot argue that they are not responsible for their actions. Some points of argument against this situation may be that they are merely teenagers and cannot account for their actions and so it decreases their responsibility. This argument is poor, as these individuals demonstrate morality in other situations just fine, yet when placed under these circumstances, they act differently. Of course, we do not judge someone for not being unfailingly moral, but teenagers act consistently immoral in these situations, which is not justifiable.
