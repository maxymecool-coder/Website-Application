Whose Judgment Counts?

Anticipated family cost, perceived public stigma and help-seeking intentions among Vietnamese university students.

Status Preprint on Zenodo, posted 21 October 2026; not yet peer-reviewedDOI 10.5281/zenodo.23534176Sample N = 308Setting Four universities, northern Vietnam

Abstract

Stigma is among the reasons young people give most often for not seeking help with mental health problems, yet perceived public stigma, measured as what “most people” think, rarely predicts help-seeking in Western samples. This study asked whether, for Vietnamese university students, the stigma that matters is the cost they expect their family to bear if their problem became known. Students at four universities in northern Vietnam completed an anonymous online survey (582 submissions; 308 after registered data-quality exclusions). After reading a depression vignette, they rated how likely they would be to seek help from family, peers, professionals, or no one. Anticipated family cost was associated with lower intentions to seek help from family (ρ = −.18) and higher intentions to seek help from no one (ρ = .22), but it was not a stronger predictor than perceived public stigma on any outcome. Perceived public stigma was associated with help from family at least as strongly (ρ = −.22), while the association for personal stigma was close to zero. Studies in Western population have usually found the opposite, but the result matches an earlier finding on help from family and friends. Family cost and anticipated peer rejection could not be separated empirically, moderation by living with family or depending on it financially was inconclusive, and students who expected a clinician to tell their family were more willing to seek professional help. In this sample, help from family was more closely tied to what students believed most people think than to their own attitudes.

Keywords mental illness stigma · help-seeking · courtesy stigma · family · university students · social psychology · Vietnam

Background

Stigma is one of the most studied barriers to help-seeking for mental health problems. Its clearest effects are on whether people seek help at all, more than on how treatment goes once they do. Most instruments separate personal stigma (one’s own attitudes) from perceived public stigma (what one thinks most people believe).

Neither fully captures a setting where the label is understood to attach to the family. In Vietnamese, the older generation’s word for mental illness, điên, covers anything from psychosis to depression. The study asked whether the expected cost of that label to one’s family, and the expected reaction of friends, relate to where students would turn for help, alongside the two standard forms of stigma.

Method

Participants

Students at four universities in northern Vietnam completed an anonymous survey. Of 582 responses, 274 were excluded as careless or incomplete on quality checks, leaving 308 for analysis.

Procedure and measures

Participants read a short vignette describing a person with depression, then rated how likely they would be to seek help from family, friends, a mental health professional, or no one. Four kinds of stigma were measured:

  • personal attitudes toward mental illness
  • perceived public stigma (“most people”)
  • anticipated cost to one’s family
  • anticipated rejection by friends

Participants were also asked whether they expected a psychiatrist or psychologist to inform their family.

Analysis

Associations were estimated with Spearman rank correlations. p values were adjusted for multiple comparisons and reported as q values.

Results

Table 1 shows the correlations between each stigma measure and intentions to turn to family or to no one.

Perceived public stigma had the largest association with intentions to turn to family. The anticipated cost to family did not exceed it. Personal attitudes were close to zero for both outcomes, unlike the pattern reported in samples from the United States and Ireland. Anticipated cost to family and anticipated rejection by friends behaved almost identically and could not be distinguished from each other.

About half of participants expected a psychiatrist or psychologist to tell their family. These participants reported greater, not lower, willingness to see one.

Limitations

  • Small effects. No correlation exceeded .24 in absolute value; each measure explains only a few percent of the variance in intentions.
  • Correlational and cross-sectional. The data cannot show that stigma causes lower help-seeking, or in which direction any influence runs.
  • Intentions, not behaviour. Ratings were responses to a vignette, not records of actual help-seeking.
  • Sample. University students at four institutions in one region are not representative of Vietnamese young people generally, and nearly half of all responses were excluded, which may have shifted who remained.
  • Measurement. Family-directed and friend-directed items, written as separate scales, did not separate empirically, so they may measure one underlying concern.
