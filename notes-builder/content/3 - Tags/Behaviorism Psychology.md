# Behaviorism Psychology
**Details**
In the first half of the 20th century, **Behaviorism** was the dominant school of thought in American psychology. Championed by figures like John B. Watson and B.F. Skinner, its core doctrine was that psychology should be the science of observable **behavior**, not of unobservable mental states like consciousness, thoughts, or feelings.

The central idea was that all complex behaviors could be built up from a few simple "atoms" of learning: classical and operant conditioning. This philosophy was rooted in **empiricism**—the belief that knowledge comes from experience and that an individual is shaped almost entirely by their environment and reinforcement history. It carried a democratic appeal: anyone could become anything, given the right environmental shaping.

However, Behaviorism ultimately proved to be an incomplete psychology. Its strict refusal to study internal mental life meant it could not address fundamental aspects of the human experience like consciousness, emotion, or subjective thought. Furthermore, it struggled to explain phenomena that pointed to innate biological preparedness, such as the rapid, seemingly instinctual acquisition of language in children. While the principles of association learning remain a critical part of psychology, the idea that they alone could explain all of human nature was shown to be too simplistic.
