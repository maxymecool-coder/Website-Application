# Kinds of Awareness of Moral Responsibility
**Details**
Moving on from the question about the epistemic conditions, now we have to move on to the question about knowledge and beliefs. To start this section, I will propose an example: a person is putting poison in another person’s drink for them to be assassinated. Now, the other person doesn’t actually die, is the perpetrator still morally responsible for what happened even if it did not result in their wanted conclusion. This brings us into our first point on kinds of awareness, or the beliefs of the person who did the act. After all, they are the person who genuinely wanted and believed that it would have killed the other person, so, they should be guilty for the act regardless of if they had the knowledge or not of what was going to happen, correct ? 	Key point here is that, regardless of if he had the knowledge or not, he would have been responsible either way due to his beliefs and that said beliefs actually bad or not.

The second point addresses the timely component of moral responsibility. The main dispute here is between those philosophers who think that the pertinent beliefs must be occurrently entertained (Zimmerman 1997: 421–2; 2017: 79; Ginet 2000: 270; Rosen 2004: 309; Levy 2011: 141) and those who think that it’s enough if these beliefs are dispositionally entertained (Haji 1997: 531; Peels 2011: 580; Timpe 2011: 18; Levy 2013: 167; 2014: 34).[3] According to the ‘occurrentist’ camp, an agent satisfies the EC only if, at the time of action, she consciously believes that her action is right/wrong and consciously contemplates some of its possible consequences (Zimmerman 1997: 421). Otherwise, and for the purposes of attributions of responsibility, she is ignorant of relevant considerations and so, in principle (recall the possibility of culpable ignorance), she has an excuse for her wrongdoing (Zimmerman 2017: 79; see sect. 3.1 below for an argument in favor of this position). As we’ll see below (sect. 2), the occurrentist interpretation of the EC plays a central role in a revisionist argument against commonsense attributions of responsibility. On the other hand, the dispositionalist argues that this is too strict and would let people off the hook or open morals up to be manipulated. After all, a person could somehow shield oneself from blame simply by avoid thinking about the moral status of one’s action, and that’s implausible (Guerrero 2007: 74; Timpe 2011: 23; Robichaud 2014: 150; Levy 2014: 31) (though this does raise some questions about if one can ACTUALLY do that or not or are they just subconsciously hiding it ?).
