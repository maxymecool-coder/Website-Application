# Market power and the markup rule
**Details**
Take MR = P + (dP/dQ) × Q and pull out P:

MR = P × (1 + (dP/dQ) × (Q/P)) = P × (1 + 1/ε)

where ε is the price elasticity of demand (a negative number). Setting MR = MC and rearranging gives

(P − MC)/P = −1/ε

The left side is the **markup**: how far price sits above marginal cost, as a share of the price. (Economists call it the Lerner index.) It depends only on the elasticity of demand:

- With perfectly elastic demand (ε = −∞), MR = P and the markup is zero. The monopolist behaves exactly like a competitive firm.
- The less elastic demand is, the bigger the markup.

In the example, at Q = 6 and P = 18, ε = (dQ/dP) × (P/Q) = −1 × 18/6 = −3. The markup is (18 − 12)/18 = ⅓, which is exactly −1/ε.

So why can't a monopolist charge whatever it likes? Buyers can't switch to another firm, but they can switch to another good. A competitive firm is disciplined by rival firms; a monopolist is disciplined only by the elasticity of demand. The only maker of spearmint gum can't charge much if people are happy with peppermint. The only maker of insulin can charge a lot.

Gruber's example: on Amazon, _Huckleberry Finn_ costs about $4 in paperback and nothing on Kindle, while _Fahrenheit 451_ costs about $9 either way. Both are old high-school staples, and their costs can't differ much. The difference is copyright. Mark Twain's book is out of copyright, so anyone can publish it and competition pushes the price down to cost. _Fahrenheit 451_'s copyright still gives its publisher a monopoly. Even so, it can't charge $100, because schools could assign a different book. Its demand is less elastic than _Huck Finn_'s, not perfectly inelastic.

One more consequence (not in the lecture): a monopolist always prices where demand is elastic, with |ε| > 1. If |ε| were below 1, then 1 + 1/ε would be negative and so would MR. Cutting output would then raise revenue and lower costs at once, so that couldn't be the profit-maximizing point.

**Welfare.** The competitive outcome is where price equals marginal cost: 24 − Q = 2Q, so 8 units at 16. The monopoly sells 6 units at 18. Gruber's Figure 11-5 areas:

||Competition (8 units at 16)|Monopoly (6 units at 18)|
|---|---|---|
|Consumer surplus|A + B + C|A|
|Producer surplus|D + E|B + D|
|Deadweight loss|—|C + E|

B moves from consumers to the monopolist, which is a transfer. C + E simply disappears.

This is the course's first market failure. Until now, deadweight loss came only from government interfering; left alone, a competitive market reached the efficient outcome. Here the market is left alone and still falls short. The monopolist's private incentive (don't cut the price) diverges from society's (sell every unit worth more than it costs). That doesn't yet show government can fix it; that's Lecture 12.
