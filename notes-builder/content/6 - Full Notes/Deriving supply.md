# Deriving supply
**Details**
A price-taking firm produces where p = MC. So the example firm makes 3 units at a price of $30, 4 units at $40, and so on. The firm's supply curve is just its marginal cost curve: for any price, find the quantity where MC equals that price. For C = 10 + 5q², that's q = p/10.

The intuition mirrors demand. The demand curve and the supply curve basically meet where the market settles: what someone will pay for the next unit equals what it costs to make. Gruber develops what that means for welfare in a couple of lectures.

The full definitions:
- **Short-run firm supply:** the MC curve above the shutdown point, which is the minimum of average variable cost. Below that price, the firm makes nothing. In the example, AVC = 5q bottoms out at zero, so there's no shutdown point, and supply is q = p/10 at any positive price.
- **Long-run firm supply:** the MC curve wherever profit isn't negative, meaning wherever the price covers average cost. In the long run, fixed costs can be changed, so a firm that would lose money leaves.

Market supply adds up the firms:

- With one firm, the curve is S1: 1 unit at $10, 3 at $30.
- Add a second identical firm, and the market makes 2 units at $10 and 6 at $30.
- With n identical firms, market supply is n times firm supply: Q = n × p/10.

So each extra firm makes the market curve flatter. Gruber calls market supply a more elastic version of firm supply. He leaves a teaser: if a competitive market can have an unlimited number of firms, what does that imply for the supply curve?

He then rebuilds the whole market from scratch. Most universities leave this for intermediate micro. Gruber credits Paul Samuelson, who once taught this course, with bringing the math in; before him, intro courses taught the graph and stopped. Gruber says his students only ever complain about too little math.

**Step A: one firm's supply curve.** Start with:

1. The production function, q = √(L·K).
2. Input prices, w = 5 and r = 10, still taken as given.
3. Capital fixed in the short run at K̄ = 1.

From these: with K = 1, q = √L, so L = q². Cost is rK̄ + wL = 10 + 5q². Marginal cost is 10q, and p = MC gives the firm's supply curve, q = p/10. Then check the shutdown condition, which never applies here.

**Step B: market supply.** Assume n = 6 identical firms; where n comes from is next lecture. Market supply is Q = 6 × p/10 = 0.6p.

**Step C: market demand.** Assume Q = 48 − p. Gruber made this one up for the example, but you could derive it from preferences, prices and incomes, as in Lectures 2–4.

**Step D: equilibrium.** Set demand equal to supply: 48 − p = 0.6p, so 1.6p = 48 and p* = 30. Then Q* = 48 − 30 = 18.

Now check the firms. Six identical firms sharing 18 units means each makes 3. At p = 30, each firm's own rule, 10q = 30, also gives q = 3. Everything is consistent at once:

- Consumers who want 18 units at $30 get them.
- Firms together supply 0.6 × 30 = 18.
- Each firm is maximizing profit, at p = MC.

Gruber calls this the magic of the market.

For supply, all you needed was the production function and three numbers. Each given gets explained later:

- w and r come from the input-market lectures.
- K̄ comes from Lecture 6's long-run cost minimization.
- n comes in the next lecture.
