# Kant’s Refutation of Idealism
**Details**
Kant introduced in his second edition of the Critique of Pure Reason the "Refutation of Idealism." His goal was to basically rid the subjective traps of Cartesian doubt and immaterialism and instead distinguish his own framework of "Transcendental Idealism." Kant agreed that we only have access to _appearances_ (phenomena) and cannot directly know "things-in-themselves" (noumena). Space and time are merely the a priori, sensible forms of human intuition. However, Kant fiercely rejected the idea that this meant the external, material world is an illusion or unprovable. He set out to turn idealism against itself.

Kant's masterstroke here was to argue that self-consciousness fundamentally relies on the existence of a feasible, permanent external world. Since as a conscious being, one is aware of their own existence as a sequence of thoughts, feelings flowing through time, or as so set in time.

And if it is set in time, it can then logically be inferred from time itself. However, one cannot measure the line of consciousness against itself. Because the inner landscape of the mind is in a constant, fleeting state of flux, this required "permanent" background cannot be internal. Therefore, for inner sense (the march of conscious events) to exist at all, there must be an external, permanent, spatial framework.
