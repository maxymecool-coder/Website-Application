# System-Dependent Habits
**Details**
Most people mistakenly use their habits and activities solely to achieve specific goals, which comes with a multitude of disadvantages. First, a goal-dependent habit is fragile; it might entirely lose its purpose and driving force the moment the goal is achieved, leading to regression (e.g., stopping exercise after running a marathon). Second, relying on goals means relying heavily on motivation or discipline—intrinsic qualities that are unfathomably difficult to intentionally increase and maintain on a daily basis. Rather than fixating on the finish line, people should think in systems. Focusing on the systems that govern the habits creates a sustainable foundation that doesn't rely on fleeting emotional states.

Expanding on the idea of systems over goals, resting on robust systems is vastly more viable for long-term success. A good system does not require you to become a fundamentally better, more disciplined person overnight. Instead, a system improves your output by actively lowering the required threshold for performing tasks. When you have a system in place (like a set time, a pre-packed gym bag, a specific sequence of actions), it removes the cognitive load of decision-making. You don't have to decide how or when to do something; the system dictates it. By lowering the energy barrier to entry, systems make consistent execution the path of least resistance
