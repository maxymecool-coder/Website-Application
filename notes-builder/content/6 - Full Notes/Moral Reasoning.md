# Moral Reasoning
**Details**
Moral reasoning follows similar principles to moral responsibility in that the person exercising it must have sufficient control over their thinking to actually perform it deliberately. It also categorizes itself neatly into reasoning about what to do: how we recognize moral considerations, cope with conflicts among them, and how they move us to act. Moreover, we also tacitly morally reason, though said thoughts never actually lead to a conclusive point; nonetheless, it might be wrong in certain situations to try and explicitly reason, like when you're driving a car, just do it bruh.

A counterpoint to this neat categorization is the pull toward meta-level questions — “are there moral truths, and if so what are they?” and “what makes those truths true in the first place?” (metaethics). So moral reasoning doesn’t sit in isolation; it bleeds into questions about the very nature of morality itself. A further challenge to its practicality is how badly we actually perform it. This may be a result of our dual thinking systems — we default to fast, intuitive processing even for moral problems that genuinely require the slower, more deliberate one. The former point mentioned in this paragraph also leads to another one, one that substantiates that we cannot use moral reasoning to collapse it into either "perceive the facts" or "apply the correct theory" because of our intuition and theoretical limits.

But nonetheless, a practical point that we can draw from moral reasoning is the rationalization that it allows people to perform. People do not actually apply their ability to morally reason just because they become better at it, in fact, it just gives them the ability to rationalize whatever they already believed in and such.
