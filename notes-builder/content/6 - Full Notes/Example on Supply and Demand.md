# Example on Supply and Demand
**Details**
The supply-and-demand model explains market prices by combining the behavior of buyers and sellers. A market is a setting where buyers and sellers interact to make transactions, whether physically in an old-style marketplace or abstractly through modern institutions and platforms. Demand represents the relationship between price and quantity from the consumer’s perspective. In the rose example, demand is written as q = 1,800 − 400p, which means that as the price rises, the quantity demanded falls. This downward slope is explained by opportunity cost: when roses become more expensive, buying roses means giving up more other goods, so consumers want fewer roses. The specific numbers in the equation are not the main point at this stage. The important point is the negative relationship between price and quantity demanded.

Supply represents the relationship between price and quantity from the producer’s perspective. In the same example, supply is written as q = 200p, meaning that as price rises, suppliers are willing to produce more roses. This upward slope is also explained by opportunity cost. Land, labor, and capital used to produce roses could be used for something else. When the price of roses is low, producing roses may not be worth giving up those alternative uses. When the price is high, producing roses becomes more attractive relative to the next best use of the same resources. Supply and demand therefore both follow from scarcity and opportunity cost, but they move in opposite directions because consumers and producers face different margins of decision.

Equilibrium is the point where these two curves meet. In the rose example, solving the two equations gives an equilibrium quantity of 600 and an equilibrium price of 3. At this point, buyers are willing to buy exactly the amount sellers are willing to sell at that price. The system has “come to rest” because there is no immediate pressure, within the model, for price or quantity to change. If the price were too high, consumers would demand fewer roses than suppliers want to sell. If the price were too low, consumers would want more roses than suppliers provide. This does not care about the moral or social implications but rather only the economic implications of it.

This relationship is easily illustrated by diamonds and water: though one is essential to life and the other is used mainly for jewelry, the scarcer one is more expensive, not the more practical or meaningful one.
