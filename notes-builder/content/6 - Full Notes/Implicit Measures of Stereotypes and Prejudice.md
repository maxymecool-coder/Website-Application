# Implicit Measures of Stereotypes and Prejudice
**Details**
A lot of people may say aloud that they may not have anything against certain groups of people, or they may not admit their prejudices, but there is no certain way for us to believe these claims. Researchers have sought out methods of measuring subtle prejudices that people might not want to admit, even to themselves.

One of the most prominent methods is the **Implicit Association Test (IAT)**, which measures prejudices and stereotypes by measuring the speed of our associations. The core principle is that it's easier and faster to respond to two concepts that are strongly associated in our minds than to two that are weakly associated.

**How the IAT Works** The test requires you to rapidly categorize items. For example, you might be instructed to press a left key for an unpleasant word (e.g., "death") and a right key for a pleasant word (e.g., "love"). Then, the categories switch to insects (left key) and flowers (right key).

The critical part comes when the categories are combined. Most people respond faster when the pairing is "unpleasant words or insects" (left key) and "pleasant words or flowers" (right key). The response time slows down when the pairing is switched to "unpleasant words or flowers" and "pleasant words or insects." This difference in reaction time reveals an implicit association: we more easily link flowers with pleasantness and insects with unpleasantness.

**Measuring Implicit Prejudice** Researchers use this same logic to measure implicit racial attitudes. In a typical study with White participants:

1. **Congruent Pairing:** Participants press one key for a photo of a Black person or a pleasant word, and another key for a photo of a White person or an unpleasant word.
    
2. **Incongruent Pairing:** The rule switches. Participants now press one key for a Black person or an unpleasant word, and another for a White person or a pleasant word.
    

**Key Findings**

- **For White Participants:** Most White college students, even those who explicitly state they have no racial prejudice, respond significantly faster to the "Black/unpleasant" and "White/pleasant" pairing. This suggests a subconscious, implicit bias. This effect is generally stronger in older White individuals.
    
- **For Black Participants:** On average, Black participants show little to no implicit preference between Black and White faces on the IAT, responding nearly equally to both pairings.
    
- **Gender Attitudes:** The IAT also reveals implicit attitudes about gender. Most women show a strong implicit preference for women over men. Men, however, tend to show an almost equal preference for both men and women.
    

These implicit biases can have real-world consequences. In one study, when White students evaluated job applicants with ambiguous or marginal qualifications, they rated the application more favorably if they believed the applicant was White than if they believed the applicant was Black. This demonstrates how unrecognized prejudices can influence important decisions, especially in situations that are not clear-cut.
