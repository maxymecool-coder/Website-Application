# The Epidemiology of Smoking
**Details**
Data from the Centers for Disease Control (CDC) and the National Survey on Drug Use and Health (NSDUH) reveal the staggering toll of tobacco on public health. In the United States alone, tobacco is linked to approximately 430,000 deaths annually—roughly 20% of all mortality. Notably, 35,000 of these deaths are attributed to secondhand smoke, a toxicity level that surprised researchers and catalyzed litigation (such as the flight attendants' lawsuit) to ban smoking in public spaces.

While overall prevalence has shifted, the data highlights a profound "education gap." Educational attainment is arguably the strongest predictor of smoking status. Individuals who did not finish high school have a smoking prevalence of 35%, whereas college graduates have a rate of only 14%. This disparity has widened since 1965, suggesting that health education and socioeconomic factors play a critical role in cessation and prevention. Furthermore, the highest prevalence of use occurs in the 18–29 age bracket (peaking at ~41%), a critical developmental window where lifelong habits are often cemented.
