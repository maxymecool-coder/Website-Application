# What to do about monopolies?
**Details**
Monopoly is the first case where government can actually have a potentially positive effect. There are two ways that they can do it:

First is price regulation: Take Lecture 11's monopoly: demand P = 24 − Q, marginal cost 2Q, monopoly output 6 at a price of 18, and a deadweight loss. Now cap the price at the competitive level, 16 (Figure 12-2). The firm's marginal revenue curve changes. It becomes flat at 16 all the way out to the demand curve (8 units), then drops back to the old MR curve. The cap removes the poisoning effect: the monopolist didn't want to sell a 7th unit before because it had to cut the price on all units to 17. Under the cap the price is already 16, so selling one more unit costs nothing on the others. It sells every unit whose marginal cost is below 16 — 8 units, the competitive amount — and the deadweight loss disappears. Profit is 16 × 8 − (12 + 64) = 52; consumers get ½ × 8 × 8 = 32.

A real world example is broadband. Laying fiber to houses is a big fixed cost, so the market is at best a natural oligopoly. Across about 20,000 US zip codes, he says, 70% have zero or one option even for basic 25 Mbps service.

How do you test for market power? Watch what happens to prices as firms enter. In a competitive market price already equals marginal cost, so one more firm barely moves it. In a monopoly market a second firm brings the price down a lot (Lecture 13 shows why). Going from one to two gigabit providers cuts the monthly price by about $60, so that market isn't competitive.

The fixes:

- **Utility-style regulation**, as with electricity and water: price set at marginal cost plus an allowed profit rate (say 10%).
- **The European model:** the public owns the lines — the fixed-cost part — and companies compete to send signals over them, which is the marginal-cost part. Broadband there is far cheaper than in the US.

So why not regulate every monopoly? Because the regulator doesn't get Gruber's handouts. It has to work out the competitive price, which means knowing both curves, and both are hard to measure:

- **Demand:** with no market price to watch, you have to ask people what something is worth, and they don't know. Asked to value saving the Grand Canyon alone, people give five times the figure they give when it's one of five questions. Asked about saving a whale and then a seal, they say $172 and $85; asked in the reverse order, they value them about the same. And people offer about the same amount to save a thousand, a million or a billion birds. No utility function from this course behaves like that.
- **Supply:** ask a firm for its costs and it will report the biggest number it can justify (recall the CEO and the private jet). The regulator is trying to control an industry that knows far more about itself than the regulator does.

Getting it wrong can make things worse (Figure 12-3). Suppose the regulator wrongly decides the competitive price is 10 instead of 16. The firm then produces only where marginal cost reaches 10: 2Q = 10, so 5 units, even though buyers want 14 at that price. The deadweight loss becomes ½ × (8 − 5) × (19 − 10) = 13.5, more than double the unregulated monopoly's 6. Set the price low enough and the firm never enters at all — and no one would ever see the industry that was lost. Gruber mentions a study finding that when the FDA loosened rules on some categories of medical devices, innovation rose enormously with no loss of safety.

That's the theme of his public finance course: almost every market has some market failure, so government can improve things in theory, but whether a given intervention does is an empirical question. A market failure justifies considering a regulation, not adopting it.

The other tool is to add competitors instead of controlling prices. Gruber's example is schools. The US spends almost $12,000 per student, far more than the Czech Republic's under $6,000, yet scores worse on an international math test. One explanation is monopoly. If you live in Lexington, your child goes to Lexington High School. A monopoly that can't set its own price can't take its rents as profit, but it can take them as slack: with no competition, there's little pressure to be efficient.

- **Public school choice** lets students pick among public schools, with money following them. It hasn't shown large effects on achievement.
- **Charter schools** are publicly funded alternatives that don't follow all the same rules (for instance, they needn't hire unionized teachers). Research by Gruber's MIT colleagues finds they generally raise achievement, most of all for minority and low-income students.
