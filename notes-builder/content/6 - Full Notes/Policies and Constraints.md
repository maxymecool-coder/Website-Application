# Policies and Constraints
**Details**
Moving into economic laws, this is where we understand more about how different types of constraints may be affected by policy. For example, one particularly popular question is the policy question is the question of how people will spend their budget on certain things if donated or given to.

So food stamps would change the budget constraint differently. If the benefit can only be spent on food then the budget line becomes flat over the initial food range and then rejoins the ordinary budget constraint. This creates a kinked or piecewise budget constraint. For someone who would have spent more than five hundred dollars on food anyway, SNAP may function almost exactly like cash. Because money is fungible, they can label the first five hundred dollars of food spending as SNAP and free up their original cash for other uses. Their final consumption may be the same as if they had received cash.

But for someone who would have preferred to spend most of the transfer on shelter, SNAP constrains choice. If they would have chosen more shelter and less food under a cash transfer, the food-only benefit forces them to consume more food and less shelter than they personally prefer. They may still be better off than with no transfer, but they are worse off than they would have been with equivalent cash. Economically, cash weakly dominates in-kind benefits from the recipient’s perspective because it allows at least the same choices and usually more. Restricting the form of aid can only reduce or leave unchanged the recipient’s attainable utility, never improve it relative to cash of equal value under standard preferences.

Nonetheless, policymakers may believe that poor people will spend cash badly on alcohol, drugs, other goods that society does not wanna subsidize. As such, this method would be effective to get these people to spend it on what matters. This reveals the tension between paternalism and autonomy in welfare policy. Cash respects local knowledge and individual preference; in-kind aid expresses social control over what counts as acceptable need. The model therefore shows why the same dollar amount can have different welfare effects depending on whether it is given as cash or restricted to a specific use.

Practically, these policies are stripping away people's potential to spend their money in a way that allows them to further their income in an even better way to ensure the mutual benefit of everyone else in similar situations. Nonetheless there is a possibility that people will just try harder to find loopholes which would be counter-productive and heavily backfire the purpose of these policies.
