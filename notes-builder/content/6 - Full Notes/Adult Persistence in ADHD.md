# Adult Persistence in ADHD
**Details**
There is a pervasive myth that ADHD disappears at puberty. While the overt "squirmy" physical hyperactivity often diminishes as the frontal lobes develop, the internal symptoms—inattention, disorganization, and impulsivity—frequently persist. However, diagnosing adults is notoriously difficult because of the "insight gap."

A famous study by Russ Barkley followed 175 children with ADHD in Milwaukee into young adulthood (mid-20s). The study highlighted a massive discrepancy in prevalence depending on who was asked. When the young adults themselves were interviewed ("Do you have symptoms of ADHD?"), only about 4.5% met the criteria. They tended to report, "I'm fine, my boss is just a jerk," or "I'm just a creative type."

However, when researchers interviewed the parents of these young adults, the prevalence rate jumped to 50%. If the researchers adjusted the criteria slightly to account for adult manifestations (e.g., internal restlessness rather than running around), the rate rose to nearly 70%.

This suggests two things. First, ADHD is highly persistent (approx. 2/3rds of cases continue into adulthood). Second, people with ADHD often lack self-awareness regarding their impairments. They tend to "externalize" their problems, blaming teachers, bosses, or partners for their failures rather than recognizing their own cognitive deficits. This lack of insight makes self-reporting a dangerous tool for diagnosis.

Additionally, the gender ratio shifts significantly in adulthood. While childhood ADHD presents as 3:1 (boys to girls), adult ADHD is closer to 1.5:1. This suggests that women may have a more persistent form of the disorder (often the inattentive type), or perhaps that men with the disorder are more likely to drop out of the system entirely or succumb to comorbid risks like the 5x higher rate of automobile fatalities associated with the condition.

**Supporting Content**
When ADHD appears in children, the ratio is often quite skewed between the boys and girls, (5:1 ratio), and the boys show clear signs of aggression and ADHD. The thing is the boys keep this trait even when they grow into adolescence or further, whilst the girls' trait instead is multifinal, meaning there are many ways this trait manifests into behaviors including depression, suicidality, somatic complaints, and relationship dysfunction.
