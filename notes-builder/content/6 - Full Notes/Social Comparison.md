# Social Comparison
**Details**
Perhaps the most common mechanism in people to protect and evaluate their own standings in society (but perhaps also one of the most inaccurate). We do not have an exact measurement for how good we are, so we have to compare it to other people. But the entire process is rigged by _who we choose to compare ourselves to_, and we almost never question that choice because we are our own standard of reasonableness.

Festinger identified two functions of social comparison that pull in opposite directions. Downward comparisons and upward comparisons, which are basically comparing ourselves to people who are worse off and people who are better than us, but both makes us feel bad or dissatisfied one way or another. This is the core relationship of these mechanisms: the comparisons that are the most informative are the most emotionally expensive, and the comparisons that feel best are informationally empty.

Cialdini's research emphasizes how people bask in reflected glory - basically how people usually want to identify with a group when they succeed - wearing school insignia after a team win, but saying "we won" and "they lost." But Tesser's **Self-Evaluation Maintenance Theory** revealed sharp limits. When someone close to you succeeds in a domain central to _your_ identity, their success becomes threatening. Your brother winning a cooking competition feels wonderful if cooking means nothing to you, but agonizing if it's your life's passion. He demonstrated this with a gameshow survey.

But, as a way of life, it is quite anomalous. In the world, there is a practically infinite amount of people that you can compare yourself to, and so its a losing game, or more accurately, an unwinnable one. We might (as a hypothesis) have to abandon this comparison standards if we want to be happy.
