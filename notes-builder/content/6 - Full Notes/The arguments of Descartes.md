# The Argument from Certainty (The Cogito)
**Details**
The foundation of Descartes' philosophy rests on a single, immovable point: the absolute certainty of one's own existence. His method begins with radical doubt—skepticism applied to every aspect of reality. He proposes that we cannot trust our senses, as they often deceive us (e.g., optical illusions). He goes further to suggest that we cannot even trust the reality of the physical world or our own bodies, as it is logically possible that we are dreaming, or that a "malicious deceiver" (a god-like entity with bad intentions) is manipulating our perceptions to simulate a reality that does not exist.

In this scenario of maximum doubt, where the sky, the earth, and my own hands might be fictions, one truth remains unassailable: **I must exist to be deceived.** Even if my thoughts are hallucinations, the act of having them proves the existence of a thinker. The proposition "I am, I exist" (_Cogito, ergo sum_) is necessarily true every time it is conceived in the mind. This creates a fundamental asymmetry: the existence of the physical world is dubitable, but the existence of the self is indubitable.

## The Nature of the "I" (The Res Cogitans)

Having established _that_ he exists, Descartes asks _what_ he is. Since he can doubt the existence of his body (it could be part of the dream), the "I" he has proven to exist cannot be defined by physical attributes. It cannot be an arm, a brain, or a human animal, as those are all dubitable.

He concludes that the only attribute inseparable from him is **thought**. He is a _res cogitans_—a thinking thing. This implies that the self is a non-physical substance, distinct from matter. If the self were physical, it would be subject to doubt; since it is not subject to doubt, it must be non-physical.
