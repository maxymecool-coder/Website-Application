# Details of the Cognitive Approach
**Details**
As we understand in the depressed patients, they have a negative regard on themselves, in the future, as well. To be more specific, he regards himself as a loser, meaning he has lost something of value to his domain. Secondly, he expects the outcome of anything he takes part in to be negative, and so he does not do anything, which ruminates him. Furthermore, he can regard himself to be deficient in achievements, as well as worth.

This is the core idea, and it will lead to other symptoms, so to solve it, we must make the patient see himself as a “winner” rather than a “loser,” as masterful rather than useless.

First of all, we will separate the syndrome of depression into its specific components. We start with any of the symptoms cluster, such as emotional, motivational, cognitive, behavioral, or physiological and concentrate on solving that first. Each cluster may be conceptualized as a problem and a potential target for intervention. As the symptoms are connected, solving one will make the other better as well. It is actually advised for us to broaden our focus to many clusters of symptoms as it is extremely effective.

The specific problems under consideration are complex and consist of more than the particular difficulty verbalized by the patient. A problem generally can be formulated in terms of three “levels”: (a) The observable abnormal behavior or symptom, for example, easy fatigability, crying spells, suicidal threats; (b) the underlying motivational disturbances (if any), such as the wish to avoid activities or to escape from life; (c) underlying the motivation, a cluster of cognitions, such as the belief that striving toward a goal is futile, that there are no satisfactions ahead, and that he is defeated, deprived, and defective.

As said, in each case, we will focus on specific problems with according techniques depending on a variety of factors. Then we will have to reach a consensus with the patient. Of course, several targets and methods may be focused on concomitantly, and trial-and-error and ingenuity is necessary. 

The approach to a presenting symptom should encompass the less obvious components of the problem. For example, a project to mobilize the patient into activity not only involves increasing his motivation but also improving his self-esteem. The patient’s own observation that he is successful in reaching a goal provides crucial information that affects his attitudes about himself. As he thinks better of himself in terms of his ability to control his environment and to be effective in everyday situations, he begins to expect that the outcome of his efforts might be of some avail. Consequently, his optimism is increased. As has already been pointed out, an increase in positive expectancies improves motivation. The increased motivation leads to still better performance. And so we can see a cycle appearing here, a cycle of improvement.

Lastly, even though the patient is doing better and is supposed to feel better, because of his tendency to downgrade or discredit positive happenings, he may screen out many of those events. We must then focus on this selective inattention and help him remove it.
