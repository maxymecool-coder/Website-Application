# Hume's Fork
**Details**
Completely different from Descartes arguments, Hume takes a completely different approach. In his book, "Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding", he divided knowledge into two categories known as relations of ideas, truths that are known by a priori and are necessarily true. These are meanings of the words, not exactly the world. On the other hand, there's matters of facts, which are truths that cannot be contradicted, perhaps objectively true.

But how do we measure cause and effects ? How do we understand that one thing leads to another ? It has to be a habit, we have to have had seen it before one time, and so. But of course, then so, we would meet the problem of induction reasoning, which states that we assume that the future will resemble the past, but we cannot justify it necessarily.
(- **Is it self-evident?** No.
    
- **Is it demonstrative (logic)?** No, because we can conceive of a chaotic universe where gravity stops tomorrow.
    
- **Is it experimental?** No, because any argument from experience ("It has always happened before, so it will happen again") is circular. It uses induction to prove induction.)'

**Supporting Content**
This could then be applied into Descartes arguments about God, as it is very much just a priori and induction.

Everyone has pretty much agreed that Hume's fork is inarguable, but even Hume himself doesn't reject inductive reasoning, in fact, he thinks that it is the ONLY way to move forward as it is the way that we think and we cannot prevent it from happening. We have to accept the probability of it being true, even if its not 100%. Then, when it eventually fails us, then we would change our approach, improve it perhaps, to combat said errors.
