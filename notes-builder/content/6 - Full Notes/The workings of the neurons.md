# The workings of the neurons
**Details**
We have mentioned before that neurons work by receiving and transmitting nerve impulses. They basically use electricity.

The neuron, in most aspects, is just a cell. It has a nucleus and a cell membrane as its components. In the middle of it is a biochemical stew of ions, amino acids, proteins, DNA and RNA, and so on. The difference between a neuron and other cells is the fact that its cell membrane is peculiar, it is irritable. Do anything to it and this thing responds with firing, forming a nerve impulse. Some might even fire on their own, at certain intervals for certain activities that we do unconsciously. Even when the cell is not firing, there is still activity in it as electrical impulses can still be received (tested by using microelectrodes). Because it is a small amount of voltage when the neuron is stable, we call this its resting potential, although maintaining the voltage takes work and the neuron is still very much not at rest.

Now what happens when it is firing ? Well since we already put two microelectrodes to test if it is not, might as well use a third to stimulate that. Normally it would be fine, until an excitation threshold is broken (voltage). Then the voltage difference between the outside and inside happens and the cell abruptly collapses to zero and begins to reverse itself. Now, the inside of the membrane will suddenly swing positive, up to 40 millivolts. This is the neuron's destabilization, a cataclysm so to say. Fortunately, this does not last for long, as the membrane restabilizes itself within 1 millisecond and returns to its -70 millivolt state.

And of course, this sequence is called the action potential, and it goes far beyond just irritating.
