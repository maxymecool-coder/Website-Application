# Stigma as a Threat
**Details**
Stigma is not only a cognitive labeling mechanism, but an existential threat within a local moral world. Drawing on Arthur Kleinman’s anthropology, stigma directly undermines an individual's personhood by jeopardizing the core lived values needed to preserve social standing.

In socioeconomically marginalized communities (e.g., Fuzhounese immigrants in New York City), what matters most centers on labor participation, income generation, and remitting money to discharge debts and support families. Stigma intensifies drastically when mental health symptoms prevent work, prompting accusations of uselessness, abandonment, and social death.

Furthermore, this cultural mechanism also precipitates stigma resistance. Because people must possess these factors to preserve “what matters most,” they will do whatever they can to keep their lives, including adhering to treatment without meaning it.

Holding a psychiatric diagnosis does not automatically produce irreversible social degradation. If an individual maintains the functional capacity to work and earn money, their moral standing and personhood are preserved, buffering against employer and community discrimination. Treatment adherence is viewed instrumentally: medication is embraced primarily when it restores work performance, not merely for psychological symptom relief.
