# Contestable markets
**Details**
Between monopoly and competition lies the contestable market: one firm may dominate, but entry is cheap enough that if it charged a full monopoly price, rivals would come in. The threat of entry keeps its price down.

Airlines are the famous case. When Gruber was a kid, airlines were a regulated monopoly: the government decided who could fly which routes and at what fares, and, fed inflated cost figures by the airlines, it set fares high. Economists argued that the market was contestable, so regulation was unnecessary. They won, and airlines were deregulated. The results:

1. **Fares fell by about a third.** In the 1980s Gruber flew Boston to Newark on People Express for $19.99, with no reservation and payment collected on board.
2. **Routes multiplied**, because many routes the government hadn't allowed turned out to be profitable.
3. **Quality collapsed.** Free food and drinks, real silverware, legroom and free bags all disappeared. Under regulated fares, airlines on the same route could compete only on quality; once they could compete on price, well they dropped the ball on everything to keep prices low (but of course quality nowadays can’t be too low).
4. **Safety didn't fall.** It was excellent before and stayed excellent.
5. **Economists missed one barrier: airport slots.** Runways and gates are almost impossible to add — Boston's Logan had one runway at night until about 12 years ago, and adding a second took 25 years. Airlines built hub-and-spoke networks around airports they dominate, like Northwest at Minneapolis, which threatened to leave and take its jobs if the city gave its slots to rivals. So competition rose less than hoped.

Is the fall in quality bad? Gruber uses revealed preference. High quality at high prices is still available: you can fly business class, or start a premium airline (JetBlue tried, then drifted back toward the pack). Low-cost airlines keep entering and high-end ones don't, so people are choosing low price over high quality. Everyone would prefer high quality at a low price, but that isn't on offer.

The lesson: industry is endlessly creative at protecting profits, so policy has to anticipate how firms will respond, not assume the industry will stay as it is today.
