# Hysteria
**Details**
Hysteria is a pathological extension of the process of somatic imaging. The current most common form of it consists some kind of physical dysfunction that is not based on illnesses (meaning organic disease or physiological abnormality). The bodily ailment may be loss of power in a limb; loss of sensation in some part of the body; sensation of pain in the absence of any stimulation of the pain receptors; and overactivity of muscles, such as in hysterical choking or pseudo epilepsy. In hysteria the patient believes he has a physical disorder. Since the imagined disorder is not fatal, he tends to accept it without severe anxiety. Patients with hysteria are essentially “sensory imagers”; that is, they imagine the particular illness and then take the sensory experience as corroborative evidence of having the illness. The patient typically experiences sensory or motor abnormalities that fit the pattern of his erroneous concept of organic pathology.


When examining these kind of patients, the physician usually is able to identify that the pattern of the hysterical symptom conforms not to the actual anatomy of the body but to the individual's conceptions of the manifestations of a physical disorder. Which means, following an injury to a part of their body, the patient may develop a "stocking-glove" anesthesia- which is a distribution of sensory loss that cannot be produced by anything. Similarly, if a patient sustain brain damage, they will experience paralysis of a limb on the same side of the presumed damage, although the anatomy of the nervous system would determine otherwise. The reason for this is vast, and there are many theories for it, witnessing it in childhood and then projecting it onto one self, Freud's theory of sexual frustrations,... 

To exemplify this, a patient had apparently suffered organic brain damage following a myocardial infarction (Stein et al, 1969). Testing the patient, they found out that he had suffered deficits in immediate recall and inability to concentrate. Yet in the process of psychotherapy, they found out that he had a cognitive distortion regarding the effects of his heart. He had believed that there was an "artery to the brain" arising from the heart at the point of his condition. He thought that the artery had become occluded, resulting in irreversible damage to the brain. When treated, cleared up, the damage disappeared.

The way to treat hysteria is alike any other conditions that have been discussed before like depression or phobias. At the end of the day it is still reframing a "pathogenic idea" of a patient into something correct so that he stops experiencing such symptoms from his psychophysiological illness.
