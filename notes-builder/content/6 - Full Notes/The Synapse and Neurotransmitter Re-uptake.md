# The Synapse and Neurotransmitter Re-uptake
**Details**
The synapse is the microscopic gap between neurons and is the primary site of action for many antidepressant medications. Understanding its function is critical to understanding the biological basis of depression treatment.

As Sapolsky explained, neurons communicate by sending an electrical signal down their length. To pass this message to the next neuron, the signal must cross the synapse. This is done by converting the electrical signal into a chemical one. The first neuron releases chemical messengers, called **neurotransmitters** (e.g., serotonin, dopamine), into the synapse. These chemicals then float across the gap and bind to receptors on the second neuron, delivering the message.

After the message is sent, the neurotransmitters must be cleared from the synapse to stop the signal. This happens in two main ways: degradation (enzymes break them down) or, more importantly, **re-uptake**. The re-uptake process involves specialized pumps on the first neuron that actively suck the neurotransmitters back in to be recycled.

This re-uptake mechanism is the key target for SSRI antidepressants. The theory of depression suggests a deficit of neurotransmitters in the synapse. By blocking the re-uptake pumps, SSRIs prevent the neurotransmitters from being cleared away too quickly. This allows them to remain in the synapse longer, stimulating the receiving neuron multiple times and amplifying their effect. This process demonstrates how medication can directly intervene in the brain's fundamental communication system to treat the biological aspects of depression.
