# Handling math
**Details**
Two reading modes: There are two key approaches, depending on your goal . If you’re reading a math textbook for its own sake, read actively front-to-back, with pencil in hand, working through proofs and problems. But if you’re reading a science text that uses math, it’s often better to skim the heavy details on a first pass. As a rule of thumb: “skipping is often the better part of valor” when the math isn’t the main goal .

Case study – Newton’s Principia: Adler & Van Doren give a strategy story with Newton’s Principia: do not solve every construction. Instead, read the statements of propositions, glance over proofs to get a sense of the methods, and focus on the lemmas, corollaries, and scholia (Newton’s explanatory discussions). This way, you see the overall architecture of Newton’s system (what builds on what) without getting lost in every diagram or algebraic detail . Notice their advice: even avoid diagrams on first read if they confuse you . The payoff is big: understanding the structure lets you grasp the “general scholium” that summarizes Newton’s grand conclusion , which is the treasure of the whole book.

Skipping vs solving – heuristics:  A useful heuristic is “Understand before you calculate.” In practice, that means you can often understand a science text without mastering every equation . Math provides precision and clarity, but your first goal is to get the narrative or concept. Adler & Van Doren show that in Newton’s Optics, diagrams look complex but the math is minimal . Conversely, lacking math can muddle understanding: Galileo’s Two New Sciences is famous but hard, because its dialogue form (no equations) hides its logic.

When math isn’t there: Not all science classics use math. Works by Hippocrates, William Harvey, or Gilbert (on blood and magnets) are readable without math, as long as you remember: your job is “not to become competent in the subject matter but instead to understand the problem” . This means focus on the ideas, not on doing math like a scientist.
