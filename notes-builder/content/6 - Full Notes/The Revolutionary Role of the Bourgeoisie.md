# The Revolutionary Role of the Bourgeoisie
**Details**
In a surprising twist, Karl Marx argues that the capitalist class—the bourgeoisie—**"has played a most revolutionary part"** in history. He doesn't mean this as a compliment. He is thinking dialectically: capitalism, in its ruthless pursuit of profit, acts as a destructive force that unintentionally creates the conditions for its own downfall.

According to Marx, capitalism's revolutionary role is to obliterate the old world. It "has put an end to all feudal, patriarchal, idyllic relations." It tears away the sentimental veils of loyalty, family, religion, and nationality, leaving behind only one connection between people: **"naked self-interest"** and **"callous cash payment."** A boss can no longer afford to be loyal to a worker; competition forces them to be ruthless. Family becomes a "mere money relation."

This destruction is crucial. The old ties—to family, to a specific craft or skill, to a nation, to private property like a home—are what keep people attached to the existing system and divided from one another. By grinding everything down, capitalism performs a vital historical function: it erases these divisions.

It creates a **"cosmopolitan character to production and consumption in every country,"** breaking down national barriers. It drives small artisans out of business, forcing everyone into the same powerless position: the proletariat. By stripping people of their individual skills, their property, their national identities, and their family loyalties, capitalism forges a single, unified mass of humankind. This unified, property-less proletariat is the new force of production that capitalism creates within itself, and it is the class that will ultimately rise up to overthrow it.
