# Rationality and the Logical Impossibility of Absolutism
**Details**
This brings us to the exact mechanism of the social contract, which hinges entirely on Locke's specific understanding of human rationality. In Locke's framework, being rational does not mean modern economic profit-maximizing; rather, it means taking prudent, far-seeing steps to secure, protect, and improve one's baseline condition. The things we rationally seek to protect are our lives, our liberties, and our estates. When free, equal individuals living under Natural Law decide to form a government, they are making a calculated risk-assessment. They are choosing to trade the volatile, unpoliced freedom of the state of nature for the security of a neutral judge, a codified legal system, and a collective enforcing power that can better protect their fundamental rights. The entire purpose of the contract is an upgrade in security and stability.

From this rational starting point, Locke elegantly and devastatingly destroys the argument for absolute monarchy. He argues that surrendering all power to a singular, unchecked sovereign fundamentally violates this rational calculus. If you give one person absolute power, that monarch remains in the unregulated state of nature relative to their subjects, but they are now armed with the entire military and legal force of the state. It is a wildly asymmetric power dynamic where the subject has absolutely no recourse, no neutral judge to appeal to, and no protection against the sovereign's whims. Locke argues that the standard state of nature is actually vastly preferable to absolute tyranny. Because no rational person would ever willingly sign a contract that leaves them far less secure than they were wandering in the wild, an absolute monarchy cannot logically originate from a valid social contract. Therefore, any absolute regime is illegitimate by default, making armed resistance against such a regime not just a political right, but a moral imperative.
