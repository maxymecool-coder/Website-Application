# The Problem of Evil
**Details**
Perhaps the most famous problem contrasting theists against atheists, and one that has been ongoing for such a long time even though it is possibly a never-ending argument (but again that's inductive reasoning). The central tension in the philosophy of religion lies between the theoretical definition of God and the empirical observation of the world. Richard Swinburne argues that we define God as "Omni-Perfect" (infinite in power, knowledge, and goodness) not because of empirical evidence, but because of theoretical simplicity; postulating a single, infinite being is logically simpler (via Occam's Razor) than postulating a complex, finite one. However, this theoretical elegance collapses when confronted with the **Problem of Evil**: the existence of profound suffering (pain) and sin (immorality).

David Hume has cited Epicurus to formulate the trilemma that is: If God is willing to prevent evil but unable, he is impotent; if able but unwilling, he is malevolent. While the "Logical Problem of Evil" (that God and evil _cannot_ coexist) is generally considered defeatable, the "Evidential Problem" suggests that the _quantity_ of gratuitous evil makes an Omni-Perfect God highly improbable.

There has been theodicies attempted against this, one being the "Soul-Making" theodicy (John Hick)—which argues suffering is necessary for character development—fails against the concept of Omnipotence, as an all-powerful being should be able to instantiate virtuous character without the inefficient mechanism of torture or pain. Another is the argument that God's conceptualization of "goodness" is incomprehensible to us, and that perhaps we ARE in a world that is inherently good, but this also fails, as it would strip us of moral responsibility if everything that we do is good at the end of the day (working off the statement that God decides what is good and what is evil).
