# The importance of a control group
**Details**
The reason these groups are so important is that they isolate factors, variables that could have influenced the other group. So for example, a summer camp where campers played video games and only video games for a week, at the end of the summer camp, they have better attention span, is this really because of the video game ? Now we add a control group, a group that receives relatively the same treatments except they do not play video games, they do normal activities. Now we can identify if the independent variable that was the video game truly caused the result that attention span gets better.

In a **treatment outcome research**, which are experiments to determine the effectiveness of a treatment, more procedures are applied to validate the tests even further. Psychologists and psychiatrists evaluating the effectiveness will be subjected to a **double-blind procedure**, meaning they have no idea the person they are evaluating will be from which group, avoiding biases. The control group will also receive something called a **placebo**, which is basically nothing, just to make them think that they are receiving something. This helps validate the test even further as the control group will function as if they received treatment as the other group.

**Supporting Content**
However, as highlighted by Irving Kirsch's meta-analyses on antidepressants, maintaining a true double-blind is frequently compromised by the physical reality of the medications. Kirsch's analysis revealed that the placebo effect alone accounts for roughly 75% of the reported benefits in antidepressant trials. More troublingly, drugs prescribed for entirely different conditions (like sleep aids or anti-anxiety meds) produced similar improvements in depressive symptoms during trials. The likely culprit is the presence of side effects. Real medications have noticeable side effects, whereas standard sugar-pill placebos do not. When a patient begins experiencing dry mouth or lethargy, both the patient and the purportedly "blind" doctor can easily deduce that they are in the experimental group. This realization triggers an enhanced psychological placebo response and alters how the doctor interacts with the patient, effectively breaking the integrity of the experiment.
