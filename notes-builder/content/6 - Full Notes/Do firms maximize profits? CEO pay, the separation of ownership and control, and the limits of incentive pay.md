# Do firms maximize profits? CEO pay, the separation of ownership and control, and the limits of incentive pay
**Details**
The model assumes firms maximize profits, and the logic seems airtight. But CEO pay often looks unrelated to performance:

- **Amgen, 2010:** lost 3% of its market value that year and 7% over five years, yet paid its CEO $21 million.
- **Abercrombie & Fitch, 2008:** stock fell 71%, yet the CEO was paid $72 million.
- **Hewlett-Packard:** Leo Apotheker was fired after a disastrous 11 months as CEO and still made $13 million.

If firms maximize profits, how can they pay so much to people who don't seem to be doing a good job?

Of course we can't know that; we have to view it in terms of opportunity costs. In other words: what would the company have lost if they didn't have that CEO? We couldn't know; only the people inside the company would.

But a clever test suggests this isn't the whole story. When oil prices rise, oil company profits rise, yet no CEO controls the world oil price. So CEOs shouldn't be rewarded for those gains. In fact, oil company CEOs get big raises when oil prices go up. That can't be a reward for good work.

What we are looking for is "agency." In the modern US economy, companies aren't actually owned and governed by the same people. In fact:

- The owners are a scattered crowd of stockholders.
- The decisions are made by managers they employ.
- Managers don't automatically have an incentive to do what maximizes the owners' profit.

Bengt Holmström, Gruber's former MIT colleague, shared the 2016 Nobel with Oliver Hart for work on this tension, which economists call contract theory.

Gruber's illustration is a manager at a large company that doesn't yet have a private jet. The manager's friends at bigger companies all have jets and mock them for it. The honest math says flying first class is cheaper. So the manager reruns the numbers assuming only peak-time flights on the priciest airlines, makes a slide deck showing that a jet "saves money," and the owners don't bother checking. The manager gets the jet; profits are lower. What's best for the manager isn't what maximizes profit.

Firms try to fix this with a **board of directors**: about 20 people elected to represent shareholders and watch the managers. But boards often fail:

- Their members are largely picked by the managers they're meant to watch.
- Many are retired CEOs who like private jets too.
- They do the job part-time, while the manager's pay is the manager's full-time focus.

Gruber's example is Richard Grasso, who rose from the shipping department to run the New York Stock Exchange. On retiring he received a $187 million package. When the press asked, the board said it hadn't understood what it had approved.

Another fix is **stock options**: the right to buy the company's stock at a set "strike" price. Suppose you join when the stock is at $100 and get the right to buy 1,000 shares at $100. If the stock rises to $200, you buy at $100, sell at $200, and make $100,000. If it falls to $80, you simply don't use the option, so it's worth nothing.

In symbols, the payoff is max(stock price − strike, 0) × shares. The idea is that if the manager's wealth is tied to the company's, he will maximize the company's profits, since that benefits him. There is a risk issue, though, because his payoff would never be zero while the potential for insane profits is high.

The deeper problem is that managers can raise the short-run stock price without raising long-run profits. Enron's managers had large option holdings. Gruber describes Enron selling to subsidiaries it also owned, so that it looked like it was booking big sales. The stock soared, some managers cashed out, and the company went bankrupt once it all came out.

**Supporting Content**
**Corporate finance** is the field that studies how to write contracts that line up managers' incentives with owners'. Gruber adds that workers' interests belong in that design too. So far this has been a story of one rich group against another.
