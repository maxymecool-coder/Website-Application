# Chronic Stress as a Pathway to Depression
**Details**
Chronic psychological stress is a primary trigger for major depression, acting as the bridge between life experience and biological illness. Psychological stress that is defined by a lack of control, predictability, social support, and outlets for frustration activates the same biological stress response (e.g., glucocorticoid release) as a physical threat.

When faced with uncontrollable stress, an individual first enters an anxious state of frantic, maladaptive coping. Over time, if these efforts consistently fail, the system transitions into helplessness, the kind where one is no longer expecting success so they shut down. This state of learned helplessness is the psychological model for depression.

Repeated exposure to major stressors can lead to a phenomenon called **kindling**. After the first few depressive episodes are triggered by external events, the brain's circuitry changes. The depressive state becomes "kindled," like a fire that has caught, and begins to run on its own internal, endogenous rhythm, no longer requiring a major external stressor to be activated.

This is particularly true for **early life stress**. Childhood adversity, especially poverty, teaches a lifelong lesson in helplessness. This early training rewires the developing brain, making it more vulnerable to depression for the rest of the person's life by altering the very structure of its stress and reward circuits.
