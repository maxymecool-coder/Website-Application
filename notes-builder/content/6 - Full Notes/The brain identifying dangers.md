# The brain identifying dangers
**Details**
Sensory information about the outside world arrives through all of our senses, eventually converging in the thalamus, which is an area inside the limbic system that outputs an integrated, coherent experience of what is happening to the body. The sensations are passed down to the amygdala (in the limbic, unconscious brain) and the frontal lobes, where they reach our conscious awareness.

The thing is, the time it takes for the info to reach the amygdala is several milliseconds faster than of the frontal lobes. This is bad, because the thalamus can also output dissociated fragments, which can create a feeling of danger, as time freezes, it will feel like it lasts forever. The amygdala identify input if it is relevant for our survival so fast and automatically that with the help of the hippocampus, a nearby structure that relates the new input to past experiences. If the amygdala senses a threat, it sends a message to the brain stem and hypothalamus, orchestrating a response from the body. And of course, because it processes the info faster than the frontal lobes, by the time we are conscious of what is happening, our body may already be on the move.

While the amygdala is usually very good at this, trauma can increase the risk of misinterpreting situations.
