# LMC
**Details**
The Library Model of Cognition (LMC) is a cognitive architectural thesis proposed by Richard Samuels (1998) as an alternative to the Massive Modularity Hypothesis (MMH). LMC posits that the human mind contains few domain-specific computational mechanisms (e.g., Darwinian modules), especially for central processes like reasoning and belief fixation. 

He instead proposes that Domain-specific structure arises from innate, domain-specific bodies of knowledge—truth-evaluable systems of mental representations (e.g., core principles for physical objects, number, or intuitive psychology). These bodies of knowledge are "inert" (representations manipulated by processes) and are operated on by domain-general computational mechanisms, which are not dedicated to specific domains but can flexibly apply knowledge across tasks. LMC denies that central cognition requires massive domain-specific modules (hardware or algorithms); innate knowledge suffices for specialization without modular encapsulation or dedication.  

Key points:
- Compatible with evolutionary psychology's commitments (e.g., computationalism, adaptations, universal human nature, evolutionary analysis) but incompatible with MMH's claim of "largely or entirely" modular central systems.  
- Addresses adaptive problems (e.g., social exchange, incest avoidance) via general mechanisms + specialized knowledge, avoiding MMH's overcommitment to unproven modular hardware.  
- Critiques of MMH: Optimality and solvability arguments fail to prefer MMH over LMC, as domain-general mechanisms with innate knowledge can handle domain-dependent error, unlearnable relations, and combinatorial explosion without modules. Experimental evidence (e.g., Theory of Mind dissociations in autism/Williams syndrome) doesn't distinguish LMC from MMH.  

LMC is argued as more effective/parsimonious than MMH because it provides innate domain-specificity without requiring implausible massive modularization of central processes, making MMH "unwarranted and unmotivated" (Samuels, 1998, p. 576).
