# Low self-esteem
**Details**
When the depressed patient reflects on adverse events (such as a separation, rejection, defeat), he usually ponders over what does it all say about himself. And more than often does he come to the conclusion that it is a heinous defect in himself. The deserted husband concludes, “I have lost her because I am unlovable.” This conclusion, of course, is only one of a number of possible explanations, such as basic incompatibility of their personalities, the wife’s own problems, or her desire for an adventure related more to thrill-seeking than to a change in her feelings for her husband.

This leads to a chasm being created in his personal domain, he suffers not only the loss but he finds out about a deficiency in himself. And of course, he would exaggerate this all. This becomes so imperative that it infiltrates every thought he has about himself, then it takes over his self-image, and then he could only describe and talk about himself of his “bad” traits. He would merely flow over his achievements and abilities, which means he will discount or gloss over the attributes he once highly valued.

Moreover, the tendency of comparing one self to others may lower the self-esteem even further. 

The harshness and inappropriateness of self-reproaches in depression have either been ignored by writers or have stimulated very abstract speculations. Freud postulated that the bereaved patient has a pool of unconscious hostility toward the deceased loved object. Since he cannot allow himself to experience this hostility, the patient directs the anger toward himself and accuses himself of faults that actually are characteristics of the loved object. The concept of inverted rage has remained firmly entrenched in many theories of depression. The convoluted pathway proposed by Freud is so removed from information obtained from patients that it is difficult to test it.
