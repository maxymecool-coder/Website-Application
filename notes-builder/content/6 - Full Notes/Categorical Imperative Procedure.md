# The Categorical Imperative Procedure and the Perturbed Social World
**Details**
The Categorical Imperative Procedure is Immanuel Kant's practical framework for determining whether our intended actions align with universal moral duty. This procedure is not a simple algorithmic trap for catching liars; it is a profound thought experiment requiring sincere moral sensibility. The process begins with an agent's subjective, sincere maxim: a rational policy outlining what they intend to do in specific circumstances to achieve and specific personal end. They must then generalize this personal maxim into a universal precept. This is then eventually converted into a hypothetical law of nature which they must adjoin to the existing laws of physics and human psychology to construct a "perturbed social world."

From then, to determine if this action is morally permissible, the agent must subject it to a two-part test: they must be able to rationally intend to act within this world, and they must be able to consistently will that this world exists. Though it is an impenetrable test, it is also one that is too strict. This would then cause the moral law to inevitably cross some of the random personal desires; and so it would need to be evaluated from [[The Original Position]] rather.
