# Cash vs In-kind transfers
**Details**
SNAP, or the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program, is something that gives low-income Americans a debit card that only works for food. People below the poverty line, about $14,000 a year for a single person, or a little above it, get a couple of hundred dollars a month. Gruber asks why the government doesn't just give them cash, and answers with budget constraints rather than intuition.

To exemplify how it affects people with different needs we can take a very poor person with $5,000 who can spend it only on food (x-axis) or shelter (y-axis). Set both prices to $1. Gruber calls this using a numéraire: fixing prices at 1 so dollars and quantities are the same number. Since "food" and "shelter" are vague baskets anyway, choosing units this way costs nothing. The budget line runs from $5,000 on each axis, with a slope of −1. A $500 cash gift shifts it out in parallel to $5,500, since prices haven't changed. Two people use the gift differently:

|   |   |   |
|---|---|---|
||**Before: food, shelter**|**With $500 cash**|
|X (mostly wants shelter)|$200, $4,800|$300, $5,200|
|Y (mostly wants food)|$4,900, $100|$5,100, $400|

Both are better off. Gruber's spoken description of Y doesn't match the figure. Going by the figure, Y puts more of the new $500 into shelter than food, $300 against $200. Nothing hangs on it, because what matters is that Y spends far more than $500 on food either way.

If we instead make it food only, the graph changes. It would cut off from the top on the Oy axis (shelter) because the budget doesn’t cover that. And well in MRS wise, it is clear that the person preferring shelter is worse off.

For Y, nothing changes. Y was going to spend $5,100 on food anyway, so Y calls $500 of it SNAP and buys exactly what they would have with cash. Money is _fungible_: a dollar is a dollar, whatever it's labeled. Economists call people who would spend more than the benefit on food anyway _inframarginal_. They should treat a food-only benefit exactly like cash.

X can't do that. With cash, X wanted $300 of food and $5,200 of shelter, but that bundle is in the cut-off corner. The best X can do now is the kink, $500 of food and $5,000 of shelter, on a lower indifference curve. X is worse off than with the same $500 in cash, though still better off than with nothing.

This generalizes. Judged by the person's own preferences, a restricted transfer can never beat the same amount in cash. It leaves everyone it actually constrains worse off than cash would.

So why do people help this way? Why does the government do so?   

The positive question is whether SNAP raises food spending compared with cash. The graph can't say, because the answer depends on how many recipients are like X. Experiments say yes, but by much less than a dollar. Gruber puts it at about 15 cents more food per dollar given as SNAP rather than cash. So some recipients are X's, but most behave like Y's. He presents this as part of the rise of field experiments in economics, which he credits to MIT's Esther Duflo and Abhijit Banerjee.

The normative question is whether benefits should be restricted at all. Here Gruber points to J-PAL, the Jameel Poverty Action Lab in MIT's economics department. He credits it to the three 2019 Nobel winners, Duflo, Banerjee and Michael Kremer (now at Chicago), and calls it the world's leading center for answering economic questions with randomized experiments. Its dozens of experiments in developing countries compare cash with goods. They find little cash goes to "bad" goods; nearly all goes to children's education, food or shelter.
