# Hume's Stance
**Details**
Of course, aforementioned in [[The Contextual Divergence of Hobbes and Locke]], we have seen how Locke wrote his framework to basically oppose what was happening at the time in his own life. Hume, however, was writing during a far more stable period in the 18th century, completely detached from the urgency of civil war. Because of this stability, Hume did not need to invent a defensive legal architecture; instead, he approached political philosophy as a detached, scientific observer. Moreover, he also adopted a more epistemological approach, trying to introduce the "experimental method" into "moral subjects." In the 18th century, "moral" did not just mean ethics; it encompassed the entirety of psychology and social theory, while "experimental" referred to the Newtonian method of observation and empirical data. He was trying to look for something practical.

Hume disagrees with Locke on the grounds of consent, and basically does so by applying practicality to humanity's history, positing how almost all existing governments were founded on usurpation or violent conquest, not peaceful agreement. Furthermore, neither the rulers nor the subjects actually believe a contract exists. Princes view subjects as property or inheritance, and ordinary citizens are entirely unaware that they are supposedly participating in a grand philosophical agreement. These citizens cannot "consent" when they do not have the view of other ways of living and the knowledge necessary to participate in such a decision.
