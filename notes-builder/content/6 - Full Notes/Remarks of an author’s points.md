# Remarks of an author’s points
**Details**
The first is saying that he is uninformed, which means to say he lacks a piece of knowledge that is relevant to the problem he is trying to solve. And so one must point this information out to state what he is lacking and how it is relevant to his conclusions.

A few illustrations here must suffice. Darwin lacked the knowledge of genetics that the work of Mendel and later experimentalists now provides. His ignorance of the mechanism of inheritance is one of the major defects in The Origin of Species. Gibbon lacked certain facts that later historical research has shown to have a bearing on the fall of Rome. Usually, in science and history, the lack of information is discovered by later researches. Improved techniques of observation and prolonged investigation make this the way things happen for the most part. But in philosophy, it may happen otherwise. There is just as likely to be loss as gain with the passage of time. The ancients, for example, clearly distinguished between what men can sense and imagine and what they can understand. Yet, in the eighteenth century, David Hume revealed his ignorance of this distinction between images and ideas, even though it had been so well established by the work of earlier philosophers.

Next is to say he is misinformed, which means what he is asserting is not the case. The error this presents is that the author himself made assertions contrary to the fact. He is proposing that something is true or probable although it is in fact false and less probable. This should be pointed out only if it is relevant to his conclusions, and you must be able to argue about the truth with that of the author.

These two points are extremely relevant to each other, and you are right, lack of information means misinformation most of the time. The difference comes when we consider the conclusions made from these two, lack of information makes the author leads to conclusions that might not be what he was trying to get at, but misinformation fully leads us to wrong conclusions.

The third point is illogicality in the reasoning of points to ideas. This is when the conclusion does not simply follow the line of thoughts presented, it is the occurrence of inconsistency, meaning the two things the author tried to connect were incompatible. And so the reader must be able to show the precise respect in where the author’s argument lacks cogency.

This is also related to the previous two points, they all lead from one to another. Poor grounds, evidence leads to poor conclusions even with good reasonings. Good grounds, poor reasonings also does the same, different destination but around the same location.
