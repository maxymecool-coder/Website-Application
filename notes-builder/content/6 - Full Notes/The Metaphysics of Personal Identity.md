# The Metaphysics of Personal Identity
**Details**
Perhaps diverging a bit from the tracks of Kant's project, we may now take a look at defining the continuity of the "self" over time. If a ship's wooden planks are entirely replaced one by one over decades, is it still the same ship? Applying this to human biology—where our cellular makeup and sensory receptors are in a constant state of death and renewal—how can we claim to have a continuous, enduring identity? Classical and scholastic philosophers typically solved this through "essentialism," positing a static, underlying substance or "soul" that undergoes accidental changes but remains fundamentally the same entity.

John Locke radically challenged this essentialist view by separating the concept of "man" (the biological entity) from "person" (the conscious self). Through his famous "Prince and the Cobbler" thought experiment, Locke argued that if the consciousness and memories of a prince were transferred into the body of a cobbler, the resulting entity would biologically be the cobbler, but personally be the prince. For Locke, personal identity is completely exhausted by the contents of consciousness and memory.

David Hume took empiricism even further, directing his skeptical gaze inward. Hume famously declared that when he introspected, he could not find a "self" at all; he found nothing but a "bundle of perceptions" passing in rapid succession, much like soldiers replacing one another in a marching formation. Because there is no single, permanent perception of a "self," Hume argued the self is an illusion. However, this radical skepticism met fierce resistance from Scottish Common Sense philosopher Thomas Reid. Reid wryly pointed out the logical absurdity of Hume's position: "for there to be treason, there must be a traitor." In other words, a bundle of perceptions logically requires a unified perceiver to hold the bundle together, setting the stage for Kant's revolutionary synthesis.
