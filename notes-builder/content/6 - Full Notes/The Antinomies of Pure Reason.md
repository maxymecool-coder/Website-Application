# The Antinomies of Pure Reason
**Details**
While Paralogisms deal with the internal illusions of the self, the _Antinomies of Pure Reason_ emerge when the mind attempts to comprehend the external "World" (defined as the absolute totality of all existing things). Kant outlines four antinomies where reason traps itself in irresolvable contradictions, presenting a thesis and an antithesis that both seem logically sound. These are divided into Mathematical antinomies (debating the limits of space/time and infinite divisibility) and Dynamical antinomies (debating determinism versus free will, and the existence of a necessary being or God).

To handle these deadlocks, Kant employs the _Skeptical Method_—provoking a conflict not to declare a winner, but to expose that both sides are vainly grasping at deceptive appearances beyond human knowledge. Crucially, this strict limitation of pure reason is exactly what opens the door to human morality. Reason cannot empirically prove human freedom or the existence of God. However, from a moral standpoint, one does not need to mathematically _prove_ freedom; one simply must act on the supposition of their own agency. When an individual is faced with deeply consequential choices, they require an ultimate, flawless guiding principle to act justly. This ideal principle—the ultimate anchor of perfect moral reasoning—is what Kant conceptualizes as God. Therefore, God and human freedom are not secured by empirical science or theoretical logic, but are demanded by the innate architecture of human morality and practical reason.
