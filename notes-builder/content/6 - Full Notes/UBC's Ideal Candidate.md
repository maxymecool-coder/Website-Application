# UBC's Ideal Candidate
**Details**
## Introduction

The University of British Columbia (UBC) is a top-ranked public research university with a global reputation and a strong international community. UBC’s official messaging and admissions materials consistently emphasize not just academic excellence, but also the holistic qualities and values that define an ideal student. Especially for applicants to programs like Psychology (UBC’s most popular major) and Education, as well as those vying for major scholarships, UBC looks for well-rounded individuals who will thrive academically and enrich the campus community. By reading between the lines of UBC’s mission statements, admissions pages, scholarship criteria, and faculty websites, we can identify recurring themes in tone and content that paint a clear picture of the kind of student UBC seeks. This report breaks down the core academic and personal traits of UBC’s ideal international undergraduate candidate – from scholarly abilities to leadership, community engagement, global outlook, and mindset – and highlights the values UBC explicitly and implicitly prioritizes. Key insights from UBC’s scholarship programs and the expectations of the Psychology and Education faculties are included, followed by a summary table of the key traits and their significance.

## Academic Excellence and Intellectual Curiosity

UBC’s first expectation is strong academic performance. As a highly competitive institution, UBC admits students who have excelled in their secondary education – typically those among the “brightest minds in the world”. The admissions process is rigorous and comparative, meaning international applicants must meet high standards and often exceed basic requirements to stand out. Superior grades in relevant courses are essential; UBC evaluates all Grade 11 and 12 academic courses, giving particular weight to subjects related to the chosen field of study. An ideal candidate for Psychology, for instance, would have a strong foundation in science or social science courses, while an aspiring Education student would excel across a broad academic spectrum.

However, UBC looks beyond just grades. The university explicitly encourages intellectual exploration and curiosity. Notably, UBC will even exclude an applicant’s lowest high-school grade (if it’s outside core requirements) “to encourage you to explore subjects outside your comfort zone”. This policy reveals that UBC admires students who challenge themselves academically rather than playing it safe. Top applicants often pursue enriched or advanced curricula (such as IB or AP programs) and seek out opportunities to deepen their knowledge. UBC’s research-intensive culture also means that intellectual curiosity and a penchant for inquiry are prized. The Department of Psychology, for example, is described as a global leader in research and scholarship, with faculty and students advancing knowledge across many sub-fields. The ideal Psychology applicant would demonstrate genuine interest in scientific inquiry and perhaps have engaged in research projects or independent study. Likewise, an Education-related candidate would show intellectual engagement with topics like pedagogy, human development, or social issues in education. Overall, academic excellence (high achievement and preparedness) coupled with intellectual enthusiasm (curiosity, initiative in learning) forms the bedrock of the ideal UBC student profile.

UBC’s tone when discussing academics – using words like “excellence” and “outstanding” – underscores that it values students who strive to be the best. In UBC’s statement of values, Excellence is defined as “the quality of striving to be, and being, outstanding”. Prospective students are expected to embody this drive. In practice, this means competitive grades, a solid grasp of English (meeting a high English Language Admission Standard), and a track record of academic rigor. At the same time, being an “outstanding” scholar at UBC also means being open-minded and innovative. UBC’s strategic plan highlights innovation as a core theme, indicating a preference for students who think creatively and embrace new ideas. Thus, the ideal candidate not only earns top marks, but also displays a love of learning, critical thinking skills, and readiness to contribute to UBC’s dynamic academic environment.

## Leadership and Community Engagement

UBC makes it clear that pure academics are not enough – the ideal candidate is well-rounded and actively involved beyond the classroom. Since 2012, UBC has used a broad-based admissions process that requires a Personal Profile essay from applicants, specifically to identify “well-rounded candidates who could perform well academically and get involved on campus.” This reflects UBC’s belief that students who engage in extracurricular activities, leadership roles, and community service enrich the university community and are more likely to be successful, engaged graduates.

Leadership is one of the most emphasized qualities. UBC looks for students who have demonstrated initiative, influence, or responsibility in their schools or communities. The top scholarships for international students explicitly require leadership potential or achievement. For example, the Karen McKellin International Leader of Tomorrow Award recognizes “exceptional…students who demonstrate superior academic achievement and exemplary leadership skills that support and empower others through collaboration, empathy and active listening,” with a positive impact through school or community projects. This description is revealing: UBC’s ideal leaders are not just title-holders, but those who empower others, work in teams, and show empathy. Leadership, as valued by UBC, can take many forms – from holding office in student government, leading a volunteer initiative, mentoring younger students, to spearheading a project or club. The tone in UBC’s materials (highlighting collaboration and active listening alongside leadership) suggests the university prizes inclusive, service-oriented leadership over mere positional authority.

Hand-in-hand with leadership is a strong emphasis on community engagement and volunteerism. UBC wants students who care about more than their own success – those who devote time and energy to bettering their community or helping others. Whether it’s volunteering for a charity, organizing events, or contributing to one’s family or cultural community, such experiences are highly valued. The Personal Profile section of UBC’s application asks applicants to list up to five significant activities or achievements, explicitly including categories like community service, volunteer work, clubs, creative pursuits, athletics, and family responsibilities. Applicants are then asked to elaborate on one or two of these activities, explaining their role and what they learned in the process. This requirement shows that UBC is looking for depth of involvement and personal growth: the ideal candidate has meaningful extracurricular experiences and can reflect on how those experiences shaped them. It’s not about superficial resume-padding, but about making a real impact and learning from it.

Notably, UBC’s largest scholarships favor students with a record of community impact. The Donald A. Wehrung International Student Award, for instance, is aimed at students from challenging backgrounds who “have achieved academic excellence under challenging circumstances” and “demonstrate leadership potential and a strong commitment to making a positive impact in their communities.” Even for students not seeking scholarships, this communicates what UBC admires: resilience (more on that later) and a service mindset. The UBC International Impact Award goes further, rewarding those who have shown leadership in finding solutions for global or societal issues like sustainability, social justice, equity, and health. This emphasis on impact indicates that UBC’s ideal student is a young person who already strives to improve the world around them. They should have a habit of involvement – whether it’s local community volunteering or broader initiatives – and plans to continue that engagement at UBC.

In summary, UBC seeks leaders, volunteers, and active citizens. The admissions tone is encouraging students to “get involved” and to be contributors to campus life from day one. Ideal candidates might be those who founded a high school club, captained a team, volunteered at a hospital, organized a fundraiser, or took care of important family duties – any activities showing responsibility, initiative, and community spirit. What matters is that these experiences have taught the student something and that they demonstrate qualities like teamwork, communication, empathy, and drive. UBC wants students who will continue to lead and serve during their university years, enhancing the vibrancy of student life through clubs, student government, research groups, and community outreach. The structural emphasis on Work programs, leadership and volunteering, clubs and societies on the UBC prospective student site underscores that such engagement is a core part of the UBC experience. The ideal UBC student is eager to take advantage of these opportunities – not just to benefit personally, but to give back and leave a mark on the community.

## Diversity, Inclusion, and Global Citizenship

One of UBC’s defining themes is its commitment to diversity and global citizenship. As an international applicant, this is especially relevant – UBC prides itself on being “North America’s most international campus community,” with a highly diverse student body drawn from 150+ countries. The ideal UBC candidate not only comes from diverse backgrounds, but also embraces diversity and demonstrates a global outlook.

UBC communicates a very explicit commitment to equity, diversity, and inclusion (EDI). The university leadership frames diversity as essential to excellence: “Equity, diversity, and inclusion are the conditions for attracting and retaining the best and brightest students, staff, and faculty from around the world.” In other words, UBC wants a campus rich in different cultures, perspectives, and experiences, believing this fuels innovation and learning. An ideal student, therefore, values and respects people of all backgrounds. In their personal profile and interviews (if applicable), a strong candidate might show evidence of cultural awareness, such as involvement in multicultural clubs, learning multiple languages, or advocating for inclusive practices. UBC’s core values include Respect, defined as “regard felt or shown towards different people, ideas and actions” – indicating that open-mindedness and respectfulness are expected traits. The tone on official pages is welcoming: UBC positions itself as a place where everyone should feel a sense of belonging. An applicant who can thrive in and contribute to such an inclusive environment – for example, someone who has bridged cultural differences or stood up for equity – fits the UBC ideal well.

Global citizenship is another recurring theme. UBC’s vision is “Inspiring people, ideas and actions for a better world.” and its purpose includes fostering global citizenship to advance a sustainable and just society. The ideal UBC student is seen as a global citizen – aware of global issues and eager to have a positive impact internationally. This outlook is especially important for international students and for fields like Education and Psychology that have worldwide applicability. In practice, global citizenship might mean a student has engaged with issues like climate change, human rights, public health, or education equity, either at home or abroad. It also suggests a willingness to collaborate with people from around the globe and an appreciation of UBC’s international context. UBC highlights and rewards such traits: the International Scholars Impact Award specifically honors students dedicated to causes in sustainability, climate action, social justice, equity, inclusion, health and wellness. Furthermore, the university’s strategic plan includes strategies for Local and Global Engagement, indicating that students are encouraged to connect their learning to communities both in Canada and internationally.

For an Education program candidate, global citizenship might manifest as an interest in diverse educational systems or a passion for teaching in underprivileged regions. For a Psychology student, it could be an interest in cross-cultural psychology or global mental health. UBC also integrates global perspectives into student life through exchange programs (e.g., Go Global), and an ideal student might express eagerness to participate in such opportunities, underscoring their adventurousness and adaptability in new environments.

In sum, UBC seeks students who will both benefit from and contribute to its multicultural, globally minded campus. The ideal international applicant is culturally fluent, appreciative of diversity, and committed to inclusive action. They should be comfortable interacting with people from all over the world and have a mindset that extends beyond their own local context. UBC’s messaging (“welcoming community members from across the globe and fostering their inclusion and belonging”) indicates that students who exemplify global citizenship and inclusivity are highly valued. Whether through personal stories of collaboration across differences or involvement in global causes, showing a global perspective and a respect for diversity will align an applicant with UBC’s core institutional values.

## Personal Qualities and Mindset

Beyond concrete achievements, UBC places great emphasis on the personal qualities and mindset of applicants. The tone of the admissions materials is often reflective and values-driven – UBC wants to know who you are as a person and what you stand for, not just what you’ve done. Several key traits emerge repeatedly in UBC’s communications:

- Resilience and perseverance: UBC explicitly asks applicants to share challenges they have overcome, because how a student deals with adversity is very telling. The Personal Profile is described as the place to tell UBC “the challenges you’ve overcome” and what you’ve learned from those experiences. Ideal candidates often have stories of resilience – perhaps moving to a new country, overcoming personal hardship, or tackling academic obstacles – and importantly, they can articulate growth from those struggles. Some of UBC’s scholarships target resilient students: those who achieved excellence “under challenging circumstances” or from war-torn areas, for example. This suggests UBC admires grit, adaptability, and determination. International students, in particular, often face the challenge of adapting to a new environment, so demonstrating resilience and flexibility is a plus. A UBC student should be able to handle setbacks, manage stress, and thrive in a demanding, fast-changing university setting.  
      
    
- Reflection and maturity: The ideal candidate is self-aware and reflective. UBC doesn’t just want a list of accomplishments; it wants to know what the student learned and how they grew. Admissions tips advise: “Instead of simply listing your accomplishments and experiences, tell us what you’ve learned from them”. Scoring highly on the Personal Profile requires showing insight and “a rare level of accomplishment and maturity.” UBC seems to value an unusually mature outlook in undergraduates – someone who can reflect on their experiences with wisdom beyond their years. This could be demonstrated by discussing meaningful life lessons, ethical dilemmas faced, or how one’s perspective has evolved. A Psychology applicant, for example, might reflect on how volunteering with a mental health helpline taught them empathy and resilience. An Education applicant might discuss what working with children taught them about patience and communication. Being able to derive personal meaning from experiences and convey thoughtful, nuanced views is a trait that sets apart top candidates.  
      
    
- Integrity and accountability: As part of its core values, UBC highlights Integrity (honesty, ethics, truthfulness) and Accountability (taking responsibility for one’s actions). The ideal student thus has a strong moral compass. This could manifest in the application as examples of ethical leadership, doing the right thing in difficult situations, or taking ownership of one’s mistakes. UBC likely screens for any signs of misconduct or dishonesty and expects applicants to be truthful in their essays (as emphasized by warnings against trying to tell admissions “what you think we want to hear”). Involvement in initiatives that promote integrity (like honor societies or peer mentoring) could further underline this trait. Essentially, UBC wants community members who are trustworthy, responsible, and uphold high ethical standards – qualities critical in fields like Psychology and Education where ethical practice is paramount.  
      
    
- Empathy and collaboration: UBC’s tone around leadership and community is full of words like empathy, collaboration, and supporting others. This indicates that compassion and teamwork are highly valued. An ideal candidate often demonstrates care for others – maybe through mentoring peers, caring for family, or community service – and an ability to work in teams effectively. Group projects, team sports, or collaborative volunteer work in a student’s background can highlight this skill. UBC fosters a collegial atmosphere (“thriving communities” is part of its strategic priorities), so being able to get along with and uplift others is key. Particularly in Education, empathic communication and inclusivity are crucial traits for future teachers, and in Psychology, understanding others is at the heart of the discipline. Students who show emotional intelligence, listening skills, and respect for differing viewpoints align well with UBC’s ideal.  
      
    
- Passion and purpose: UBC asks applicants “What is important to you? And why?”. This question is aimed at uncovering an applicant’s values and motivations. Ideal UBC students have a clear sense of purpose or passion that drives them – whether it’s a passion for mental health advocacy, a dedication to education reform, a love of scientific research, or any other defining interest. UBC tends to highlight student stories where a personal passion led to accomplishments or contributions. Thus, showing genuine passion (rather than saying what you think the university wants to hear) is recommended: “Be true to who you are… Use your unique voice to tell us what you want to say.”. The underlying message is authenticity. UBC celebrates individuality; the tone of their admissions advice is that there is no single “cookie-cutter” ideal – instead, the ideal is that each student brings a unique passion or perspective backed by substance. Whether one is an aspiring neuroscientist or a budding social entrepreneur, UBC wants to see authentic enthusiasm and drive.  
      
    

In combination, these personal qualities describe someone who is balanced in heart and mind. UBC’s ideal student is ambitious yet humble, driven yet compassionate. They have the confidence to lead and achieve, but also the humility to learn and listen. They are open-minded, self-reflective, and growth-oriented, constantly evolving from their experiences. The repeated themes of inclusion, global citizenship, service, innovation, and excellence suggest a mindset geared towards both personal achievement and the greater good. Importantly, the ideal candidate’s values seem to align with UBC’s own institutional values – striving for excellence, acting with integrity, showing respect, and taking responsibility. UBC’s communications often highlight students who exemplify these traits in various ways, effectively holding them up as models for prospective applicants to emulate.

## Alignment with Psychology and Education Program Expectations

While the above traits apply broadly, students targeting Psychology or Education programs should pay special attention to aligning with the specific ethos of those faculties:

- Psychology: UBC’s Psychology Department is a part of the Faculty of Arts and is one of the most academically rigorous and prestigious programs at the university. As UBC’s “most popular undergraduate major”, admission to Psychology can be quite competitive. Academically, an ideal Psychology applicant has strong preparation in biology, math/stats, and social sciences, indicating they can handle the scientific and research aspects of the discipline. The department emphasizes research at all levels – undergraduates have opportunities for enriched learning experiences, lab involvement, and even an Honours thesis program. Therefore, a demonstrated interest or experience in research can set candidates apart (e.g. science fair projects, psychology club, or assisting a research lab if possible). The department also values critical thinking and curiosity about human behavior. On a personal level, psychology intersects with understanding people, so qualities like empathy, communication, and analytical thinking are important. The Psychology Department explicitly states a commitment to an inclusive, supportive community, so students who will contribute to a positive department culture are desired. Being aware of ethical issues and showing a desire to help others (for those interested in clinical/counseling avenues) can also be positives. In essence, the ideal Psychology student at UBC is academically strong in science and analysis, passionate about understanding mind and behavior, open to research, and attuned to working with diverse people. They will likely take initiative in things like the Psychology Students Association, research conferences (UBC frequently celebrates undergrad research achievements), and community outreach on mental health.  
      
    
- Education: UBC’s Faculty of Education has a distinct focus on preparing future teachers and educational leaders who are socially conscious and community-oriented. Unlike Psychology, undergraduate entry to Education often means pursuing a B.Ed. program after completing another bachelor’s degree (the B.Ed. is a post-degree professional program at UBC). Thus, an “undergraduate candidate” in Education might be a transfer or second-degree applicant. Whether one is applying to UBC’s B.Ed. or a related Bachelor’s (like a BA with an Education minor, or a specialization in Early Childhood Education), the expected qualities are very clear. UBC Education’s mission statement highlights improving teaching and learning through innovative, inquiry-driven programs, research, reflection, and critique in partnership with communities. They “seek to inform our research and practices by consideration of issues such as poverty, minority rights, inequality, and social justice”. This means the ideal Education student is deeply committed to social justice, diversity, and the well-being of communities. Practical experience is also crucial: the Teacher Education program strongly prefers applicants who have experience working with youth or children and can obtain references attesting to their abilities in real educational settings. So, a prospective Education student should ideally have volunteered or worked in schools, summer camps, tutoring programs, or similar environments, showing both commitment to teaching and understanding of learners. Other key traits include patience, communication skills, creativity in teaching, and an inclusive mindset (to support learners of all needs and backgrounds). UBC’s Education faculty values reflection and ethical practice – they ask their members to be “reflective, ethical, and self-critical” and to value multiple perspectives. An ideal candidate might demonstrate this by talking about lessons learned from working with diverse students or how they adapted to challenges in a teaching role. They should also show leadership potential in educational contexts (e.g., curriculum design in a club, leading workshops, etc.), aligned with the faculty’s emphasis on producing leaders in education. In short, the ideal Education program applicant is service-oriented, passionate about teaching and equity, experienced with youth, academically competent, and eager to advance the cause of inclusive, quality education. This aligns with UBC’s portrayal of its Education students and alumni as champions of change in their communities.  
      
    

By tailoring their applications to reflect these department-specific expectations – research enthusiasm and academic rigor for Psychology, and educational passion with social awareness for Education – international candidates can demonstrate an even stronger “fit” with UBC. The underlying institutional values (excellence, integrity, global outlook, community engagement) remain the same, but their expression may differ by discipline. UBC’s tone on faculty pages is proud of the unique strengths of each faculty: Psychology’s cutting-edge research and scientific breadth, and Education’s role in fostering inclusive, lifelong learning and community well-being. Ideal candidates affirm these priorities through their interests and experiences.

## Conclusion

Across its communications, UBC conveys a clear picture of the kind of student it hopes to attract: scholars who excel academically and who carry a spark – be it leadership, creativity, compassion, or vision – that they use to uplift others and contribute to a better world. The tone is aspirational but inviting; UBC signals that it values excellence not as an elitist concept, but as a combination of talent, hard work, character, and a commitment to positive change. From the Personal Profile prompts to the scholarship criteria and faculty mission statements, UBC consistently emphasizes qualities like global citizenship, inclusion, community impact, and resilience. International students applying to UBC, especially in fields like Psychology and Education, should present themselves as holistic candidates: not only meeting high academic standards, but also bringing rich experiences, diverse perspectives, leadership and volunteerism, and a strong alignment with UBC’s values. In the end, UBC’s ideal undergraduate is someone who will make the most of the university’s resources and actively contribute to the campus ethos of learning, engagement, and service.
