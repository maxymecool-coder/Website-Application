# Neural Circuits In ADHD
**Details**
The neurological underpinnings of ADHD can be understood as a dysfunctional relationship between two of the brain's major operating systems: the **Default Mode Network (DMN)** and **Task-Related Networks**. In a neurotypical brain, these networks operate like a seesaw; they are **anti-correlated**, meaning when one is active, the other is suppressed. The DMN is the brain's "idle" state, responsible for mind-wandering and self-referential thought, while Task-Related Networks engage when we need to focus on a goal-oriented, external task. This opposition allows for a clean switch between internal reflection and external focus.

In the ADHD brain, this critical anti-correlation is broken. The two networks are often active at the same time, creating a state of internal conflict and neural noise. It's akin to trying to listen to an important lecture while a loud, distracting conversation is happening right next to you—except the distracting conversation is coming from inside your own head. This makes it incredibly difficult to filter out internal chatter and sustain focus on the outside world. The neuromodulator **dopamine** acts as the "conductor" responsible for orchestrating this delicate seesaw balance. In ADHD, the weak or dysregulated dopamine signal means the conductor can't keep the two networks in their proper, opposing rhythm, leading to the core symptoms of distractibility and an inability to stay on task.
