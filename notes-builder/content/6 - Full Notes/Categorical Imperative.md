# Categorical Imperative
**Details**
To properly grasp Immanuel Kant’s moral framework, we must strictly separate the concept of the "rational" from the concept of the "reasonable." When an individual formulates a maxim—a personal policy for action—we can evaluate its rationality using a hypothetical imperative. A hypothetical imperative simply asks whether the chosen means are effectively calculated to achieve the agent's subjective desires or aims. However, Kant argues that our empirical desires cannot serve as the foundation of morality. Instead, true morality requires the Categorical Imperative Procedure, which represents the "reasonable." In Kant's philosophy, the reasonable has absolute priority over the rational and subordinates it completely. When an agent tests their intended action against the Categorical Imperative, they are never allowed to use the intensity or urgency of their personal desires to counterbalance or override the moral law. If an intended action fails the procedure, the agent must abandon or alter their maxim, even if it means repressing a deeply held desire.
