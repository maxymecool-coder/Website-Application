# Generalized Other
**Details**
George Herbert Mead's concept of the "generalized other" describes the crucial mental step we take when we move beyond understanding individual roles (like "mommy" or "teacher") to internalizing the abstract expectations of society as a whole. This isn't about any specific person, but rather the composite attitude of our entire social group. We develop this by participating in organized activities, like games, where success requires us to anticipate the actions and expectations of every other player simultaneously. By taking the perspective of this "team," we learn to see ourselves through the eyes of the community. This internal viewpoint becomes our guide for social conduct, allowing us to coordinate our actions with others and, most importantly, to form a stable sense of self judged against the norms and values of our society.

**Supporting Content**
Babies do not inherently have self-awareness, instead hey have “other-awareness,” which is imitation and monitoring of their caregivers and other people. And so we build our concept of ourselves not through reflection but from other people’s reaction to our being. According to sociologist George Herbert Mead, this is “reflected appraisal.” Over time, this generates a construct for who we are known as the “generalized other.” Whenever we engage in any societal endeavors, we are engaging with what other thinks of us and values in us.
