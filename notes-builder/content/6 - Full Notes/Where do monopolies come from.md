# Where do monopolies come from
**Details**
Monopolies are an extreme case that usually doesn’t happen. I say usually because sometimes it does. So where do these cases come from?

The first is a natural monopoly: Some markets have a fixed cost so large relative to marginal cost that average cost keeps falling over every quantity anyone would buy (Figure 12-1). Gruber's examples are a rock quarry and a water utility. Laying pipes to every house costs a fortune; pumping the water through them costs almost nothing. Average cost does eventually turn up in theory, but only at quantities nobody demands.

In such a market, we can understandably see how it would support the existence of a monopoly. Say if someone already paid an enormous price to have the pipes in one town, they would be able to control the marginal costs between everything. So when someone is looking to enter the market, the monopolist can just lower its’ prices immensely so the marginal revenue would never fulfill the fixed cost, barring entry. Then it raises its’ prices again.

A worked example (not in the lecture) makes the regulation problem concrete. Say demand is Q = 100 − P and cost is C = 600 + 20Q, so marginal cost is a constant 20 and average cost, 600/Q + 20, falls forever.

- **Unregulated:** MR = 100 − 2Q = 20 gives Q = 40 at P = 60. Profit is 2,400 − 1,400 = 1,000. The deadweight loss is ½ × (80 − 40) × (60 − 20) = 800.
- **Price = marginal cost (20):** 80 units are sold, the efficient amount, but revenue is 1,600 against costs of 2,200. The firm loses 600, exactly its fixed cost, so it won't operate without a subsidy.
- **Price = average cost:** solving 100 − Q = 600/Q + 20 gives Q ≈ 71.6 and P ≈ 28.4. The firm breaks even, and the deadweight loss shrinks to ½ × 8.4 × 8.4 ≈ 35.

That's why regulators of utilities don't set price at marginal cost. They allow marginal cost plus a "fair" rate of return, which is roughly average-cost pricing.

Then we have the monopolies the government creates. Governments sometimes run a service themselves, such as the postal service, and in young economies often airlines, steel or banks; these usually move to the private sector as economies mature. The more common route is property rights: copyrights (Lecture 11) and patents. A patent is an exclusive right to sell an invention for a fixed period.

Patents involve a genuine trade-off:

- **The cost:** a patent creates a monopoly, with its deadweight loss.
- **The benefit:** without patents, a competitor could copy an invention the moment it appeared and sell it at marginal cost, so nobody would bother inventing.

So the optimal patent is neither zero years nor forever. It's somewhere in between, long enough to reward innovation without hurting consumers more than necessary.

Pharmaceuticals show both sides. Roughly 1 compound in 10,000 becomes an effective drug, and after animal tests and three phases of human trials, the average new drug costs about $2 billion. The patent lets the inventor recoup that; when it expires, generic makers enter and the price should fall to marginal cost. Practically, however, it isn’t that simple:

1. **Pay-for-delay.** A brand-name company making $1 billion a year can pay a would-be generic maker, say, $500 million to stay out. The generic maker gets paid for doing nothing, and the brand keeps its monopoly.
2. **Evergreening.** Small tweaks earn new patents. Insulin is cheap to make, and its inventors gave away the patent for $1 so it would be widely available. Yet it's expensive, because a stream of genuine but modest improvements in how it's delivered keeps renewing patent protection.
3. **Generic markets aren't fully competitive.** Epinephrine is generic, yet an EpiPen costs about $600: the injector device is expensive to make and few firms enter.
4. **Biologics** — drugs made from living cells rather than simple chemicals — are hard to copy, so generic competition may barely exist for the newest class of drugs.

The innovations are real. Spinal muscular atrophy is a random genetic disease that used to kill babies by age 2. A gene therapy now fixes it by injecting a virus carrying a corrected gene. It costs $2.1 million. That's arguably a bargain for 80 or more years of life — and a country that paid that much for everything would go broke. Gruber notes that President Biden's Inflation Reduction Act lets the government regulate some drug prices for the first time, a sign that policymakers think the patent balance had tipped too far.
