# Limiting entry
**Details**
Before Uber and Lyft, taxis should have been a textbook competitive market. All you need is a car, which is a low fixed cost, and cabs are close to identical. Instead, cities capped the number of cabs. Only holders of a medallion (once literally a metal medallion, now a paper permit) could legally drive one.

Gruber's Figure 10-3 has two panels: one cab on the left, the market on the right.

- **Free entry.** Each identical cab runs where P1 = MC = minimum AC, giving q1 rides, its most efficient amount. At P1, riders want Q1 rides in total, so the number of cabs is n1 = Q1/q1. If there were fewer cabs, the price would be higher and profits positive, and new cabs would enter. So long-run supply, S1, is flat at P1.
- **A cap of n2 cabs.** The new supply curve, S2, is flat at P1 up to n2 × q1 rides, because until then each cab can stay at its efficient q1. Past that point, extra rides have to come from the same cabs driving more than q1 — longer hours, more tired, at less convenient times — so marginal cost rises and S2 slopes up. S2 meets demand at a higher price P2, with fewer total rides Q2.
- Each cab now sets P2 = MC, gives q2 > q1 rides, and earns a profit π = (P2 − AC) × q2.

|   |   |   |
|---|---|---|
||Free entry|n2 medallions|
|Consumer surplus|A + B + C|A|
|Producer surplus|0|B (= n2 × π)|
|Deadweight loss|—|C|

Producer surplus under free entry is zero because long-run competitive profit is zero (Lecture 8). The cap moves B from riders to cab owners and destroys C. Unlike the gas cap, there's no rationing cost here: the market clears at P2, so anyone who values a ride above P2 gets one.

Is the transfer B fair? If riders are rich bankers and drivers are poor immigrants, maybe it's a good thing; if riders are poor people getting to work, maybe not. Gruber's twist is that it doesn't matter, because medallions can be sold. Someone wanting to drive would pay up to π for a medallion, since owning one earns π. So drivers who buy or rent medallions are back to zero profit. Competition still drives profit to zero — not through entry, but by selling the barrier to entry. The winners are whoever was originally handed medallions when the system began, back in the 1930s. Renting one cost about $12,000 a year in San Francisco; buying one cost about $400,000 in Boston. Gruber puts the lost consumer surplus at about $2 billion.

Why have such rules at all? Some regulation makes sense: you don't want just anyone driving strangers around, and you want doctors licensed — otherwise you get Dr. Nick, the quack from The Simpsons who trained at "Hollywood Upstairs Medical College." This is **occupational licensing**: rules that check people can do the job they claim to do. But licensing should test quality. A fixed cap on the number of cabs just hands money to medallion holders.

Then came Uber. Ride-hailing effectively restored free entry, so the price fell back toward P1. Taxi and Uber drivers earn a little less than taxi drivers did, but they were never making much. Medallion values fell about 90%, because the profits they capitalized on were gone. Gruber calls medallion owners not a sympathetic crowd. The big winners are riders: total surplus and consumer surplus both rose. It's a case of a market innovation increasing both.
