# Long-run competition
**Details**
Producer theory is consumer theory plus one step. The production function is like a utility function, and input prices are like the prices of pizza and cookies. But there's no income: the firm chooses how much to produce. So instead of one tangency between an isoquant and an isocost line, there's one for every output level. Together those trace out the supply curve, which is the marginal cost curve above the shutdown point.

We did this in [[Short-run competition (and perfect competition)]], but now the long-run competition would introduce something else: **free entry and exit**. Firms can come and go as they please:

- They enter when profits are positive.
- They exit when profits are negative.

In the long run there's no separate shutdown decision; only the sign of profit matters. That harmless-looking assumption has a stark implication: **long-run profits are zero**.

Gruber's first example is the personal computer market around 1990 (Figure 8-1), with a few firms like Dell and Gateway, all assumed identical. The left panel shows one firm, Dell. Its marginal cost slopes up. Its average cost curve is U-shaped: the fixed cost gets spread over more units, and then rising marginal costs take over. MC crosses AC at AC's lowest point. The entry would turn out:

1. At the market price P1, Dell produces q1, where P1 = MC. Its profit is the rectangle π1 = (P1 − AC) × q1.
2. Those profits draw in new firms. With more identical firms, the market supply curve flattens, from S1 to S2.
3. The flatter supply curve meets demand at a lower price, P2, and a larger market quantity, Q2.
4. At P2, Dell cuts back to q2, and its profit shrinks to π2.

Dell's own output falls (q2 < q1) while the market's rises (Q2 > Q1), because there are more firms. As long as any profit remains, more firms keep entering, until profits hit zero.

The mirror case is mainframe computers (Figure 8-2). At Gruber's 1987 MIT graduation, the speaker was Ken Olsen, an MIT alum who founded DEC, then a giant of big computers. Olsen spent the speech on how wonderful he and DEC were. Within about a decade, PCs had wiped DEC out. (Compaq bought what was left in 1998.) Gruber's lesson for graduation speakers: don't spend the whole speech praising yourself.

Take IBM, which had bet heavily on mainframes:

1. As demand for mainframes collapsed, the price P1 fell below IBM's average cost, so it was losing money.
2. It still covered its average variable cost, so it was above its shutdown point and kept producing (the Lecture 7 shutdown rule).
3. Losses drive firms out. Exit steepens the market supply curve and raises the price.
4. Exit continues until the price reaches P2, where P = MC = AC and profits are zero.

The proof that every firm ends up at minimum average cost (Figure 8-3) takes three steps:

1. Zero profit means P = AC.
2. Profit maximization means P = MC.
3. So MC = AC, which happens only at the minimum of average cost.

You might object that profits obviously aren't zero: Apple makes enormous amounts of money. Short-run profits are allowed, but firms like General Motors have made profits for decades. Gruber gives three reasons long-run profits can stay positive, or at least why the simple model breaks.

**1. Barriers to entry.** Firms don't enter freely, and the ultimate barrier is a sunk cost. Think of becoming a doctor as an economic decision. If being a doctor weren't profitable enough to repay about $300,000 of medical school, you wouldn't go. And well, if profits won't cover the cost of entry, it isn't worth it. Profits don't need to be enormous but it can't be zero either.

**2. Firms aren't identical.** If firms differ in their minimum average cost, the cheapest ones can profit even in the long run. Gruber's example, from an old empirical study, is cotton (Figure 8-4). Each country is treated as having a flat marginal cost per kilogram, up to some capacity:

- Pakistan: $0.71
- Argentina: $1.08
- Australia: $1.15
- …and so on, up to the United States at $1.56

The market supply curve is a staircase. If world demand is 1 billion kilograms, Pakistan supplies it all at $0.71. Price equals marginal cost equals average cost, so profit is zero.

If demand is 5 billion kilograms, the market has to reach the US step, so the price is $1.56. Every lower-cost producer now earns the gap on each kilogram: Pakistan 1.56 − 0.71 = $0.85, Argentina $0.48, Australia $0.41, and the US $0.

**3. Input prices rise with output.** So far the wage has been taken as given; now it comes from a labor market (Figure 8-5).

- **Demand for workers slopes down.** At a higher wage, firms switch toward capital and hire fewer workers; that's the isoquant–isocost logic.
- **Supply of workers slopes up.** At higher wages, more people want to work, and to work more. This is only an assumption for now. A later lecture shows it can even slope down, through income and substitution effects.

Here's the chain of events:
1. If people want more of a good, firms need more workers, so the demand for labor shifts out.
2. With supply sloping up, the only way to get more workers is to pay more, so the wage rises.
3. A higher wage raises every firm's marginal and average cost (MC1, AC1 to MC2, AC2, Figure 8-6).
4. So the zero-profit price, minimum AC, is higher.

The long-run supply curve slopes up, yet every firm still makes zero profit at its minimum AC.

The key distinction: cases 1 and 2 give positive long-run profits, while case 3 gives an upward-sloping long-run supply curve with zero profits. So an upward-sloping long-run supply curve doesn't by itself prove anyone is making profits.

A student asked how you'd tell the cases apart. First check whether long-run profits are zero. If they are, it's the base case or case 3; if they're positive, it's case 1 or 2. Then, to separate the base case from case 3, ask whether wages rise as the industry grows. To separate cases 1 and 2, ask whether there are sunk costs and entry barriers, or whether firms simply differ in cost.

Another student asked about accounting versus economic profit. Apple's roughly $18 billion a quarter is accounting profit. Economic profit subtracts opportunity costs too, which makes it much harder to measure. Even accounting profit is slippery. Gruber says Apple reports billions to shareholders but almost nothing to tax authorities, by claiming its profits come from intellectual property held by an Irish company with two employees. That's one reason "just tax profits" is harder than it sounds: profits are hard to define.
