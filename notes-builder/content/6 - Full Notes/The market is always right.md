# The market is always right
**Details**
The **First Fundamental Theorem of Welfare Economics** says that **competition maximizes welfare**. Gruber credits the grand name to Paul Samuelson, "not a humble man." Define welfare as consumer surplus plus producer surplus, weighting consumers and producers equally. Then the competitive equilibrium that Lecture 7 derived as a positive prediction is also the normatively best outcome.

Gruber shows it with a price ceiling (Figure 9-12). Someone argues that lower prices are always better for consumers and caps the price at P2, below the equilibrium P1. At P2, producers supply only Q2 because they must stay on their supply curve, so only Q2 is traded. The areas are:

- **R:** consumer surplus that survives (above P1, below demand, up to Q2)
- **S:** the rectangle between P2 and P1 on the Q2 units still sold
- **T:** producer surplus that survives (below P2, above supply)
- **U:** producer surplus on the units between Q2 and Q1 that are no longer made
- **V:** consumer surplus on those same lost units

Before the cap, consumer surplus is R + V and producer surplus is S + T + U. After it:

- Consumers lose V on trades that no longer happen, but gain S because they pay less on the units they still buy. Consumer surplus becomes R + S.
- Producers lose S to consumers and U on lost trades. Producer surplus becomes T.

S is a _transfer_: it moves from producers to consumers. Since welfare is the sum of the two, transfers don't change it. U and V simply vanish. That vanished amount is the **deadweight loss**: the net loss from trades that would have made both sides better off but don't happen. The competitive equilibrium has no deadweight loss, because every trade that benefits both sides gets made. Any departure from it removes some of those trades and lowers welfare.

Here are worked numbers (not in the lecture), using Lecture 7's market (Q = 48 − P, Q = 0.6P, P1 = 30, Q1 = 18) with a cap at $20:

- Producers supply 0.6 × 20 = 12 units. The 12th unit was worth 48 − 12 = $36 to its buyer.
- The areas are R = 144, S = 120, T = 120, U = 30 and V = 18.
- Before the cap, consumer surplus is 162 and producer surplus 270, a total of 432.
- After it, consumer surplus is 264 and producer surplus 120, a total of 384.
- The deadweight loss is U + V = 48, which matches ½ × 6 × (36 − 20).

Consumers as a group gain here, from 162 to 264, because the transfer S outweighs the trades V they lose. A price ceiling can help consumers and still shrink the total.

Under these assumptions, Gruber says, we should even let people sell kidneys on eBay: the poor seller and the rich buyer are both better off. Lecture 1 covered why we might object: fairness, and markets that may not work well. Those are exactly the assumptions the course relaxes later. That's why Gruber calls economics, at bottom, a right-wing science. Its starting point is that under perfect competition the market knows best and should be left alone. Everything after builds from there.
