# Dominance, the Prisoner's Dilemma, and why cooperation needs enforcement
**Details**
A situation becomes strategic when the outcomes depend on other people's actions as well as your own. Perfect competition involves no strategy because firms would simply dominate or be comfortable with what they have. Everything in between these two outcomes, however, is strategic.

The first factor is dominance. Strategy X _strictly dominates_ strategy Y if X pays you strictly more than Y whatever the others do. And, well, in the market, or strategy in general, one should never play a strictly dominated strategy, as switching to the dominant strategy is better.

Polak's grade game shows how this works. Each student secretly writes α or β. α against β gets you an A and your partner a C, two αs get B− each, and two βs get B+ each. Here are the payoffs for someone who cares only about their own grade (A = 3, B+ = 1, B− = 0, C = −1; your payoff first):

α beats β whether the partner picks α (0 > −1) or β (3 > 1), so α dominates. Two students picked α for different reasons. One predicted her partner would pick α and replied to that. The other saw that α was better whatever the partner did. The second argument is stronger. It needs no prediction about the other person, only your own payoffs, and it contains the first as a special case.

Lesson 2 is the Prisoner's Dilemma's point: individually rational choices can produce an outcome that's bad for everyone. The term is Pareto-inefficient, meaning another outcome would leave someone better off and nobody worse off. Both players choose α and get 0 when both could have had 1. This only needs saying because economics long held that self-interest adds up to efficiency. That is true under perfect competition, which is the non-strategic case.

Of course, it could become efficient the other way around, but achieving that efficiency is practically unachievable because people have different goals and lines of reasoning. Furthermore, if everyone picks Beta, then the dominant strategy becomes picking Alpha.

So, the obstacle to mutual benefits is enforcement. If the two players can promise each other that they won't take advantage of each other's absence, and that doing so would warrant betrayal.

The most obscure way this shows itself is through morality, as it makes the person experience guilt and indignation as payoffs.
