# Glucocorticoids and the Stress-Depression Cycle
**Details**
The body's primary stress hormones, **glucocorticoids** (cortisol in humans), play a central role in the biology of depression. In a healthy stress response, cortisol levels rise to meet a challenge and then fall once the challenge is over. In major depression, this system is broken.

Individuals with depression often exist in a state of **hypercortisolism**, meaning they have chronically elevated levels of cortisol. This is because the brain's negative feedback loop, which is supposed to detect high cortisol levels and signal the adrenal glands to stop producing it, is impaired.

This creates a destructive, self-perpetuating vicious cycle:

1. Psychological stress (sad thoughts, rumination) triggers the release of cortisol.
    
2. High levels of cortisol contribute to the neurobiological changes of depression (e.g., hippocampal atrophy).
    
3. The state of depression itself acts as a powerful, chronic stressor, which keeps cortisol levels pathologically high, further damaging the brain and deepening the depression.
    

This constant internal state of emergency helps explain the profound exhaustion and vegetative symptoms of the illness. The body is always "on," fighting a battle that has no external cause and no clear end as the MPFC is overdosing on cortisol, making the amygdala constantly report threats back to the body.
