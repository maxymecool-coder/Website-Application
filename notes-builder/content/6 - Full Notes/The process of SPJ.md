# The process of SPJ
**Details**
The SPJ process involved a synthesis of these data streams. The SCU practitioner would first consider the level of concern generated by the QuAIC. They would then review the Triage Warning Signs Checklist to determine if any specific red flags were present. Finally, they would integrate these structured inputs with their own professional judgment—informed by the specifics of the case, the context of the report, and their professional experience—to determine a final, overall **"triage risk level"**.  

This final risk level was categorized into a five-tier system designed to directly inform institutional action: "NA" (not assessed), "low," "moderate," "high," and "highest". Each of these tiers was linked to a pre-defined and escalating management response. A "low" risk case would trigger a response within five working days, typically resolved at a local level. A "moderate" risk case required a response within three working days and formal case management. A "high" risk case mandated a response within one business day and a potential referral to Student Misconduct. Finally, a case deemed "highest" risk required "immediate escalation" and a potential referral to the university's multi-disciplinary Safety and Triage (SAT) team.  

This use of an SPJ framework represents a sophisticated strategy for enhancing the quality, consistency, and defensibility of the university's threat assessment decisions. Relying solely on a quantitative score from a single tool like the QuAIC, while standardized, could be criticized as overly simplistic or mechanistic, potentially missing critical contextual factors unique to a case. Conversely, relying solely on unstructured professional judgment, while flexible, is vulnerable to charges of being subjective, inconsistent, and biased.

The SPJ model implemented at Victoria University skillfully mitigates both weaknesses. The QuAIC and the checklist provide the "structured," "evidence-based" foundation, ensuring that key risk domains are systematically considered in every case. The "professional judgment" component provides the necessary flexibility to integrate nuance, context, and case-specific information that a rigid algorithm might miss. This creates a transparent and highly defensible decision-making pathway. A practitioner can clearly articulate their rationale: for instance, "The QuAIC generated a 'Moderate' level of concern based on the reported intrusion and persistence. However, the Triage Warning Signs Checklist flagged the presence of 'last-resort thinking' by the IoC and 'significant distress' in the target. Therefore, exercising professional judgment, the final triage risk level was elevated to 'High,' triggering an immediate referral for management." This type of documented, multi-faceted reasoning demonstrates a thorough and thoughtful assessment, which serves to protect the institution from liability while simultaneously protecting the campus community from unmanaged risk.
