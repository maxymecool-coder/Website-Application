# MMH as an Unwarranted and Unmotivated Commitment
**Details**
The commitment to the Massive Modularity Hypothesis (MMH) by many evolutionary psychologists is, at present, both unwarranted by the arguments and unmotivated by the needs of the research program [cite: Samuels_1998.pdf].

The primary arguments for MMH, such as the optimality and solvability arguments, fail to provide a compelling reason to prefer a massively modular architecture over a competing model like the Library Model of Cognition (LMC). These arguments successfully demonstrate the need for innate, domain-specific cognitive structure, but they do not successfully argue that this structure must take the form of computational _mechanisms_ rather than representational _bodies of knowledge_ [cite: Samuels_1998.pdf].

Furthermore, the existing empirical evidence, particularly for central cognition, is unable to distinguish between the two hypotheses. Plausible explanations for the same data can be generated from both frameworks, resulting in an empirical stalemate [cite: Samuels_1998.pdf].

Finally, the rejection of LMC is unmotivated because the model is fully compatible with the core tenets of evolutionary psychology. An evolutionary approach to the mind requires a commitment to innate, evolved, adaptive specializations, but it does not require that these specializations be modules in the sense advocated by MMH [cite: Samuels_1998.pdf]. Therefore, if the case for massive modularity is to be made, it cannot rest on general, a priori evolutionary arguments. It will depend on the "successive accumulation of specific, empirical evidence for the existence of particular modules"—a project that is still in its infancy [cite: Samuels_1998.pdf].
