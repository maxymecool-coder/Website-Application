# Propositions against Skepticism
**Details**
In the 20th century, philosophers like Roy Harrod and Simon Blackburn tried to solve Hume's problem using probability. They used the analogy of a traveler crossing a desert of unknown length.

If the traveler constantly predicts "The desert will continue for another 10% of the distance I've already traveled," they will be right 10 out of 11 times. (e.g., at mile 100, they predict 10 more miles. If the desert ends at 110, they are right until the very last prediction). Since this method has a 10/11 success rate, induction is rationally justified.

Some argue against this that the probability is sequential, as a probability of a former group that was being evaluated would naturally not be useful after that group has "expired". Its as if buying lottery tickets for weeks and weeks, but of course, those of the previous week would have expired once you get to evaluating for the current week.

One other prominent attempt, championed by Max Black, is the "Inductive Justification of Induction." This argument suggests that while we cannot use an inductive _premise_ to justify induction (which would be circular), we might be able to use an inductive _rule_.

Professor Millican argues that this distinction is essentially semantic hair-splitting that misses the sheer radicalism of Hume’s attack. Hume is not merely pointing out that induction lacks the certainty of deductive logic (like mathematics); he is arguing that we have absolutely _no_ reason—deductive, inductive, or sensory—to believe that the past is a reliable guide to the future. Whether you call it a rule or a premise, any attempt to validate induction by pointing to its past success is circular. It assumes the very thing in question: that the "success of the past" has any bearing on the "success of the future."
