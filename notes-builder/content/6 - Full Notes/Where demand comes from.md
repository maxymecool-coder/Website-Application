# Where demand comes from
**Details**
Obviously, as the root of the chain of supply and demand, we would naturally have to understand where it comes from. These don’t necessarily begin from the already given variables of prices or budgets but rather of preferences: what people want, how they rank alternatives, and how those rankings can be formalized enough to produce a mathematical demand curve later. The important move is that economics treats consumer choice as constrained optimization, but before adding the constraint, it first asks what the consumer would want in a world where the budget constraint is temporarily ignored. This is why the lecture begins by imagining that everyone has effectively won the lottery. Without worrying about affordability, what combinations of goods does the consumer prefer?

To model preferences, economics imposes three simplifying assumptions. The first is completeness, which is when the consumer is faced with a choice they can rank them: being preferring or being indifferent. The second is transitivity, meaning that if a consumer prefers A to B and B to C, then they must prefer A to C. This prevents preference rankings from becoming internally incoherent. The third is non-satiation, meaning that more of a good is always better than less. This does not mean each additional unit gives the same happiness as the previous one, only that an extra unit of a good is never harmful or unwanted within the model. The assumption applies to goods, not bads. If something is harmful, like being poked in the eye with a needle, then it is not being modeled as a good in the relevant sense
