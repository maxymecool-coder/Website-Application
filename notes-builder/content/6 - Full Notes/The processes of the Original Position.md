# The processes of the Original Position
**Details**
Rawls splits his argument for the principles of justice into 2 distinct phases. The first involves us to presume that the parties involved are rational but are absolutely _not_ swayed by "special psychologies"—most notably, envy. Rawls defines envy in a very specific, stringent way: it is the destructive desire to see others have less, even if achieving that reduction means _you_ also end up with less, simply to close the gap in social standing. The first phase assumes this specific kind of envy does not drive the parties. The second phase of the argument (addressed much later in Chapters 8 and 9) acts as a stability check: it evaluates whether the chosen principles, when actually implemented in the real world, would inadvertently generate destabilizing levels of envy that would tear the society apart.

To be able to determine the best principles for the first phase, OP relies on "two-way comparisons." Meaning Rawls would pit the principles against historically significant alternatives on the menu. The most fundamental of these is the First Comparison: The Two Principles of Justice vs. The Principle of Average Utility (which seeks to design institutions to maximize the per capita well-being of society). This comparison is paramount because Rawls's primary goal in the book is to prove that Justice as Fairness provides a superior, more stable normative foundation for a democratic society than the dominant utilitarian tradition.
