# ADHD vs. ADD, Genetics, IQ, Rates in Kids & Adults
**Details**
Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder, or ADHD, is a condition with a longer history than many realize, first appearing in medical literature around 1904 under the name Attention Deficit Disorder (ADD). The name evolved in the mid-to-late 1980s to more accurately include the hyperactivity component that so often accompanies the attentional challenges. This crucial change in terminology also led to a significant improvement in diagnostic accuracy and public understanding. ADHD is not a failure of character but a neurodevelopmental condition with deep biological roots, stemming from a strong genetic component that influences how the brain's circuits are wired and how they use key neurochemicals. The heritability is remarkably high; an identical twin of someone with ADHD has about a 75% chance of also having it, while a fraternal twin has a 50-60% chance. This genetic link underscores that it is a condition of brain function, not a choice.

A critical and often misunderstood point is that ADHD has absolutely no correlation with a person's intelligence. It is a disorder of attentional _regulation_, not a deficit in cognitive _capacity_. Individuals with high, average, or low IQs are found across the spectrum of those with and without the condition. The struggle lies in directing and sustaining focus, not in the ability to think or learn. Today, it's estimated that 10-12% of children have ADHD, with about half of those cases potentially resolving with effective treatment. Simultaneously, we are seeing a notable rise in adult ADHD diagnoses. This has sparked a debate as to whether these are cases that went undetected in childhood or if the relentless demands and constant context-switching of modern life, particularly driven by smartphones, are capable of inducing ADHD-like symptoms in the adult brain.
