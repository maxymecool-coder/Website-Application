# The Empirical Stalemate in Central Cognition
**Details**
While the theoretical distinction between the Massive Modularity Hypothesis (MMH) and the Library Model of Cognition (LMC) can be debated, the more pressing issue is whether empirical evidence can distinguish between them. Currently, for central cognitive processes like reasoning and belief-fixation, the evidence is ambiguous, leading to an empirical stalemate [cite: Samuels_1998.pdf].

There is substantial evidence for modularity in peripheral systems, such as vision and language processing. This evidence, often from neuropsychological studies of selective impairment, is well-established [cite: Samuels_1998.pdf]. However, since the LMC does not deny the existence of these modular "input systems," this data does not help differentiate it from MMH. The core of the disagreement lies in the architecture of central cognition.

The primary difficulty is that for any piece of evidence supporting a central module, a plausible counter-explanation can be constructed from the LMC framework. This is because both models predict innate, domain-specific structures. One model calls this structure a "module" (a mechanism), while the other calls it a "body of knowledge" (a representational system). Without a clear method for empirically distinguishing an innate specialized process from a general process operating on innate specialized data, the debate cannot be resolved by current experimental paradigms [cite: Samuels_1998.pdf]. This challenge is exemplified by the case of the "Theory of Mind" module.
