# What an Argument Is (and Isn't)
**Details**
An argument is a set of sentences where the premises entails a certain conclusion. It is where the conclusion is considered to be true and the premises are what gives rationale for it to be interpreted as so.

A sentence, then, can be interpreted differently depending on how they are presented and worded as it can be a conclusion and premise depending on the vocabularies that constitute it. There are many words in the English dictionary that can be quite misleading and make somebody infer the function of a sentence different than the function that it actually serves. This means you can't identify arguments by their content. You identify them by spotting the relation: is someone offering reasons for a claim? Words like _therefore_, _hence_, _so_, _since_ often signal arguments but aren't reliable — _because_ can indicate causal explanation rather than rational justification, and arguments can exist without any indicator words at all.

**Not arguments:**

- Mere contradictions ("Yes it is" / "No it isn't" — the Monty Python problem)
- Causal narratives ("Clouds formed, the sky blackened, the storm broke" — describing a sequence of events, not offering reasons)
- Single assertions, even complex ones ("If it's snowing, the mail will be late" — one conditional sentence, not a premise supporting a conclusion)
- Random sets of sentences with no inferential relation
