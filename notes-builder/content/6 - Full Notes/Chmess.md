# Chmess
**Details**
In 2006, Daniel C. Dennett wrote about a fictional game he called "chmess." This game, Dennett said, existed in the context of philosophy. I would suggest that it exists across all academic pursuits. The game of chmess is played by professional academics. In this game, a series of rules are mutually agreed upon. These rules may be Plato’s theories on this or that, Nietzsche’s ideas about some other thing. One individual enters the game of chmess. This happens in the form of papers, studies, or dissertations which are written about the agreed-upon rules. Another player engages the first individual, publishing some other thing about how the first player’s chmess moves were either triumphant or embarrassing. The two players fancy themselves quite good at chmess, and so they stick to it. The implications and applications of the rules are then debated, articulated, refined, and spun in circles. This goes on and on as more people enter the ongoing game, until we have a whole segment of individuals who have dedicated their lives to playing this game.

Thing is, all these people are just playing a game. They are not engaging with reality, the very reality that created the game, they are merely thinking thoughts upon thoughts of interpretation of that reality. The players are both parasites and producers, they cite each other work and make a result of their own, not knowing if that will even function in practice. We no longer have any experts on how to do a thing, but instead experts on how to do responses to a thing, or how to do a thing about a thing, or what is the nature of how to do a thing. We stop asking how knowledge can be applied to life, but instead how it can be applied to other knowledge. The result is some bizarrely irrelevant, self-sustaining cesspool of highly applauded works

Then the question becomes: "What do we do with all this knowledge when its only context is a self-contained circuit of other knowledge?"
