# Mischel's Cognitive-Affective Theory
**Details**
Walter Mischel expanded on the social cognitive perspective by emphasizing how our thoughts and interpretations shape our personality. He argued that in addition to learning behaviors, we also learn **expectations** about the outcomes of those behaviors and develop personal **constructs** or ways of interpreting situations. This leads to the development of unique **competencies**—skill sets we use to navigate different social situations.

For example, an extrovert and an introvert interpret a party with strangers very differently. The extrovert sees an exciting challenge, while the introvert sees a threatening situation. Each then deploys learned behaviors (competencies) to achieve their desired outcome in that context (e.g., being the center of attention vs. avoiding embarrassment).

**The Marshmallow Test and Delay of Gratification** Mischel's most famous research is the **"marshmallow test,"** a study on **delay of gratification**.

- **The Experiment:** Young children were placed in a room with a marshmallow and told they could eat the one marshmallow now, or, if they could wait a few minutes without eating it, they could have two marshmallows later.
    
- **The Findings:** The study revealed that children had developed different competencies for resisting temptation. Some distracted themselves, some turned away, and some couldn't resist at all.
    
- **Long-Term Correlation:** The most groundbreaking finding was that this early ability to delay gratification was a powerful predictor of long-term life outcomes. Decades later, the children who were able to wait for the second marshmallow reported higher rates of college graduation, more stable marriages, and greater overall life satisfaction.
    

Mischel's work showed that cognitive skills like self-control are core components of personality that have a profound and lasting impact on our lives.
