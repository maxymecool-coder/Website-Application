# The Two-Axis of QuAIC
**Details**
A central innovation of the QuAIC is its evaluation of problem behavior along two distinct axes: **Aggression** and **Intrusion**. This dual-axis model represents a significant departure from many traditional screening tools, which "focus largely on aggression" and may only trigger intervention when overt threats or physical violence occur. The elevation of "Intrusion" to an equal footing with "Aggression" is a deliberate and theoretically grounded design choice.  

The inclusion of the intrusion axis is explicitly informed by stalking research, which has highlighted that "the degree of intrusion and disruption to the lives of stalking targets cause as much, if not more, psychological harm as actual violence". The QuAIC operationalizes this concept by assessing intrusion across three distinct facets:  

**frequency** (how often the behavior occurs), **persistence** (how long it has been going on), and **disruption** (its impact on the target's life). This multi-faceted approach allows the tool to capture the cumulative and corrosive impact of behaviors that, when viewed in isolation, might appear "innocuous". Behaviors such as "rudeness, passive aggression, repeated unwanted correspondence" may not meet the threshold for aggression but can, through their relentless and disruptive nature, create a profoundly unsafe and harmful environment for the target.  

This reframing of threat is uniquely suited to the specific context of Australian university campuses as described in the source material. The report notes that, unlike in the United States where campus threat assessment models were forged in response to mass casualty incidents, such events are exceedingly rare in Australia. Instead, the most commonly reported problem behaviors involve "bullying, inappropriate communication, cyber-abuse, stalking, physical altercations, sexual harassment, communicated threats, and threatening behavior". These behaviors are characterized far more by their intrusive qualities—their unwantedness, persistence, and disruption to academic and social life—than by overt, targeted physical aggression.  

An assessment tool focused solely on a linear scale of aggression would be poorly calibrated to this reality. It would systematically fail to capture the severity and psychological harm associated with the most frequent types of campus concerns. The QuAIC's dual-axis framework, therefore, represents a direct methodological adaptation to the specific threat landscape of the environment in which it is being applied. It is engineered to be sensitive to the _actual_ problems that campuses like Victoria University most often face, not just the most extreme and feared scenarios.
