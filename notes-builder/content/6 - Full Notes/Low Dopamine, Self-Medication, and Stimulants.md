# Low Dopamine, Self-Medication, and Stimulants
**Details**
The leading theory for ADHD, the "low dopamine hypothesis," posits that the core symptoms arise because key brain circuits have insufficient levels of the neuromodulator dopamine. This chemical deficit creates "noisy" neural signaling, preventing the brain from properly coordinating its focus networks. This theory powerfully explains the common, and often unconscious, tendency for individuals with ADHD to self-medicate with substances that boost dopamine. The craving for sugary foods, the heavy consumption of caffeine, or even the use of illicit drugs like cocaine and amphetamine can be seen as the brain's attempt to correct its own chemical imbalance and achieve a state of focus and calm.

This brings us to the paradox of treating a hyperactivity disorder with stimulant medications like Ritalin and Adderall. These drugs are effective precisely because they address this underlying dopamine deficit. By increasing the amount of available dopamine in the brain, they effectively "turn up the volume" on the weak signals that are supposed to regulate attention. For a person with ADHD, this influx of dopamine doesn't cause more hyperactivity; instead, it provides the necessary chemical tool for the brain to organize itself. It allows the "conductor" (dopamine) to properly manage the brain's networks, suppressing distracting internal chatter and enabling sustained focus on external tasks. This is why a substance that would make a neurotypical person feel jittery can make a person with ADHD feel centered and in control.
