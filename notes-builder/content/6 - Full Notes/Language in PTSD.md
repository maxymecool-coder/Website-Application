# Language in PTSD
**Details**
The reason it is hard to communicate the trauma that one has is that it effectively makes one relive the old scenes in one’s head, or communicate with the other what transpired. When the latter was chosen, the patient would quickly lose touch and start just focusing on what the other opinion is. The slightest hint of doubt or judgement would shut them down, and would shift the attention to regaining their approval. 

A concept that we need to know is the distinct forms of self-awareness: one that keeps track of the self across time and the other in the present moment. The first, the autobiographical self, creates connections among experiences and makes a coherent story. It is rooted in our language. It can change with the change of our perspectives as others provide their own inputs. The other operates on a moment-to-moment basis instead, which is based in physical sensations, but if we feel safe and not rushed, we can also communicate these feelings into words. These two ways of knowing are localized in different parts of the brain that are largely disconnected from each other. 

Now to the important part, one of these system creates a story for the public, and if we tell that story often enough, we are likely to believe it is the whole truth. But the other registers a different truth, of the feeling that we have towards the situation. It is this that needs to be accessed, befriended and reconciled.
