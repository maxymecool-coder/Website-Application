# The fear of mathematics
**Details**
Most people think that they cannot read this at all. They all have their reasons for feeling such a way. But the problem is partly that math is a language, and we must learn it like one. Different from other languages, it only needs to be learnt once, not written and verbally but only written.

As so we should treat it like learning a language, we should search up syntaxes that we do not understand, concepts, expressions, everything.

Another point I want to make is about the beauty of math, which is not told enough, or because academic math is truly repetitive and boring. But if you start with an author like Euclid, you will be able to experience one of the most lucid and beautiful works of any kind that has ever been written (Elements of Geometry).

Moving on to propositions and their construction.
Constructions can be interpreted as serving another purpose. They bear an obvious similarity to postulates; both constructions and postulates assert that geometrical operations can be performed. In the case of the postulates, the possibility is assumed; in the case of the propositions, it is proved. The proof, of course, involves the use of the postulates. Thus, we might wonder, for example, whether there is really any such thing as an equilateral triangle, which is defined in Definition 20. Without troubling ourselves here about the thorny question of the existence of mathematical objects, we can at least see that Proposition 1 shows that, from the assumption that there are such things as straight lines and circles, it follows that there are such things as equilateral triangles.

Consider the difference between the argument of Proposition 5, as outlined here, and even the simplest of syllogisms, such as the following:
All animals are mortal;All men are animals;Therefore, all men are mortal. 
There is something satisfying about that, too. We can treat it as though it were a piece of mathematical reasoning. Assuming that there are such things as animals and men, and that animals are mortal, then the conclusion follows with the same certainty as the one about the angles of the triangle. But the trouble is that there really are animals and men; we are assuming something about real things, something that may or may not be true. But Euclid’s proposition does not suffer from this. He is saying if these things exist, then this is right.
