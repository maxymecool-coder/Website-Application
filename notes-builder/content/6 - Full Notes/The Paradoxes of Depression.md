# The Paradoxes of Depression
**Details**
In depression, there is a big "tell" of those who are suffering from it, it is the inconsistency in the individual's previous behavior and values. Self-preserving actions usually vanish and thwarted. "Social instincts," such as attraction to other people, love, and affection evaporate. The pleasure principle and the reality principle,... etc are turned around. Even the strong emotions that they usually convey when a specific event happens disappear.

Initially, the reason of this seems to defy logic, without an explanation. Any normal wanting or feelings are completely flipped onto their head. To understand this, we can attempt to arrange the various phenomena into some kind of understandable sequence. There exists a sequence that writers have assigned primacy to: intense sadness, wishes to "hibernate," self-destructive wishes, or physiological disturbance.

In his seminal work, _Cognitive Therapy and the Emotional Disorders_, Aaron T. Beck challenges the foundational assumption that the painful emotion of sadness is the primary catalytic agent for the entire syndrome of depression. He questions whether a primary affective disorder can logically account for the complex cognitive and behavioral symptoms that characterize the condition. Through a compelling critique, Beck methodically deconstructs the idea that the unpleasant emotional state is a sufficient explanation for the debilitating changes a depressed individual undergoes.

Beck's argument hinges on a crucial distinction between the emotional state in depression and other forms of human suffering. He observes that states such as physical pain, nausea, or intense anxiety, while deeply unpleasant, seldom produce the symptoms typical of a major depressive episode. These other forms of suffering do not ordinarily lead to a profound renunciation of life's major goals, the erasure of affectionate feelings for loved ones, or a wish for death. On the contrary, Beck suggests that individuals enduring physical ailments often develop a heightened appreciation for the very aspects of life they find most meaningful. This contrast strongly implies that the negative emotion of depression is qualitatively different and that its downstream effects cannot be attributed to mere suffering.

Furthermore, Beck scrutinizes the nature of sadness itself, concluding that it lacks the specific properties required to generate the hallmark symptoms of depression. The pervasive self-castigation, significant distortions in thinking, and the profound loss of drive for formerly gratifying activities are not natural consequences of feeling sad. A simple state of sorrow does not inherently possess the power to warp one's entire perception of self and the future so destructively.

In extending his critique, Beck also addresses alternative biological theories, such as the notion that the passivity and withdrawal seen in depression are an atavistic "hibernation" response designed to conserve energy. He skillfully points out a fundamental contradiction in this hypothesis: if the ultimate goal were energy conservation, it makes little sense for the individual to be simultaneously driven to engage in relentless and mentally exhausting cycles of self-criticism and rumination.
