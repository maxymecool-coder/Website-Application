# Fundamental Attributional Error
**Details**
In our daily social lives, what do we do ? We get into a lot of situations, and define them by our subjective interpretations, not their objective self. Not only that, but we also have a bias to ourselves, thinking that our interpretation and our rules is the bible. And so when we see someone acting different, driving faster than us for example, they're "crazy", if they're slower then they're "stupid" so there's really no winning there. This is called FAE by social psychologists, a phenomenon that happens when we attribute anything to someone's traits rather than the situations that they're in, giving an unfair judgement of them as a whole, while rarely applying that same judgment to ourselves.

Same as the situation presented in the lecture, you who know the situation that happened already (giving the middle finger to the lecturer), wouldn't feel the anxiety that the new students know, which could be called an Empathy Gap.

Now let's take an example the [[Game Theory and the Prisoner's Dilemma]], name it "Wallstreet Game" and the "Community Game", keep everything else the same and distribute it randomly. Depending on the context, those who were in the "Wallstreet Game" cooperated much less (30%) compared to those in the latter (70%).

**Supporting Content**
When we try to explain someone's behavior, we often make a common mistake: we overestimate the influence of their personality or internal disposition and underestimate the power of the situation. This tendency is known as the **fundamental attribution error**, or the **correspondence bias**. We assume a person's actions directly correspond to who they are, even when there are clear external pressures influencing them.

A classic experiment powerfully illustrates this bias. Participants were asked to read an essay, either praising or condemning Fidel Castro, the former leader of Cuba. Crucially, the participants were told that the student author had been _assigned_ their position and had no choice in the matter.

Despite knowing this powerful situational constraint, when asked to guess the author's true attitude, most people still assumed that the student who wrote the pro-Castro essay was genuinely pro-Castro. They failed to fully account for the external influence (the assignment) and instead made an internal attribution (the author's personal belief).

This error appears in many contexts. For example, even though we know celebrities are paid large sums of money for endorsements, we still tend to believe they genuinely favor the products they promote. The fundamental attribution error shows how difficult it is for us to separate a person's actions from their character, leading us to misjudge the true cause of their behavior.
