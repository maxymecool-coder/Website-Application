# Turning to Myth
**Details**
Still in the 19th century, Nietzsche aligned himself with a counter-Enlightenment tradition that sought to rehabilitate "Myth" as the superior mode of understanding compared to the abstract "Didactic" or scientific history. This was particularly exemplified by Plato's "Republic" which featured mostly dialogue between people, which Nietzsche argued that led to nowhere and wasn't applicable to humanity's condition.

Nietzsche then turned to the Platonic function of Myth. In this context, a myth is a narrative that expresses a "concrete necessity." Think of a character like King Lear or Oedipus. When Oedipus kills his father, we cannot say it was logically necessary (in the way 2+2=4), nor can we say it was a random accident. Instead, we feel it was _characterologically appropriate_; given who Oedipus is, this is exactly the sort of thing that would happen to him. Myth captures this "appropriateness"—a form of necessity that is tied to individuals and types rather than abstract laws. _The Genealogy of Morality_ is best understood not as rigorous history or sociology, but as a Myth in this specific sense. It presents typological characters (The Priest, The Warrior, The Slave) whose actions unfold with a psychological and dramatic inevitability, serving as a template to understand human drives across different eras.
