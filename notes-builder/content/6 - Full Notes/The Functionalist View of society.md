# The Functionalist View of society
**Details**
As mentioned in [[The People Needed for Society to Work]], there exists a sociologist view that attempts to explain how society includes concepts, how it functions as an organism and how these concepts all contribute to the organism in some way, shape, or form.

The size of the reward for any given position is determined by two factors: its **functional importance** (how crucial it is to society) and the **scarcity of personnel** (how rare the talent and training for it are). A physician, for example, is highly rewarded because the role is vital and requires years of difficult training. These high rewards motivate capable individuals to make the necessary sacrifices.

However, this theory has significant flaws. First, it assumes rewards are based on value to _society_, but the market often rewards skills that serve the wealthy (like designing luxury watches) far more than those essential for public good (like ensuring water safety). This suggests rewards reflect market power, not just societal function.

Second, the theory wrongly assumes societies maximize competition. In reality, institutions like professional associations (e.g., for doctors or lawyers) and national borders actively _limit_ competition to protect the privileges of insiders.

Finally, the model ignores how existing inequality in class and race creates unequal opportunities, preventing many talented individuals from ever competing for top positions. Despite these flaws, the theory’s vision of a merit-based system remains a powerful and intuitive model for understanding our own experiences of striving for success.
