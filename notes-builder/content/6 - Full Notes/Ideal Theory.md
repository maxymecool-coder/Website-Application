# Ideal Theory
**Details**
To understand the urgent necessity of Immanuel Kant’s philosophy—and specifically his later "Refutation of Idealism"—one must first understand the profound epistemological crisis inherited from his predecessors. The intellectual landscape of the time was dominated by what philosopher Thomas Reid critically called the "Ideal Theory." This theory posited that humans have no direct contact with the objects of the external world; rather, we only ever have access to our own mental representations or "ideas" of those objects.

This representational wall began earnestly with John Locke, who divided the world into "primary qualities" (solidity, extension, figure, motion—things inherent to the object) and "secondary qualities" (color, sound, taste—powers in the object to produce sensations in us). However, Bishop George Berkeley brilliantly and devastatingly dissolved this distinction. Berkeley pointed out that all experience, whether of a primary or secondary quality, is mediated by the mind. He took this to its ultimate, radical conclusion: _esse est percipi_ (to be is to be perceived). Berkeley championed "Dogmatic Idealism," arguing that the very concept of a mind-independent material world is intellectually incoherent. For Berkeley, a mountain does not exist without a mind to perceive it (ultimately relying on the mind of God to sustain reality).

René Descartes, on the other hand, offered "Problematic Idealism." Through his method of radical doubt, Descartes realized that the sensations he experienced could simply be the product of a dream state or the machinations of an evil demon. Descartes could only establish his own existence as a "thinking thing" (_Cogito, ergo sum_), leaving the existence of an external, spatial world entirely doubtful.

Finally, David Hume delivered the fatal blow of total skepticism. Hume argued that reason and experience are utterly impoverished when it comes to proving an external world. We only ever experience a succession of fleeting perceptions. We cannot prove cause and effect, nor can we prove that our internal perceptions are caused by external objects, because we can never step outside our own minds to verify the connection. If Kant could not refute these three thinkers, philosophy would remain trapped inside the subjective prison of the mind, completely unable to explain the remarkable objective successes of human understanding (like, in a modern context, using math and physics to navigate to the moon and back).
