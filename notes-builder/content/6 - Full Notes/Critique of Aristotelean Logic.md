# Critique of Aristotelean Logic
**Details**
Kant pays Aristotle a backhanded compliment. Aristotelian logic, he says, constitutes the "formal" foundation of all reasoning — it is the "vestibule of the sciences." It enables us to form correct judgments and serves as a truth-preserving device: if your premises are true and your reasoning is valid, your conclusion is guaranteed to be true. But logic alone cannot generate new knowledge. It is a tool of consistency, not a tool of discovery.
This distinction matters enormously for Kant's larger project. When reason attempts to use logical forms not merely as a method of checking arguments but as a mode of discovering truths about reality — when it tries to prove the existence of God, the immortality of the soul, or the infinity of the universe through pure logic alone — the result is what Kant calls the "antinomies of pure reason." Logic spins out conclusions that appear valid but are actually illusions, because the logical machinery is operating without any grounding in possible experience. You end up "discovering" all sorts of things that are really just artifacts of the formal structure you're using.

The real substantive knowledge, Kant insists, is to be found "only in the sciences, properly so-called" — the objective, empirical sciences like physics and mathematics that combine formal reasoning with content drawn from intuition and observation. Logic tells you whether your argument is valid. Science tells you whether your premises correspond to anything real. The vestibule is necessary for entering the building, but you cannot live in the vestibule.
