# The unendingness of the Selfish Need
**Details**
In his seminal work, _Suicide_, Émile Durkheim posits a fundamental and troubling aspect of the human condition: that desire, when left to the individual's own devices, is a "bottomless abyss." Unlike basic biological instincts which have natural stopping points, our psychological craving for comfort, luxury, and well-being has no inherent limit. This inherent insatiability, Durkheim argues, becomes a source of profound and constant torment. By definition, a limitless desire can never be fulfilled, condemning the individual to a state of perpetual unhappiness and futility, as a sense of progress is impossible when one's goal is infinitely distant. Furthermore, any satisfactions achieved along the way offer no lasting peace; instead, they merely stimulate new and greater wants, creating a vicious cycle where, as Durkheim notes, "the more one has, the more one wants." This state of unending pursuit is also inherently fragile. When life presents an inevitable "insurmountable obstacle," the individual who lacks an external framework for accepting limits is left with only raw restlessness and pain.

Ultimately, Durkheim identifies this entire condition—the psychological suffering caused by the absence of external limits—as the foundation for **anomic suicide**. It is not a consequence of personal moral failing, but rather a social pathology that arises from the breakdown of collective moral regulation. Society, in Durkheim's view, is the only force capable of providing the necessary boundaries that make human happiness and stability possible.
