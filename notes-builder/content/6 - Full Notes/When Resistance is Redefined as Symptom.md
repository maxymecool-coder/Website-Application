# When Resistance is Redefined as Symptom
**Details**
A devastating distinction separates the mental hospital from nearly all other total institutions. While a prisoner’s defiance is understood as insubordination, a mental patient’s defiance is uniquely reinterpreted through the "medical model" as a symptom of their illness. This diagnostic reframing constitutes the ultimate institutional trap, a psychiatric Catch-22.

This co-optation of resistance is a brutally effective instrument of control. The very signs through which an inmate might normally assert their selfhood—insolence, withdrawal, or protest—are, in the mental hospital, "read as signs of their maker's proper affiliation." The patient's struggle against their assigned role becomes clinical proof that they belong in that role. A patient who insists they are not sick demonstrates a "lack of insight"; one who rebels is "acting out." The inmate is robbed of the common expressions of disaffection that allow a person to maintain distance from an organization.

The result is that "all adjustments are primary." The crucial distinction between primary adjustments (conforming behavior) and secondary adjustments (subversive behavior) collapses. Because acts of subversion are absorbed back into the diagnostic machinery, they no longer provide a space for the self to exist outside the official framework. The "movements of liberty" that allow a prisoner to feel like "his own man" are transformed into entries in a medical chart that confirm the patient's sickness. In this way, the mental hospital can be even more destructive to the self than a prison.

This critique illustrates what Michel Foucault termed "governmentality"—power that operates by shaping conduct. The medical model is a "discourse" that produces authoritative "truth" about the patient, redefining their reality. To be deemed "cured," the patient must learn to conduct themselves as a "good patient" by internalizing the institution's truth.

Goffman's analysis culminates in a poignant summary of the human condition: "Our status is backed by the solid buildings of the world, while our sense of personal identity often resides in the cracks." The self is not liberated, but it endures, hidden in the small, illicit spaces of non-compliance. The book uses the asylum's extreme environment to reveal a universal truth: identity is a fragile, constant negotiation between imposed social roles and the secret, subversive, and utterly vital self we nurture in the margins.
