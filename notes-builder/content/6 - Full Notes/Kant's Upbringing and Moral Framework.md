# Kant's Upbringing and Moral Framework
**Details**
Coming from an impoverished background and deeply resenting the rigid, oppressive dogmatism of his early education, Kant did not set out to create an eccentric, inaccessible philosophical system just for the sake of complexity. Rather, Transcendental Idealism functions as a rigorous defense of "reasonable faith." Kant basically designed this system in response to the rise of Newtonian physics to reconcile the knowledge of the physical world with the absolute necessity of human freedom. Crucially, Kant does not believe that philosophy’s job is to invent the moral law or teach ordinary people right from wrong. He explicitly argues that any person of sound, uncorrupted natural reason already intuitively understands the moral law. However, human innocence is fragile and easily deceived by the sophistry, cynical arguments, and temptations of high culture. Therefore, the purpose of moral philosophy is to strengthen our grip on the moral law by illuminating how it is fundamentally rooted in our own free nature.

Within this defense, Kant draws a vital architectural distinction between the Moral Law, the Categorical Imperative, and the Categorical Imperative Procedure. The Moral Law is an ultimate idea of reason that applies universally to all perfectly rational beings, including God or angels. However, God does not experience the moral law as a burden or a constraint because a divine being has no selfish inclinations to overcome. On the other hand, because we humans constantly experience this friction between what we want to do and what reason demands, the moral law comes to us in the form of a command or a duty—this is the Categorical Imperative. The Categorical Imperative is simply the Moral Law experienced by imperfect beings who are tempted to violate it. Finally, the Categorical Imperative Procedure is the deliberative mechanism by which fully rational human agents apply this absolute command to the messy, everyday circumstances of human life.
