# The Theory of Mind Case Study
**Details**
The "Theory of Mind" (ToM) provides the most well-developed experimental case for a non-peripheral, central cognitive module, yet it perfectly illustrates the difficulty in distinguishing the Massive Modularity Hypothesis (MMH) from the Library Model of Cognition (LMC) [cite: Samuels_1998.pdf].

The key evidence for a ToM module comes from dissociative studies. For instance, individuals with autism, even those with normal IQs, consistently fail standard false-belief tasks. Conversely, individuals with Williams syndrome, who often have low IQs and broad cognitive deficits, can pass these same tasks [cite: Samuels_1998.pdf].

An advocate for MMH offers a straightforward explanation: there is a ToM _module_ that is selectively impaired in autism but selectively spared in Williams syndrome.

However, an advocate for LMC can "mimic this explanatory strategy" [cite: Samuels_1998.pdf]. They can argue that what is impaired or spared is not a computational mechanism, but an innate, domain-specific _body of knowledge_ about mental states. On this view, autistic individuals lack access to this crucial information, while Williams subjects retain it, allowing their general-purpose reasoning mechanisms to solve the task.

The situation is further complicated by findings that Williams subjects, while passing simple false-belief tasks, may fail more complex "higher theory of mind tasks" [cite: Tager-Flusberg_1997, as cited in Samuels_1998.pdf]. This pattern of results can be explained in at least three ways:

1. **MMH Explanation:** The ToM module is partially, not fully, impaired.
    
2. **Mixed Explanation:** A basic ToM module is intact, but the impaired general-purpose processors fail on the more complex tasks.
    
3. **LMC Explanation:** The body of ToM knowledge is intact, but the impaired general-purpose processors lack the computational power to solve the more demanding tasks.
    

Since none of these explanations is obviously more parsimonious or less ad hoc than the others, the currently available data does not provide a reason to prefer a modular account over an LMC-style account [cite: Samuels_1998.pdf].
