# The False Consensus
**Details**
The False Consensus Effect describes the tendency for people to overestimate the extent to which others share their beliefs and preferences. In a study by Tom Gilovich, participants were asked whether they preferred music from the 1960s or the 1980s and then asked to estimate what percentage of their peers would share that preference. The results showed a clear projection bias: those who preferred 1960s music believed the majority of others would too, while those who preferred 1980s music believed the 80s were the popular choice. Essentially, individuals treated their own subjective preferences as a reflection of objective reality or the "reasonable person" standard, assuming that any rational person would naturally agree with them.

Gilovich’s research went a step further to identify _why_ this happens, tracing it back to subjective construal. When asked to list specific bands they associated with each decade, the participants revealed that they were defining the categories differently. A 1960s fan might be comparing the Beatles (a high-quality 60s representation) to Mötley Crüe (a lower-quality 80s representation), whereas an 80s fan might compare the Police to a lesser 60s band. The disagreement wasn't necessarily about the inherent quality of the decades, but rather that the two groups were mentally constructing different realities for the same terms. When a new group of subjects was shown the specific list of bands generated by a peer, the disagreement largely vanished; once the subjective construal was fixed and shared, people generally agreed on which music was better. This suggests that many social disagreements are actually failures to realize that we are talking about different things while using the same words.
