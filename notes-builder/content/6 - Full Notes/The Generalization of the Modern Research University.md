# The Generalization of the Modern Research University
**Details**
The holistic vision of the early Chicago school proved unsustainable. The pressures of professionalization and specialization—forces inherent in the research university's DNA—quickly eroded the integrated ideal. Within two decades, the university that sought to bring coherence to society began to merely mirror its fragmentation. It became, in the author's evocative phrase, a "multiversity cafeteria" or "Harper's bazaar." The institution ceased to be a community bound by a shared vision, devolving instead into a collection of individuals pursuing their own ends, held together only by the thin threads of bureaucratic administration. Instead of interpreting the world, it simply reflected its chaos.

The modern university is pulled in two opposing directions. On one hand, it is seen as the primary vehicle for socioeconomic advancement—the most tangible version of "the American dream." It promises to equip individuals with the technical and professional skills needed to succeed in a complex industrial or postindustrial economy. On the other hand, this focus on individual success leaves unanswered the older, more fundamental questions about character, citizenship, and the moral fabric of society. By prioritizing education as a means to an occupational end, the university implicitly sidelines its role in fostering a shared moral or civic vision, contributing to the very fragmentation it once sought to mend.
