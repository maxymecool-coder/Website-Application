# Knowledge vs. Mechanism as a Trivial Distinction
**Details**
A key critique of the Massive Modularity Hypothesis (MMH) is that arguments for innate cognitive structure (like the poverty of the stimulus argument) do not necessitate innate _mechanisms_ (modules), but could instead point to innate _bodies of knowledge_ operated on by general-purpose processors [cite: Samuels_1998.pdf]. However, from a fitness-functionalist perspective, this distinction is largely semantic and does not undermine the core of the evolutionary psychology project [cite: Frankenhuis_and_Ploeger_2007.pdf].

The central claim of evolutionary psychology is that the mind is not a "blank slate" but is richly and intricately structured by natural selection to solve adaptive problems. The "solvability arguments" are designed to argue against a truly domain-general architecture that "lacks any content, either in the form of domain-specific knowledge... or domain-specific procedures" [cite: Cosmides_and_Tooby_1994, as cited in Frankenhuis_and_Ploeger_2007.pdf]. Therefore, a model that posits innate, domain-specific knowledge (like the Library Model of Cognition) is, in this context, simply another form of adaptive cognitive specificity.

From the perspective of natural selection, the functional outcome is what matters. It makes no difference whether an organism avoids incest because of an innate piece of knowledge ("do not mate with those you were raised with") or a dedicated cognitive procedure that produces the same aversion. In both scenarios, the organism exhibits an adaptive behavior resulting from innate, domain-specific cognitive structure [cite: Frankenhuis_and_Ploeger_2007.pdf]. Thus, the retort is that the debate over "knowledge vs. mechanism" misses the larger point: both are forms of adaptive specialization, and the arguments for MMH are fundamentally arguments for this kind of specificity in the mind.
