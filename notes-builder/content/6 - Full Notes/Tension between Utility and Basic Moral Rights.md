# Tension between Utility and Basic Moral Rights
**Details**
Mill defines basic moral rights as having three distinct features: (1) they carry a special weight and urgency because they concern the absolute essentials of human well-being; (2) they have a peremptory character, meaning an individual has a justified claim to demand protection of these rights via state coercion or social pressure; and (3) they exist independently of whatever legal institutions happen to be currently established.

There are then two paths to take from this: justifying rights via a general teleological appeal to maximize utility, or, justifying rights by appealing to antecedent, absolute moral essentials (like freedom of speech). If Mill wants to be consistent, then these two paths cannot diverge, it must invariably result in general public rules that assign equal rights to everyone. The persistent critique against utilitarianism is that it could theoretically justify repressing a minority if it produced a massive aggregate pleasure for the majority (e.g., maintaining an oppressed underclass to support a "high culture"). Mill attempts to resolve this not by arguing that "each counts for one" (which he viewed as a mere measurement rule), but by fundamentally redefining utility. Which means he is including a need for individuality/autonomy in utilitarianism itself, thus disallowing the aforementioned situation from happening.
