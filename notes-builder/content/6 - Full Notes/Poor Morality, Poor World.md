# Poor Morality, Poor World
**Details**
_"'I have done that,' says my memory. 'I cannot have done that,' says my pride, and remains adamant. At last — memory yields." - Nietzsche, Beyond Good and Evil, §68_

Rarely, and I do mean it, rarely, do I ever quote or engage with Nietzsche at all due to my strong disagreement with his philosophical views. Yet, under these certain circumstances, I find that this quote strongly reflects how I feel about the world of which I will address how the world as it operates, and why that operation constitutes a moral failure that most people are structurally prevented from recognizing in themselves. This essay may be less academic and less rigorous than previous essays that I have written due to it not being a critique off of rationality but a genuine discontentment and indictment of how the world operates.

## **I. The Gap Everyone Admits**

For the first gap, and perhaps the most discussed gap in moral philosophy - the theory and practical gap. Ever since Aristotle, who named it _akrasia_ - weakness of will - and gave it formal philosophical treatment in _Nicomachean Ethics_. Ovid dramatized it centuries before systematic philosophy could categorize it: _"I see the better and approve it, but I follow the worse."_ Paul, writing to the Romans, rendered it with the particular anguish of a man who experienced it as spiritual rupture: _"I do not understand what I do. For what I want to do I do not do, but what I hate I do."_

The gap is clear, ancient, well-documented — and in Western moral consciousness, perhaps that is precisely the problem. Familiarity has made it comfortable. To name akrasia is to normalize it, to file it under 'human nature' and move on without self-examination. But in Eastern collectivist societies, the failure runs deeper - the gap is not named at all, not because people are less moral, but because the gap was never addressed in the first place. It feels like no one ever revises or tries to understand and see morality for its limits, only for the practicality that is applied in their own lives. Confucianism is the most prevalent philosophy here in Eastern Asia, and people here takes morality less scientifically and more socially. Specifically, they mistake morality for socially-preferred values. And furthermore, mistake the information that actually show those values. A mother who criticizes her son for not being virtuous, being irresponsible, having empathy merely for not having a flawless room fundamentally already is misunderstanding and misusing the very moral framework that she is trying to operate from. This effectively shows that people are entirely unaware of the gap between theoretical moral frameworks and what they themselves or the people around them constitutes. But I also feel like this is not merely _akrasia_, as the mechanism is not that the person - the mother - sees the weakness of her own reasoning, rather she is not aware of it at all.

## **II. The Gap No One Sees**

This is the gap that I want to address. The gap that perhaps has more effect on practical morality than any other gap. It exists between the existence of the first gap and the individual’s _awareness_ of it in themselves. It is the gap that you are failing to live according to your own values and your complete inability to see that this is happening. Why and how does this gap exists ? I would argue that it is alike heuristics, that we fundamentally have to allow this gap to exist to ensure functioning. After all, if one were to ever notice that they themselves commit so many immoral acts in their everyday lives, how would one feel about themselves ? The question is in of itself already disturbing, and I myself cannot imagine what the actual experience actually is like.

This is not _akrasia_. The akratic person may see, approve, and does the worse nonetheless, but they see. There exists a conscious thought in their mind that they are failing their own morality, no matter how small and buried it may be. But the mechanism I describe is more corrosive: it is the constant failure mentioned previously, yet the recognition is not present. Not because the information has been hidden in any way, but because the architecture of the self has demanded that it be discarded.

The quote mentioned at the beginning captures this mechanism. Memory _yields_. Not that it is overruled, not that it is suppressed by our conscious acts; but rather, because it restructures itself to accommodate whatever the mind demands. It is not dishonesty in any ordinary sense — the person never intended the lie. But some part of them decided to perform the trick before consciousness arrived to object.

Dostoevsky, through Father Zosima in _The Brothers Karamazov_, articulated this with a precision that I find almost unbearable:

_“Above all, don’t lie to yourself. The man who lies to himself and listens to his own lie comes to a point that he cannot distinguish the truth within him, or around him, and so loses all respect for himself and for others.”_

Zosima here is not merely describing something singular, he is talking about the existence of a process that terminates the capacity of one to detect the lie, and also terminates the ability to do so in every other human being.

## **III. The Machinery of Invisibility**

This passage will serve to identify and provide psychological evidence for the mechanism mentioned above, even if the moral implications of these mechanisms are rarely followed to their conclusions.

The first is self-serving bias. When I do well, it reveals who I am. When I do poorly, it reveals what I was up against. This example is all that one needs to know to understand the bias: that people attribute failings to situation and successes to character. And so when we apply this across a life, it produces someone who is, by definition, unable to ever recognize and incorporating the possibility of a deep moral failure to themselves.

The second is moral licensing (Thank you Dad for mentioning this mechanism in an offhanded conversation). Moral licensing allows the performance of smaller virtues to rationalize large vices. People donate to charity, use the right language, perform acts of conscience and care - these people believe that there is a “psychological credit” that they have accumulated and therefore can spend on moral failures that never reach the ledger. Worst of all, they believe that it is fair and balanced when in reality - they virtually commit more wrongs than rights.

But more corrosive than all is, perhaps, the mechanism of theatrical function. La Rochefoucauld again: _"Hypocrisy is the tribute vice pays to virtue."_ This quote practically alludes to the fact that even the _language of virtue_ itself has been captured by this mechanism. People who are fluent in moral discourse may already be precisely the ones using it as currency, rationalizing their failures whilst maintaining the most moral seriousness in comparison to their behaviors.

T.S. Eliot described the human cost of this machinery with devastating accuracy:

_“Half the harm that is done in this world is due to people who want to feel important. They don’t mean to do harm — but the harm does not interest them. Or they do not see it, or they justify it because they are absorbed in the endless struggle to think well of themselves.”_

This goes further than the quote mentioned in the previous section, as it is the project of maintaining self-regard, one that is so consuming that the harm that they cause becomes inherently _uninteresting_. The mind is now prioritizing the maintenance of its own moral self-image rather than the moral self-image itself.

## **IV. The World That Results**

So, one may wonder: What does a world look like when it is populated by people who are, in this specific way, morally blind?

It looks like this one.

It looks like a world where moral language exists in every social media post, in every conversation, yet the behavior itself is scarce. It looks like a world where people will argue passionately about justice in the theoretical and fail to apply it in the practical. Where cruelty is not performed by people who think of themselves as cruel individuals but rather people who have made it so that they never see their cruelty as cruelty. The world’s suffering is not maintained by villains who delight in suffering but by ordinary people who have organized their attention so that the harm they cause never arrives as harm at all.

Solzhenitsyn understood this:

_“If only it were all so simple! If only there were evil people somewhere insidiously committing evil deeds, and it were necessary only to separate them from the rest of us and destroy them. But the line dividing good and evil cuts through the heart of every human being. And who is willing to destroy a piece of his own heart?”_

And the answer that we have come to is: almost no one.

So I look at the world and I see people who will weep over injustice in a film and perpetuate it in their homes. People who will share an article about understanding and treat the waitress with contempt. People who will preach forgiveness from a position of power and practice vengeance from a position of insecurity. None of these people recognizes the inherent contradiction in their being, ever, because the machinery that requires so requires them to destroy their own pride.

People have access to moral knowledge. Every ethical tradition, framework in human history has told them substantially the same things: Be honest. Do not harm others needlessly. Treat people as you wish to be treated. It is not rooted in the knowledge, it is rooted in the application. It is rooted in the act of treating oneself with the same rigor and the same lack of mercy that one might direct at a murderer.

And that is the most damning feature of this moral blindness: it is asymmetric. The same person who cannot see their own failures can see that of others with clarity that the other person themselves do not see. The judgment that is never applied inward is applied outward with surgical precision. Yet this asymmetry is never asymmetry, it is objectivity, it is: Naive Realism - the belief that one sees the world as it is, in all of its objectivity; and that others are clearly biased.

## **V. The Impossibility of This Essay**
Of course, I am aware of the meta-problem that is residing in the grounds of my writing.

C.S. Lewis observed: _"If you think you are not conceited, it means you are very conceited indeed."_ The belief that one has escaped the trap is, in of itself, the very evidence of one being inside it. This applies to me. It applies to this essay. It applies to the very act of writing about moral blindness, which is itself a form of moral performance as a substitute for the moral practice that it is describing.

I cannot exempt myself from the indictment. Not because I do not want to, but because to do so would to instantiate the very mechanism I am describing. The only honest position is that I am almost certainly as blind in my own personal ways as how I have described other people and that this essay may not be evidence to the contrary, but rather evidence in its favor.

But that was never the point, the point was that the people who most need to read this essay are the ones who are structurally least capable of receiving it. What I have described in the previous passages will, with perfect efficiency, prevent the morally blind from realizing themselves in the description. These people will read and think of someone else. They will nod along and believe that this only confirms their morality. Perhaps they would even share it, which would function as another deposit in the moral licensing account.

Upton Sinclair wrote: _"It is difficult to get a man to understand something when his salary depends upon his not understanding it."_ We can use this quote for ourselves, substitute self-image for salary and you have the whole process. One’s self-image is not a luxury. It is, for most people, the legs, and if you pull the legs, you will collapse.

Which is why people don't pull it out. Which is why the gap persists. Which is why memory yields.
