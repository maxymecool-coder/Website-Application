# Price discrimination
**Details**
The single-price assumption is not only unrealistic but also inefficient. So what do monopolists do? They learn the needs of each potential customer and then price accordingly. And that actually posits quite an interesting welfare situation:

- **Consumer surplus is zero.** Nobody pays less than what the good is worth to them.
- **Deadweight loss is zero too.** With no single price, there's no poisoning effect: selling the 7th unit doesn't force a price cut on the first six. So the monopolist sells every unit worth more than it costs.
- **The whole surplus goes to the firm**: 96 in the example, the same total as perfect competition.

That's uncomfortable. By the course's welfare measure, total surplus, perfect price discrimination is exactly as good as perfect competition, because both give 96. Yet in one world consumers get 32 and in the other nothing. Hardly anyone is truly indifferent between those. This is the course's first hint that economics has to care about who gets the surplus, not just its size.

But of course they aren't mind readers, so how do they do this? Well, they just look for signs that correlate people into these groups.

- **Airlines** charge more for last-minute tickets. Someone booking late (a business meeting, a sick relative) has few options; someone booking four months ahead is clearly shopping around. The seat costs the same to fly either way.
- **Movie theaters** charge less for matinees. People free in the daytime, such as retirees, could easily come another time, so they're more price-sensitive. (A student suggested lower demand; Gruber's point is that it's elasticity that matters.)
- **McDonald's** charges little extra for a large drink over a small one (from an earlier lecture), because the upgrade faces more elastic demand than the small drink does.

The markup rule from the monopoly note says how far apart the prices should be. An illustration (not in the lecture): if an airline seat has a marginal cost of $100, business travelers have ε = −1.5 and leisure travelers ε = −4. Setting P = MC/(1 + 1/ε) for each group:

- Business: P = 100/(1 − 1/1.5) = $300.
- Leisure: P = 100/(1 − 1/4) ≈ $133.

Sometimes firms deliberately make a worse version of the same product to sort their customers:

- **Tesla** sold a cheaper Model S with less range, using the same battery limited by software. When a hurricane hit Florida in September 2017, Tesla unlocked the extra range so owners could evacuate, which revealed the trick.
- **IBM**'s cheaper home laser printer was the office printer with something added to slow it down.
- **Amazon**, Gruber says, tried pricing by IP address — charging more to orders from, say, Goldman Sachs than from a homeless shelter — and was stopped.

The logic is the same each time. One high price would lose the lower-income buyers. One low price would give away money on buyers willing to pay more. Two versions capture both groups.

Why were people outraged at Amazon? In the standard framework, discrimination like this raises efficiency: it sells units that wouldn't otherwise be sold and shrinks the deadweight loss C + E. People object because they don't value consumer surplus and producer surplus equally—another sign that fairness matters.
