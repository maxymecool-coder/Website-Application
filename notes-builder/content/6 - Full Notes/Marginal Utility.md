# Marginal Utility
**Details**
Truly taking in how most situations actually function, we can come to the assumption that additional units of good increase utility will have diminishing returns. We can then assume that yes it is true that a consumer would prefer more cookies to fewer cookies while also acknowledging that they will become less satisfying as we purchase more.

Gruber illustrates this by holding pizza slices constant and asking what happens to utility as the number of cookies rises. The first cookie adds a lot of utility. The second adds less than the first. The third adds less than the second, and so on. This pattern is not a universal law of human psychology, but it captures a very common structure of consumption. A starving person values the first portion of food intensely. A person who has already eaten several portions still might enjoy more, but the additional unit is much
less important. This is why diminishing marginal utility feels common-sensical: abundance reduces the urgency and value of the next unit.

Of course we have to also consider the implication of other factors like if it fits or not. If one has an abundant of pizzas and no cookies, adding another pizza may result in practically nothing more for their satisfaction at all. This all then relates back to opportunity costs.
