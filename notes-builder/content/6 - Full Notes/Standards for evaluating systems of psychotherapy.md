# Standards for evaluating systems of psychotherapy
**Details**
1. The system procedures should be well-defined and clearly described.
2. The general principles of treatment should also be the same so that different therapists dealing with the same problem among similar patients will have the same methods, relatively. Furthermore, the blueprint of therapy should be sufficiently clear and comprehensive so that the neophyte therapist is not forced to proceed like an automaton following the same formula for each patient.
3. There should be empirical evidence to support the validity of these principles. For example, if a therapy is based on "reliving" an alleged traumatic memory, there should be evidence that (a) such episode occurred; (b) it was traumatic; (c) it is currently contributing to the patient's problem; and (d) reliving it will enable the patient to solve it.
4. The efficacy of the treatment should also have empirical support, such as: (a) analog studies in which certain procedures identical to or analogous to those used in therapy are applied to experimental subjects under highly controlled conditions; (b) carefully investigated single cases in which quantitative measures are used to demonstrate improvement at various stages of the treatment and at follow-up; (c) well-designed therapeutic trials, including quantitative measures, control groups, ratings by independent judges, and long-term follow-ups. In view of the well-known phenomena of spontaneous remission and “cures” based on suggestion, we need more than the claims of the therapist or testimonials from his patients in order to judge the value of a therapy.
At the time CBT was created, only a few popular therapies met the requirements. Rogerian, was one of them, client-centered, therapy is reasonably specific in its processes but not a comprehensive model of psychopathology. Instead, it focused on the few theoretical postulates; the principles of therapy and the model that are readily testable.
