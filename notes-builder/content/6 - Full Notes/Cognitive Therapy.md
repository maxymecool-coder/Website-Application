# Cognitive Therapy
**Details**
The cognitive therapist assists the patient to apply the problem-solving techniques he has used throughout his life on himself. Basically they help patients to identify their faulty thinking and learn better ways to formulate these experiences. By viewing it as more of an everyday misunderstanding, it would start to make sense to the patient because it is in line with his previous experiences. 

Psychoanalysis was all about digging deep into the unconscious mind, looking for hidden drives and past conflicts. Behaviorism, on the other hand, wanted to be strictly scientific and objective, focusing only on observable behaviors and how they were learned through rewards and punishments. Both were influential, but Beck points out they both kind of ignored the active role of conscious thoughts, interpretations, and beliefs in people's emotional problems. They hit a wall, realizing there were "legitimate and important problems left unsolved" because they weren't paying attention to the "cognitive realm." 

Interestingly, both schools started shifting slightly towards this middle ground. Within psychoanalysis, Ego Psychology started getting more attention. Thinkers like Hartmann, Kris, and Rapaport began focusing more on how people adapt to reality and the functions of the conscious mind – essentially, cognitive processes. Meanwhile, behaviorists, who had always studied learning, started looking beyond simple physical actions (like pressing a lever) or memorizing nonsense. They got interested in how people learn concepts, words, and complex thought processes – things that are definitely cognitive. Beck calls this a "growing confluence," like streams merging together. 

This is where cognitive psychology and therapy really stepped in. It directly addressed those thinking processes that the other schools were starting to edge towards. It focused on how our interpretations, assumptions, and core beliefs shape our feelings and actions. Beck makes a key point: even therapists who still identified as psychoanalytic or behaviorist began increasingly using cognitive techniques with their patients. Behavior therapy, for example, started incorporating methods aimed at changing thought patterns, even if the overall therapy was still presented as behavior-based
