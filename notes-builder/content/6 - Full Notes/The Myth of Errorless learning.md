# The Myth of Errorless learning
**Details**
Beginning in the 1950s and 1960s, B. F. Skinner, one of the most influential behavioral psychologists, advocated the "errorless learning" methods in education. He believed that encountering these errors would impeded learning processes instead of helping them learn.

Of course these days we now understand that errors, and subsequently adequate feedback, can help someone learn better rather than not making any errors at all. Like how letting someone struggle with a problem before eventually giving them the answer after they cannot figure it out will help them remember the answers and principles better. Moreover, these people will understand that learning takes a lot of effort and are more prompt to follow through the struggles and difficulties.

In fact, in experiments, it has shown that those who understand that failing is a part of the way of learning usually do tests better, with less anxiety as they do not have to dedicate a part of their cognitive function to thinking about if they are right or wrong, rather, they can just focus on the test before them. Another example comes in the form of the works of Carol Dweck and Anders Ericsson, they showed that people who believed their abilities to be hardwired, unchangeable from birth, usually shun away from challenges at which they may not succeed because they are scared/ashamed by it. Those who understand that their intellectual abilities lie in their control and they can grow and are not hardwired, are more likely to charge into difficult tasks and persist at them. This is a necessary trait, as any area of expertise requires someone to spend thousands of hours in it and fail a lot.
