# The three stages of memories
**Details**
The dominant framework for understanding how memories are formed, first proposed by Atkinson and Shiffrin at Stanford in the 1960s, organizes memory into a sequence of three stages. This model posits that information from the outside world first enters a fleeting **sensory register**. Visual inputs might last for less than a second, while auditory inputs can linger for a few seconds, a phenomenon that explains how one can recall the last few words someone said even when not paying full attention.

For this transient sensory information to progress, it requires **attention**. With attention, the information is transferred to **short-term memory**, a temporary holding area with a limited capacity and duration. This stage can typically hold about seven pieces of information (plus or minus two) for up to 30 seconds. New incoming information can easily displace what's already there.

To create a lasting memory, the information must be transferred from short-term to **long-term memory** through processes like rehearsal and consolidation. This final stage is where we see actual physical changes in the brain's neural connections. Unlike short-term memory, long-term memory is believed to have a virtually limitless capacity and can endure for a lifetime, though it is subject to decay. Overseeing this entire process is a "central executive" function, which allows for the conscious manipulation of information, such as using mnemonic tricks to aid in consolidation.
