# Productivity
**Details**
The idea of productivity comes from a famous application of diminishing marginal product. In 1798 Thomas Malthus looked at farming, where the two inputs are people and land. Unlike machines, land is fixed even in the long run; there's only one Earth. So as population grows, each extra worker adds less food on the same land. Eventually, output per person falls below what it takes to stay alive.

Malthus predicted cycles. Population grows, food runs short, and people die. The smaller population can then feed itself again, and the cycle restarts. His own framing was that population grows geometrically while food grows only arithmetically. Gruber notes that Malthus didn't call it diminishing marginal product, but that's the logic.

A simple version of the math shows the squeeze. With land fixed at 1, let food be q = √L. Food per worker is √L/L = 1/√L, which falls as L grows. Double the workforce and total food rises only by a factor of √2 ≈ 1.41, so food per worker drops by about 29%.

Malthus was wrong. World population has grown roughly eightfold since he wrote, from about 1 billion to about 8 billion. And people are fatter than ever: Gruber says world food consumption per person has risen since 1950. Starvation still exists, but he cites Amartya Sen's observation that there has never been a famine in a democracy. Famines happen when governments fail to get food to people. They are political failures, not technological ones.

What Malthus missed was technology. Land is fixed, but how much each acre yields isn't: fertilizers, machines, disease-resistant seeds and better land management keep raising it. So the production function should be written

q = A × f(L, K)

What is A? A is technology, which is the factor that determines how much output you get from given inputs (a variable from the black box, so to speak).

In the example above, food per worker stays level when the workforce doubles only if A rises by about 41% (a factor of √2). That's the kind of gain farm technology delivered.

When news reports cite "productivity," they usually mean output per hour, which rises with both A and the number of machines per worker. A is the narrower part: the output growth that more inputs don't explain.

It isn't just farming:

- **Henry Ford's assembly line.** Before 1900, cars were built one at a time by a few craftsmen. Ford used interchangeable parts and a line where each worker did one task over and over. Gruber says it cut the cost of making a car roughly in half almost overnight: the same workers and machines made far more cars. The moving line Ford introduced in 1913 cut the time to build a Model T from over 12 hours to about an hour and a half.
- **The Tata Nano.** An Indian car priced around $2,500. It's lighter and smaller, it pushes the wheels out to the corners to make room inside, and it uses fewer parts so it's easier to repair.
- **Electric cars.** Gruber calls electrification one of the biggest innovations in cars in decades. Whatever you think of Elon Musk, he says, a Tesla is a pretty cool car.

Productivity is the main driver of living standards, meaning how much stuff each person gets. Three things set that:

- how hard we work (L)
- how many machines we have (K)
- how productive we are with them (A)

But of course the first two are costly, either financially or in terms of effort. What does that mean? That means A is suddenly magical, at least for the normal workers, as they are inheriting the work from innovations of other researchers and such.

Small differences in growth rates compound into big ones. At 2.5% a year, output per worker multiplies by 1.025^25 ≈ 1.85 over 25 years, nearly doubling. At 1% a year, it rises by a factor of only 1.01^25 ≈ 1.28. A handy rule is that something growing at g% a year doubles in about 70/g years: 28 years at 2.5%, and 70 years at 1%.

US productivity growth has gone through phases, by Gruber's figures:

- **World War II to 1973:** about 2.5% a year. Working just as hard with just as many machines, each person could consume 2.5% more every year.
- **1973 to the 1990s:** under 1% a year. More stuff now meant working harder or building more machines. The IT revolution started in the late 1980s, yet productivity didn't move. You could see IT everywhere except in the productivity numbers.
- **Mid-1990s to mid-2000s:** a burst back to about 2.3% a year, and economists thought IT had finally arrived.
- **After 2005:** back down to a little over 1% a year.

**Supporting Content**
Gruber draws four questions from this:

1. **Why did IT give only a brief burst?** Life is radically different from 25 years ago, yet the economy isn't much more productive. His half-joke is that maybe our innovation goes into watching TikTok rather than making stuff.
2. **Where does A come from?** It isn't actually free or magic. It comes from education, which makes people more creative and better at new ideas. It comes from firm research and development, which is mostly applied: turning ideas into products. And it comes from public research funding like the National Science Foundation and the National Institutes of Health, which pay for much of the basic research. So society can raise A on purpose, through more education and more R&D.
3. **How should we spend productivity?** Higher A can mean more output with the same hours, or the same output with fewer hours. Gruber jokes that the hardest thing about teaching at MIT is that in the real world, people don't like work.
    - Since World War II, the US has mostly taken its gains as more goods, and Europe as more leisure.
    - A new worker in Europe gets six weeks off, against two in the US.
    - Europeans work from about 25 to 60 and Americans from about 20 to 65, and Americans work more hours per week.This wasn't necessarily a conscious choice. Gruber says the young him would have picked stuff, and the older him might pick time off. The pandemic may have shaken things up: labor supply dropped, many people retired early, and people started valuing leisure, better jobs and working from home.
4. **Who gets A?** From World War II to 1973, every group's income grew about 2.5% a year, poorest and richest alike. Since the mid-1970s, nearly all the gains have gone to the richest, who own the capital, rather than to workers.
    - A typical wage buys little more than it did in 1980, because prices rose along with wages.
    - The top 1%'s share rose from 26% in 1989 to 35% today. (Gruber says "wealth" and then "income" in the same breath.)
    - In 2021, coming out of the pandemic, the top 1% of earners saw their incomes rise 9%, while everyone else's fell as inflation ate into wages.A more productive economy doesn't lift everyone automatically. Who benefits depends on choices by individuals and by government. Gruber returns to this as economic fairness near the end of the course.
