# The Contextual Divergence of Hobbes and Locke
**Details**
Political philosophy is rarely a continuous timeline of abstract thinkers trying to answer a single, universal question; rather, it is a desperate attempt to solve the immediate, bleeding crises of their specific historical moments. To truly understand the friction between Thomas Hobbes and John Locke, we have to look at the visceral, physical realities they were surviving. Hobbes was writing under the shadow of the English Civil War, deeply traumatized by the horrific violence of sectarian factions tearing the country apart. For Hobbes, the absolute worst-case scenario for humanity was the total collapse of society back into the state of nature, which he viewed as a perpetual, chaotic war of all against all. Because his primary goal was to prevent this violent collapse, he argued that all rational people sharing a basic self-interest in preserving their own lives and securing a "commodious living" should willingly submit to an absolute sovereign. In Hobbes's view, any effective sovereign—no matter how tyrannical—was a rational necessity because the alternative was violent death.

Locke, however, was in completely different political waters. Writing primarily during the Exclusion Crisis of 1679 to 1681, Locke was embedded in the radical Whig faction led by his patron, the Earl of Shaftesbury. They were actively, and often treasonously, conspiring to prevent Charles II's Catholic brother, James, from succeeding to the throne, fearing he would establish a French-backed Catholic absolute monarchy. Locke was not an armchair philosopher writing abstract theory; he was a political operative living in Shaftesbury’s home, deeply involved in dangerous resistance efforts that eventually forced him into exile. Because Locke was personally invested in armed resistance against a monarch who was overstepping his bounds, his entire philosophical project had to be reverse-engineered to ethically justify rebellion, protect a mixed constitution, and mathematically prove that royal absolutism was fundamentally illegitimate.
