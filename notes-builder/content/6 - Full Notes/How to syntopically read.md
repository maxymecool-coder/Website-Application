# How to syntopically read
**Details**
Step 1: Surveying the Literature

This first step focuses on identifying and selecting relevant texts. Unlike analytical reading, where you read one book thoroughly, syntopical reading requires you to scan many texts quickly to judge whether they are worth deeper study. This is not about reading for full comprehension yet—it’s about discovering which works address your chosen topic, and how. You may not rely solely on titles or summaries; you look for structure, central themes, and relevance to your inquiry. For example, if you’re studying “human nature,” you might briefly look into works from psychology, philosophy, and theology to find those that present strong, differing views.

Step 2: Formulating Questions

Once you’ve selected a set of texts, the next step is to develop specific questions that will guide your comparative analysis. These questions act like a lens—focusing your reading across multiple texts. Importantly, they are created by you, not provided by any one author. They arise from your purpose for reading and reflect what you want to learn or clarify. For example, rather than asking “What does this author say about freedom?” you ask, “What are the common assumptions about freedom in these works?” or “How do authors disagree on the limits of freedom?” This shifts your role from passive reader to active investigator.

Step 3: Identifying Terms and Concepts

This step requires you to define and standardize the key terms used across the texts. Different authors often use the same words differently, or different words to mean the same thing. If you fail to clarify these terms, your comparison will be inaccurate. For instance, if one author uses “liberty” to mean legal protection and another uses it to mean personal autonomy, treating those as equivalent would distort their arguments. Your task is to understand each usage and then create a neutral framework for comparison. This step ensures conceptual clarity and avoids misunderstanding rooted in language.

Step 4: Comparative Analysis

Here, you look at how each author answers your questions and how they relate to one another. This means actively comparing arguments, tracing patterns, identifying disagreements, and understanding the foundations of those differences. You’re not merely noting who said what—you are asking why they said it that way, what assumptions underlie their views, and how their reasoning holds up in light of others. This process creates a map of the intellectual landscape around your topic.

Step 5: Synthesizing Insights

The final step is to bring all of this work together into a coherent synthesis. You’re not choosing sides, nor simply summarizing. Instead, you build your own perspective that draws from all the material you’ve read, showing how ideas interact and how a new understanding emerges. The result is an original insight, based not on isolated reading, but on broad and critical engagement.

Syntopical reading teaches us to think with—not just about—books. It is the foundation for deep learning, scholarly research, and intellectual independence.
