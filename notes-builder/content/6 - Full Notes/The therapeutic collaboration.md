# The therapeutic collaboration
**Details**
There are certain details that are incredibly important in the context of psychotherapy, in which we will discuss in the following essay. One obvious component is genuine collaboration between the therapist and patient. The two moving blindly in different directions, which happens more often than not, can make both participants extremely annoyed. It may be noted that the perspective of the therapist and the patient may envision the relationship between them completely differently. And so to minimize such hazards, both must reach a consensus regarding what the process of therapy will be. This agreement can be extremely important to a successful outcome, if the patient expectations corresponds to the therapy he receives, it will enhance the effectiveness (Orne and Wender, 1968).

Furthermore, the therapist must be in-tuned with the patient, he must understand the patient's problems and what they prefer to talk about, an "agenda" of topics so to say; and if the therapist disregards this, he might develop an unnecessary strain on the relationship. For instance, a patient who is disturbed by a recent altercation with his wife may be alienated by the therapist's rigid adherence to a predetermined format such as desensitizing him to his subway phobia.

**Supporting Content**
Some more benefits to this approach may be the fact that the patient experiences less shame, a sense of inferiority, and defensiveness if the two approach it as working against the problem of the patient, not correcting the patient's bad habits. By placing this emphasis on solving problems, it helps the patient detach more, leading to all the above. And it also helps the therapist to obtain feedback about the efficacy of his techniques and detailed information about the patient's thoughts and feelings. Lastly, the patient will also increase their ability to empathize and be more emotionally mature in this approach as proven by Carl Rogers own theory, as he is less likely to view the therapist like some God.
