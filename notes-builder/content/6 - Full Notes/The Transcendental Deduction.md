# The Transcendental Deduction
**Details**
After having established how the mind synthesizes data into unified experiences, Kant is not left with a massive burden of proof: how can we be sure that the concepts we impose on reality are actually valid and objective, rather than arbitrary fictions? This is the task of the _Transcendental Deduction of the Categories_. In 18th-century German jurisprudence, a "deduction" was a formal legal brief meant to establish the legal right, warrant, or legitimacy of a claim. Kant must provide epistemic justification rather than a simple logical deduction for his arguments.

After establishing the basic principles of metaphysics to outline the minimum, finite number of pure conceptual categories required for objective knowledge to exist, Kant gets to proving they are universally operative in the human cognition. He does this through differentiating between two concepts: "Judgments of Perception" and "Judgments of Experience."

Perception is strictly subjective and phenomenological in the sense that different people can experience it differently, and as such, it is merely a statement about one's internal state. A _Judgment of Experience_, however, transcends the individual. Because all rational humans share the same _a priori_ categorical framework, when we subsume sensory data under these pure categories, we arrive at conclusions that are valid for everyone. For example, recognizing that a person walking toward you is not physically growing in mass—despite their retinal projection getting larger—is a concept-driven judgment of experience that overrides raw perception. This allows for non-absolute subjectivism, which is the whole point of Kant's argument.
