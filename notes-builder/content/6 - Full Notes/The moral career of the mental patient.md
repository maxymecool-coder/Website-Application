# The moral career of the mental patient
**Details**
Erving Goffman uses the concept of the "moral career" to map the profound transformation an individual undergoes upon entering a total institution. This career is two-sided: it involves internal, subjective changes in an individual's beliefs about themselves, alongside external, public changes to their official status and way of life. The core process Goffman documents is how this public career systematically colonizes and rewrites the private one.

The journey begins long before admission in the "pre-patient phase," where the individual is caught in a "funnel of betrayal." This sequence of events strips them of autonomy and delivers them to the institution. It involves a cast of characters: a "complainant" who initiates action, "mediators" (police, doctors, social workers) who pass the person along, and, most critically, a "next-of-relation" (often a spouse or parent) who betrays the pre-patient's world by authorizing their removal. Each step is typically justified as being "for their own good," masking a series of humiliations.

This process does more than physically move a person; it reinterprets their past. The pre-patient’s biography undergoes a "reconstruction," where past eccentricities and struggles are reframed as pathological symptoms to justify their commitment. Upon admission, the "in-patient phase" begins with the stark realization of being "deserted by society." The institution becomes the sole mediator to the outside world, and the patient's own narrative of their life is discredited.

At its heart, the moral career is a process of narrative appropriation. An individual’s sense of self is tied to their life story. The institution’s primary act is to seize control of this narrative through case files and staff interpretations, becoming the official author of the patient's life. This reframes mental illness itself as a social status. As Goffman argues, what defines patients is not just their illness, "but from contingencies." Entry into the asylum depends on social accidents like class, family tolerance, and the authorities involved. The boundary between "sane" and "insane" is revealed to be a porous social line, and the asylum is where society sends those who have failed at the performance of social competence.
