# The Biology of the Brain of Transexuality
**Details**
A landmark 1980s study by Simon LeVay provided the first major biological correlate for sexual orientation. He found that a specific hypothalamic nucleus, the INAH-3, was on average twice as large in heterosexual men as in heterosexual women. In gay men, the INAH-3 was, on average, the same small size as in heterosexual women. This finding, which has been replicated, suggested a biological basis for orientation rather than it being a "lifestyle choice." Other, more subtle biological markers have also been found, such as differences in the ratio of the second to fourth finger length and in the frequency of the inner ear's otoacoustic reflex. These markers are believed to be indicators of the prenatal hormone environment.  
  
Neurobiological research suggests that transsexuality is a condition where a person's brain structure aligns with their gender identity rather than their anatomical sex at birth. A key study focused on the bed nucleus of the stria terminalis (BSTc), a brain region that is reliably larger in men than in women. The study found that male-to-female transsexuals had a small, female-sized BSTc, while female-to-male transsexuals had a large, male-sized BSTc. Crucially, this was true regardless of whether they had undergone hormone therapy, indicating it was not a result of adult hormone levels. This reframes transsexuality not as a psychological issue, but as a biological one where the brain's sex and the body's sex are mismatched.
