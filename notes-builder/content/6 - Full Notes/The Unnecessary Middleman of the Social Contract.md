# The Unnecessary Middleman of the Social Contract
**Details**
Hume now turns his argument to the purely philosophical, positing that this contract is unnecessary to justify political obligation. To do this, he separates human duties into wo categories: natural duties, which are spontaneous biological sentiments like the love of children or gratitude to benefactors, and artificial duties, which require a rational understanding of society's general necessities. For Hume, both the duty to keep a promise (fidelity) and the duty to obey the government (allegiance) are artificial duties. Though from here he argues quite poorly.

He argues that we follow the king/sovereign because they ensure our survival and what not, so basically a Utilitarianism stance - one dependent on the utility of the sovereign depending on what they could do and not. This completely misses the point though. Hume attacks the social contract as if it were simply a historical claim about how governments start, completely ignoring the fact that Locke uses the contract as a strict, logical _constraint_ on the form a government can legally take. For Locke, the real question is not whether a peasant actually signed a piece of paper; the question is whether the existing regime is structured in a way that free, equal, rational people _could have_ contracted into it without violating their natural rights. Hume's stance allows for an absolute, unchecked dictatorship could technically be justified if it happened to be highly efficient at managing the economy and maximizing overall societal pleasure or stability. This would be impossible under [[The Original Position]] because no rational person would want to advocate for this result.
