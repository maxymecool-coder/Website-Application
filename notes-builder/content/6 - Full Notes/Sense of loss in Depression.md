# Sense of loss in Depression
**Details**
The first thing when encountering something like this is to consider the requirements and criteria of it first, try to read it carefully and such. This can also vary from subject to subject and field to field, and so the information and criteria may be different. In exploring the theme of loss, the psychological disorders revolves around a cognitive problem. This is in which the patient shows specific distortions, he has a negative view of his world, of himself, and a negative appraisal of the future which we call the cognitive triad.

These evaluations concern shrinkage of his domain, and lead to sadness. As said before, this is because it affects heavily on his domain of the self. When he considers his present position, he sees a barren world; he feels pressed to the wall by external demands that cheat him of his meager resources and keep him from attaining what he wants.

The term “loser” captures the flavor of the depressive’s appraisal of himself and his experience. He agonizes over the notion that he has experienced significant losses, such as his friends, his health, his prized possessions. He also regards himself as a “loser” in the colloquial sense: He is a misfit—an inferior and an inadequate being who is unable to meet his responsibilities and attain his goals. If he undertakes a project or seeks some gratification, he expects to be defeated or disappointed. He finds no respite during sleep. He has repetitive dreams in which he is a misfit, a failure. 

In considering the concept of loss, we should be sensitive to the crucial importance of meanings and connotations. What represents a painful loss for one person may be regarded as trivial by another. It is important to recognize that the depressed patient dwells on hypothetical losses and pseudo losses. When he thinks about a potential loss, he regards the possibility as though it were an accomplished fact.


**Supporting Content**
Granted that the perception of loss produces feelings of sadness, how does this sense of loss engender other symptoms of depression: pessimism, self-criticism, escape-avoidance-giving up, suicidal wishes, and physiological disorders? 

In order to answer this question, it would be useful to explore the chronology of depression, the onset and full development of symptoms. This sequence is most clearly demonstrated in cases of “reactive depression,” that is, depression in which there is a clear-cut precipitating factor. Other cases of depression, in which the onset is more insidious, show similar (although more subtle) patterns.
