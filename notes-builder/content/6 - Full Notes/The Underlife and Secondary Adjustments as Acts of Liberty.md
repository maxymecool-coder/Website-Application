# The Underlife and Secondary Adjustments as Acts of Liberty
**Details**
Despite the totalizing pressure of the institution, Erving Goffman identifies a vibrant and secret social system he calls the "underlife." This is a covert world of practices, relationships, and values that operates "in the cracks" of the official establishment, functioning as an underworld does to a city.

The building blocks of the underlife are "secondary adjustments." These are the "unauthorized means" or "unauthorized ends" that inmates habitually use to get around the organization's assumptions about them. Ranging from using a radiator to dry personal clothes to developing complex barter systems, these adjustments are how inmates reclaim a piece of the world for themselves.

These practices are more than simple rule-breaking; they are fundamental acts of self-preservation. By engaging in a secondary adjustment, an inmate obtains "important evidence that he is still his own man," asserting that some part of his being remains under his own control. These are small but vital "movements of liberty" that allow an inmate to survive without total submission ("conversion") or self-destructive rebellion ("intransigence").

This dynamic reveals that the self is a dialectical entity that emerges through resistance. As Goffman profoundly observes, "it is... against something that the self can emerge." Total commitment to any social unit implies a "kind of selflessness." Secondary adjustments are the "little ways in which we resist the pull," and it is this friction against the institutional machinery that generates a potent sense of personal identity. The underlife is the primary stage where the self is actively forged and defended.

This micro-sociological analysis complements macro-level theories of power, such as those of Michel Foucault. If the institution's official structure is a Foucauldian network of power, the underlife's myriad secondary adjustments are the points of resistance that are inherent to, and produced by, that very network. Goffman provides the ground-level, ethnographic texture for how these abstract power structures are lived, navigated, and subverted in everyday life.
