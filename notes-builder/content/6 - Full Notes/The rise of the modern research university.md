# Universities 18th Century
**Details**
One observes in the late nineteenth century a pivotal shift in the landscape of American higher education. The classical college, with its focus on character and civic virtue for an established elite, was rendered anachronistic by the ascendance of a new cultural paradigm: scientific knowledge. The modern research university—exemplified by new institutions like Johns Hopkins and transformed older ones like Harvard—emerged not merely as a new type of school, but as the veritable cathedral for this new faith in science and its potent alliance with industry. Its purpose was no longer to polish the gentry, but to produce the specialized expertise—the chemists, engineers, and electricians—demanded by a modernizing world. This institution was conceived as an engine of progress, fundamentally altering the perceived purpose of higher learning.

It is a profound irony that the research university, in its infancy, harbored a vision of democratic integration, not sterile specialization. The early University of Chicago under William Rainey Harper and John Dewey stands as the key exemplar of this lost ideal. Their project was to create an institution that served the public, broke down departmental silos, and fused academic inquiry with direct community engagement. Dewey's "Chicago school" saw philosophy, psychology, and education as an integrated whole, with figures like George Herbert Mead bridging disciplines and faculty deeply involved in civic affairs like the Hull House settlement movement. This was the university as a force for democratic coherence, open to all with merit and concerned with all subjects relevant to the modern world.
