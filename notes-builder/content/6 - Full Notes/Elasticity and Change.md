# Elasticity and Change
**Details**
Elasticity is the variable to determine how strongly quantity responds to price and the changes between the two. Its core driver is substitutability. Goods with many substitutes tend to have elastic demand because consumers can switch away easily. Goods with few substitutes, such as insulin, tend to have inelastic demand because consumers have fewer practical alternatives. But it is also psychological and institutional as well, as people will only change and substitute if they perceive it as a viable alternative. Institution-wise, it influences how policymakers make their decisions.

The other graph to consider is the Engel curve, where quantity changes depending on the income. Of course this is simple to understand as we already have the [[Consumer Choice]], perhaps it may practically mirror the concept meaning-wise. Nonetheless, it is more varied than price elasticity because it can be positive, negative, greater than one, or between zero and one. A normal good is one where consumption rises as income rises. A necessity is a normal good whose consumption rises slower than income, so it takes up a smaller budget share as people get richer. Rent or basic groceries fit into this pattern while luxury goods fit the normal good pattern.

Inferior goods on the other hand are things that are consumed less when people become richer. Instant noodles is the simplest example: people like it, but they generally only do it because of budget constraints. And so when they have money they will purchase more high quality ramen from restaurants and such. The Engel curve then helps us understand how demand changes when constraint relaxes, which is essential for understanding poverty, welfare, development, and consumer behavior.
