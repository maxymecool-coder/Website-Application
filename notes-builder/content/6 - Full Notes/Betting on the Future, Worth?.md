# Betting on the Future, Worth?
**Details**
The thing about human life is, there will always be some chance of recovery. Be it by medicine advances, or just miracle remissions. Death would close that chance permanently. So the argument can be that the option that preserves the chance to live must win.

Kagan's first move is to deny that anyone gets to opt out of gambling. Continuing is a bet too, made under the same ignorance. There is no cautious choice available and no non-gamble on the menu, so "don't gamble" cannot be the advice.

The second move is the two doors. Door one: overwhelmingly likely a week of torture, with a one-in-ten-thousand chance of a superb holiday instead. Door two: certain dreamless sleep for the week, which is neither good nor bad — call it zero. Someone insisting door one is the only rational choice, on the grounds that door two discards the chance of the holiday, is not tracking any decision rule we use anywhere.

A student objects that the analogy cheats, since a week of sleep ends and death does not. Kagan rebuilds it at full scale: fifty or sixty or seventy years of torture at the same odds against a permanent dreamless coma ending in death. He still takes door two to be a permissible choice. Note carefully what he claims and what he does not: not that door two is required, but that door one is not the uniquely rational option. He concedes real room for argument about where the balance tips — how bad the torture, how good the holiday, one percent versus five — and rests only on the denial that no probability, however small, could ever be outweighed.
