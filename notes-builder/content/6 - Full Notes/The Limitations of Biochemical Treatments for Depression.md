# Drugs in Therapy
**Details**
While depression is a biological disease, the available biochemical treatments, though powerful, are significantly limited in their efficacy and reach. This highlights the vast gap that still exists in successfully managing the illness on a global scale.

The primary treatments and their limitations include:

- **Pharmacology (Antidepressants):**
    
    - **Standard Drugs (SSRIs, etc.):** These medications, which target monoamine systems (serotonin, norepinephrine, dopamine), are miraculous when they work. However, Sapolsky emphasizes their shortcomings: roughly **one-third of patients are treatment-resistant** and do not respond to any conventional medication. Of those who do respond, about **half must stop due to intolerable side effects**.
        
    - **Newer Drugs (e.g., Ketamine):** While revolutionary for their rapid action on the glutamate system, these are still new and not a universal solution.
        
- **Electroconvulsive Therapy (ECT):**
    
    - For severe, treatment-resistant, and often hospitalized cases, ECT can be highly effective, metaphorically "breaking the back" of the depression.
        
    - However, it remains a last resort due to its history of misuse and the fact that its precise mechanism is still not fully understood.
        
- **Brain Stimulation (TMS, DBS):**
    
    - Techniques like Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation (TMS) and Deep Brain Stimulation (DBS) are exciting, cutting-edge approaches.
        
    - They are still in relatively early stages of use and require much more research to become widespread, reliable treatments.
        

The most significant limitation, however, is diagnostic. Sapolsky states that an estimated **80% of major depression cases are never diagnosed**. When combining this with treatment resistance and side effects, it becomes clear that only a small fraction of the people suffering from this disease are ever successfully treated with biochemical interventions. This underscores the desperate need for more effective treatments and, just as importantly, better systems for diagnosis and care.
