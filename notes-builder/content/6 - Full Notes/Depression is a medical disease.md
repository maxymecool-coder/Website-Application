# Depression is a medical disease
**Details**
Major depression is fundamentally a biological disease, not a failure of character or a lack of willpower. The suffering is rooted in quantifiable, physiological malfunction. To treat it as anything less is analogous to telling a person with diabetes to simply will their pancreas to function correctly. The biological basis can be understood through three interconnected domains.

First, depression involves profound **neurochemical dysregulation**. This goes far beyond a simple "serotonin deficiency." The entire system of neurotransmitters is disrupted. A deficit in **norepinephrine** contributes to psychomotor retardation—the overwhelming physical and mental exhaustion that makes basic tasks feel impossible. A failure in the **dopamine** system, which governs reward and motivation, leads to anhedonia, the core symptom where the capacity to feel or even anticipate pleasure is lost.

Second, the body's **stress response system is chronically activated**. In healthy individuals, the release of stress hormones like cortisol is a short-term survival mechanism. In depression, this system becomes stuck in the "on" position, leading to hypercortisolism. The brain's negative feedback loop, which normally shuts the response down, is broken. This constant exposure to stress hormones is neurotoxic, causing brain regions like the hippocampus (vital for memory and regulating stress) to atrophy.

Third, these processes result in **altered brain structure and function**. The prefrontal cortex, the seat of executive function and emotional regulation, often becomes underactive, impairing one's ability to break cycles of negative thought. Concurrently, the amygdala (the brain's fear center) can become overactive, intensifying feelings of anxiety and dread. The brain becomes physically locked in a self-reinforcing loop of negativity, where a sad thought is not just a feeling but a biological event that perpetuates the disease state.

Ultimately, psychological pain manifests as and causes tangible biological damage. This understanding is the foundation for effective treatment and the most powerful tool against social stigma.

**Supporting Content**
The thoughts of depressed patients usually centers on a significant loss. They perceive that they have lost something they consider essential to their happiness or tranquility; and so they anticipate negative outcomes from any important undertaking; as well as regarding themselves as void of the attributes required to accomplish their goals. This theme can be formulated in terms of the cognitive triad: a negative conception of the self, of life experiences and a nihilistic view of the future.

And so after that these senses can lead to typical emotions associated with depression: sadness, disappointment, and apathy. Furthermore the feeling of being trapped in unpleasant situations or insoluble problems increases, spontaneous constructive motivation dissipates. The patient, moreover, even feels compelled to escape via suicide.
