# Defense Mechanisms against acknowledgement
**Details**
According to psychoanalytic theory, the ego must mediate the conflicting demands of the id, the superego, and reality. To cope with the anxiety that arises from these conflicts, the ego employs various unconscious strategies known as **defense mechanisms**. These mechanisms protect the individual from unacceptable thoughts, desires, and impulses by distorting reality in some way.

**1. Repression** This is the foundational defense mechanism. **Repression** is the motivated forgetting of unacceptable thoughts, desires, and memories by banishing them to the unconscious. For example, a person might forget a traumatic event. While central to psychoanalytic theory, the evidence for repression is debated; emotionally traumatic events are often highly memorable, not forgotten.

**2. Denial** **Denial** is the refusal to believe information that provokes anxiety. While repression is about forgetting, denial is about asserting that the information is incorrect. For example, a person with a terminal illness might refuse to accept the diagnosis, or someone with a drinking problem might insist, "I'm not an alcoholic."

**3. Rationalization** When people use **rationalization**, they attempt to prove that their actions are rational and justifiable, thus worthy of approval. It's a way of creating a logical excuse for an unacceptable behavior. For example, a student who wants to skip studying for a movie might say, "More studying won’t do me any good anyway."

**4. Displacement** **Displacement** involves diverting a behavior or thought away from its natural target toward a less threatening one. This allows the person to express an impulse with less anxiety. For example, if you are angry with your boss, you might go home and yell at a family member instead.

**5. Regression** **Regression** is a return to a more immature level of functioning to avoid the anxiety of a current situation. By adopting a childish role, a person escapes responsibility. For example, after a new sibling is born, an older child might start crying or pouting more often.

**6. Projection** **Projection** involves attributing one's own unacceptable characteristics or impulses to other people. By seeing one's own faults in others, the faults may seem less threatening. For example, someone who is angry might accuse someone else of being angry.

**7. Reaction Formation** With **reaction formation**, a person goes to the opposite extreme to hide an unacceptable impulse or truth from themselves and others. For example, a man troubled by doubts about his religious faith might become an overly zealous missionary, or someone with strong aggressive tendencies might join a pacifist group.

**8. Sublimation** **Sublimation** is the channeling of unacceptable sexual or aggressive energies into socially acceptable, and even admirable, behaviors. According to Freud, this is a constructive way to express an impulse without consciously admitting its existence. For example, aggressive impulses might be sublimated into a career as a surgeon. Sublimation is considered the most mature and socially constructive defense mechanism.
