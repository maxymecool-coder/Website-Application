# Mortification of the Self
**Details**
The engine driving the inmate's transformation within a total institution is what Erving Goffman terms "mortification." This is not random cruelty but a systematic series of "abasements, degradations, humiliations and profanations of self" designed to methodically dismantle the inmate's pre-existing, civilian identity. This process is often a functional by-product of the need to manage large groups through impersonal routines.

The mortification begins with "role dispossession." The new inmate is stripped of the multiple roles that defined them—parent, worker, friend—and is left with the single, all-encompassing role of "inmate." This is physically enacted through the confiscation of their "identity kit": the personal possessions, clothing, and grooming tools used to maintain a specific personal front. Replacing these with standardized institutional issue inflicts a "personal defacement," erasing their unique identity.

A key technique is "contaminative exposure," a violation of the boundaries of the self. This includes informational contamination, where discreditable facts from an inmate's past are made public; physical contamination, where the body is profaned through public and intrusive procedures; and social contamination, where one is forced into unwanted intimacy and their degradation is witnessed by outsiders.

Furthermore, the institution employs "looping": an inmate's defensive reaction to a humiliation (e.g., anger) is itself interpreted by staff as another symptom of their pathology. This traps the inmate in a self-reinforcing cycle of control.

This entire architecture is a direct assault on the dramaturgical principles of everyday life. Mortification systematically deconstructs the resources—roles, props, and a private "backstage"—that an individual requires to project a coherent self. The process reveals that the self is an ecological phenomenon, requiring a specific environment of social and material supports to thrive. The asylum's grim experiment shows that the self rapidly collapses when that ecology is deliberately poisoned, revealing our own sense of identity to be far more fragile and socially dependent than we imagine.
