# Changing of Rules
**Details**
Humans have a set of rules that they follow to go by their daily lives, these rules sometimes may be framed in absolute terms, are unrealistic, or are used inappropriately and excessively, which will make them produce maladjustment. And so it will lead to mental illnesses. Solving this problem will require the therapist to help the patient adjust their own rules. Though some therapists do look over the fact that if a patient's incorrect assumptions or personal mythology are not related to their difficulties, it is not necessary to change them.

In recoding these rules, there are two main axes: danger vs safety and pain vs pleasure. The difficulties that most people encounter are in their assessments of risk and safety or in their conceptions of pain and gratification.

In order to minimize danger, people generally will apply rules to estimate the probabilities in degree of harm and the likelihood of them being able to deal with the threat. The ratio between these concepts can be labeled the risk. If a patient overestimates the risk, they are more likely to be anxious and be unable to function normally in their day to day life, if a patient underestimates the risk, they may be more prone to encountering accidents.

The complexities of interpersonal relations and the lack of a reliable index of another person’s intentions to cause hurt add a fuzziness to the rules used in interpersonal situations. Some people regard themselves as highly vulnerable in all interpersonal contacts and thus feel constantly on the razor’s edge. Conversely, those who are oblivious to signals from other people, of course, may regularly get into interpersonal difficulties.

Because most problems presented by patients seem to arise in the context of interpersonal relations, some of the common interpersonal attitudes will be considered first. Interpersonal dangers are epitomized by a rule such as the following: “It would be awful for someone to form a low opinion of me.” The other person might be a close friend, a parent, a peer, remote acquaintance, or a stranger. In clinical practice, we find that our patients are generally most afraid of being devalued by members of their peer group—their classmates, fellow workers, colleagues, or friends. Many patients, however, are even more afraid of the prospect of appearing ridiculous to strangers. They are apprehensive of the reactions of clerks in stores, waiters, taxi drivers, passengers on a bus, or passersby on a street. It is possible that the reactions from strangers are more threatening because they do not know what to expect. And most of the time, they cannot define why this is bad, only that it is bad.
