# Studying the brain in Psychology
**Details**
A newcomer to psychology might reasonably ask, "Why do psychologists study the brain? Isn't that the job of biologists?" The answer lies in the fundamental difference in perspective between the two fields. While both disciplines examine the same physical organ, they ask fundamentally different questions and seek different levels of explanation.

The biological perspective, championed by neurobiologists and neuroscientists, is primarily concerned with the brain as _hardware_. It seeks to understand the brain's structure and mechanics: the anatomy of its regions, the function of individual neurons, the chemical processes at the synapse, and the electrical signaling within neural circuits. The goal is to map the physical and chemical machinery itself.

The psychological perspective, in contrast, is concerned with how this hardware runs the _software_ of the mind. Psychologists study the brain to understand how its physical processes give rise to subjective experience and observable behavior—phenomena like thought, emotion, memory, consciousness, and personality. The psychologist's question is not just "How does the amygdala work?" but "How does activity in the amygdala relate to the feeling of fear and subsequent avoidance behavior?"

A useful analogy is studying a computer. A biologist or electrical engineer would analyze the motherboard, transistors, and flow of electricity to understand how the machine physically operates. A psychologist is like a software engineer who studies that same hardware to understand how it executes an operating system, runs programs, and creates a user experience. One cannot fully understand the software without knowing the capabilities of the hardware it runs on, and the purpose of the hardware is only fully realized through the software it enables. Thus, psychology studies the brain not merely as a biological organ, but as the substrate of the mind.
