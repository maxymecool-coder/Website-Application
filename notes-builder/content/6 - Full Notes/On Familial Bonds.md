# On Familial Bonds
**Details**
As sciences developed throughout history, I believe that the term “family” has become something worth questioning. Most of us laypeople recognize it as a societal default, something that is already accepted to be rather than thinking about the definition of it. The priori that most people go by is the biological implications of it, which is usually about the blood relatedness of one person to another. Furthermore, I also contend that people have also taken the functionality of a family for granted, depending on basic human morals or societal values rather than implicating their own.

While this approach may allow society to function steadily, it’s not something that is exactly satisfying for academics or just thinkers. The term “family” being purely biological becomes substantially more ambiguous as people mature as they take into account adoptive, foster families and so on. The definition no longer satisfies the generality and variability of people using the term, alongside its function as well. In the following essay, I will attempt to redefine the word in a way that allows it to be utilize across more situations, make it better fitting and more sensical as well.

When examining the literature on this problem, the models I stumbled upon were mostly top-down in their approaches, depending on a preexisting framework rather. I would personally argue that these models are unsatisfying, most of them. Instead, I was more intrigued by the idea of a “bottom-up” approach, a family defined by its members’ actions rather than any pre-conceptual frameworks. Though this was an interesting idea, it isn’t exactly equipped to deal with situations where it is pushed to its limits. A “bottom-up” approach to defining families may sound compelling at first, but for example, when a child is birthed into an abusive family when its parents do not want anything to do with it, the model collapses on itself, requiring a certain top-down framework for it to fall back upon. For example, even if the family is hostile, a developmental psychopathology approach detailing how the hostility would negatively impact the child’s overall life would entail the parents to act differently.

I believe that the hybrid model still falls short in the sense that it overcompensates and perhaps overrides the model. Combining with the sociological definition of a family, I instead moved to a certain top-down approach. This approach would fit the bottom-up framework of depending on the members’ experiences with each other whilst eliminating the dependence on the individuals. A family’s definition would still depend on the actions, experiences, and emotions that people have inside their times together, but these factors would have a certain threshold relative to the effectiveness or positivity of said factors. Unfortunately, this would require an additional definition of the positivity, but I believe it is much simpler than my former proposition.
