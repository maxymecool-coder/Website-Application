# Theoretical Models of PTSD
**Details**
According to Wessa and Flor (2007), aversive learning in PTSD is extremely resistant to extinction due to how it is supported by a network of second-order conditionings that maintain behavioral strength despite the disappearance of the unconditioned trauma/condition. Of course, most models also include the impaired responsivity of the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (VMPFC), a crucial mediator of that learning method and modulation of amygdalar responses.

Another theory being the bioinformational theory set forth by Foa and her colleagues sees emotions as information networks in the person's learning and not just including their response to it but its relationships to other possible responses and meanings as well. According to this mode, treatment of PTSD would then require elicitation of the fear experience as fully as possible and modification of the information to change the structure of it.

Thirdly, moving on to the dual representational theory, Brewin and Dalgleish hold that traumatic memories are stored in two separate forms: explicit, verbally accessible, and implicit, situationally accessible memories. These are store in different manners and different parts of the brain. PTSD is theorized then to occur when traumatic events produce incomplete records of neither the kinds of memories and those are incompatible with the view of the world of the patient. And so healing would be integrating the narrative of the event back into their structure and beliefs, as well as coming to terms with the nonverbal part of the experience by reexperiencing it.

Finally, coming to the heaviest hitter of them all, the Temporal Dynamics Model of Emotional Memory Processing. According to Diamond et al. (2007), PTSD is explained by temporally sequenced patterns of long-term potentiation (LTP) and long-term depression (LTD) in the hippocampus, amygdala, and prefrontal cortex (PFC) as part of a generalized response to stress. LTP is assumed as the process when short-term memory is transferred to long-term memory. It includes protein synthesis and physical changes in the structure of neurons at the synapses. LTD is the phenomenon that inhibit the creation of memories more generally and subsumes a more diffuse set of phenomena. The theory extends further that as LTP happens after trauma, a refactoring process which causes the memory to be basically marked into the hippocampus and not easily made gone.
