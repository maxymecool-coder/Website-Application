# Theory of Stigma
**Details**
Evolutionary psychology offers a compelling, albeit theoretical, explanation for the persistence of stigma across human history. This perspective suggests that early humans, living in small hunter-gatherer bands, developed cognitive "modules" to ensure survival by avoiding threats. These innate mechanisms included the avoidance of those who appeared contagious to prevent disease, the rejection of those who seemed exploitative to protect social resources, and the suspicion of those who looked tribally different to guard against conflict. In the modern context, people with untreated or chronic mental illness may inadvertently trigger these ancient alarms. Their erratic behavior or disheveled appearance can mimic signs of contagion or a lack of social reciprocity, causing others to instinctively distance themselves not out of malice, but out of a misfired evolutionary survival instinct.

Attribution theory attempts to explain stigma through a cognitive lens, positing that we stigmatize behaviors we believe are under a person's control while forgiving those that are not. The National Institute of Mental Health utilized this logic in campaigns promoting mental illness as a "brain disease," assuming that framing it as a biological inevitability would reduce blame. However, experimental studies revealed a paradoxical result: while a biological explanation does reduce blame, it increases the desire for social distance and punitive measures. When mental illness is viewed as genetic and permanent, the sufferer is perceived as fundamentally different—almost a different species—rather than a fellow human facing a difficult situation. This "essentialist" view suggests that the person is flawed at a molecular level, leading to deeper exclusion and the sentiment that they can never truly be reintegrated into society.
