# Analogies of Experience
**Details**
To counter David Hume's skeptical view of causality as a mere psychological habit formed by constant conjunction, Kant proposes the Analogies of Experience. These analogies are not mere observations, but rather the vital a priori rules that act as the "instruction manual" for the mind's spontaneous synthesis, transforming subjective sensory sequences into an objective, external reality. Without these rules, the mind could not distinguish between its own subjective sequential eye movements across a static house and the actual objective sequential movement of a boat floating down a river. The first analogy establishes the permanence of substance, arguing that we can only perceive localized changes against the permanent backdrop of an enduring reality, effectively providing the philosophical foundation for principles like the conservation of mass. The second analogy dictates that all alterations must follow a necessary, rather than coincidental, law of cause and effect, elevating causality from a mere associative habit to a structural requirement of conscious experience. Finally, the third analogy establishes thoroughgoing interaction, allowing us to perceive distinct objects as coexisting simultaneously in a shared spatial environment rather than merely experiencing them in a disjointed timeline. This mutual interaction forms the philosophical justification for Newtonian physics, asserting that objects sharing a space must inherently influence one another, thereby weaving raw intuition into the coherent, physical world.
