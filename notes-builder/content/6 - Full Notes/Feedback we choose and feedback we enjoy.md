# Feedback we choose and feedback we enjoy
**Details**
There are two competing theories that attempt to explain what kind of feedback people seek about themselves. The first is Self-Enhancement Theory, rooted in Freud and has been dominant for decades, which posits that people universally want positive feedback, and that they only care about the good stuff, not the truth stuff. Self-Verification Theory, developed by Bill Swann, says that people want feedback that may already confirm what they believe about themselves. Its not necessarily that you want the truth, but you want to know what's already known about yourself.

Swann and Pelham's studies revealed something that neither theory alone could explain. When subjects chose which attribute to hear about, they picked their best one, which would confirm both theory as the best usually correlates with people thinking positively of it. The distinction only came when people were asked about the negative trait. People in these situations wanted to hear negative feedback because that would correlate to what they already think about themselves, that they are not good at the task.

Nonetheless, the results of these studies confirm both theories, as people DO want feedback that makes them feel good, the negative feedback does make them feel awful, but they also want consistent feedback, as it is accurate and real. The word "want" is doing double duty. People _want_ (choose) consistency. People _want_ (enjoy) positivity. These are two different systems producing different outputs.

And so we come to the point of a shared reality, something that is perhaps the most important concept in a relationship. Accepting negative feedback that matches your self-view creates the feeling that someone genuinely understands you. Rejecting it to feel momentarily good costs you the sense that your relationships are built on real knowledge rather than comfortable fictions.
