# The History of Psychophysiological Disorders
**Details**
The 20th-century understanding of physical disorders with a psychological overlay has evolved dramatically, moving from personality-based psychoanalytic theories to a more integrated, evidence-based framework. This progression reflects a deepening comprehension of the intricate pathways connecting our psychological states to our physiological health.

The formal study of psychosomatic medicine gained traction in the 1930s-1940s, led by figures like **Franz Alexander** and **Flanders Dunbar**. Alexander's "specificity hypothesis" proposed that specific unconscious conflicts led to specific diseases (e.g., repressed anger causing hypertension). Dunbar, in contrast, focused on "personality profiles," suggesting certain temperaments were prone to specific ailments, a precursor to the "Type A" personality concept for heart disease. While groundbreaking for their time, these theories were criticized for their lack of empirical rigor and for creating an overly linear, almost blame-oriented, model of illness.

A pivotal paradigm shift occurred in 1977 with **George L. Engel's biopsychosocial model**. This model revolutionized medicine by rejecting simple mind-body dualism and instead proposing that health and illness are the products of a dynamic interplay between biological (genes, physiology), psychological (thoughts, emotions, stress), and social (culture, relationships, economics) factors. This holistic framework became the dominant paradigm, acknowledging the complex, multifactorial nature of health.

The current era is defined by **psychoneuroimmunology (PNI)**, the scientific field that maps the precise biological links between the psyche, the nervous system, and the immune system. PNI provides the mechanistic proof for the biopsychosocial model, demonstrating how psychological stress, via hormones like cortisol, can directly suppress immune function, thereby increasing vulnerability to illness. This evolution from broad psychoanalytic concepts to the molecular evidence of PNI marks a profound maturation in our understanding, leading to more effective, integrated treatments that address the whole person.
