# Individual Differences vs. Species Averages
**Details**
A primary source of confusion in genetics is failing to distinguish between two different levels of analysis. Behavioral genetics focuses on **individual differences**—it studies the _variance_ within a population to understand why people are different from one another. In contrast, much of psychology and biology focuses on **species-typical or normative behavior**—it studies the _average_ member of a species to understand what is typical. For example, an experiment might show that knocking out a gene in mice causes obesity. This tells us what _can_ happen and informs us about a biological pathway. However, if that gene doesn't naturally vary in the human population, it cannot explain why some people are more prone to obesity than others in the real world. Confusing these two perspectives leads to incorrect conclusions, such as assuming that a finding about a species average applies uniformly to every individual.

The paper highlights a critical distinction between the experimental method and the study of individual differences. Experiments manipulate variables to see what effect _can_ be produced (e.g., does intensive language exposure boost vocabulary?). This demonstrates possibility but doesn't explain the variation that naturally occurs in the real world. In contrast, behavioral genetics studies "what is"—the naturally occurring genetic and environmental factors that make people different. A successful experimental intervention does not invalidate heritability, just as high heritability does not imply that a trait is immune to intervention (immutability). These are different questions answered by different levels of analysis.
