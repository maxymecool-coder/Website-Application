# Mill's Utility
**Details**
The most philosophically important revision Mill makes occurs in Chapter Two. Classical Benthamite utilitarianism faces a fundamental difficulty: if we abstract entirely from the _kind_ of activity producing pleasure, the _object_ the activity aims at, and the _relations between persons_ involved, why should pleasures estimated solely by intensity and duration be the only thing that matters? Mill felt this acutely after his mental crisis of 1829–1830.

Mill addresses this through what can be called the decided preference criterion. He distinguishes higher pleasures — arising from intellectual, moral, and imaginative capacities — from lower pleasures arising from physical and bodily needs. Rather than attempting precise measurement, Mill argues that those acquainted with both categories, under conditions of free choice, without moral obligation pushing them either direction, demonstrate a decided and stable preference for a life giving central place to higher pleasures. This preference is strong enough that at some point individuals would not exchange any further exercise of higher pleasures for any quantity of additional lower pleasures.

The asymmetry between the two threshold points is important: the lower bound on lower pleasures reflects biological necessity — we cannot indefinitely defer food, sleep, shelter without undermining our capacity to engage in higher activities at all. The lower bound on higher pleasures reflects something different — to entirely abandon them violates one's sense of dignity. This appeal to dignity is significant and potentially non-utilitarian in character, a tension worth tracking throughout Mill's work.

The result is that instead of calculating aggregate pleasure minus pain, Mill ultimately asks us to choose between modes of existence — manners of life — rather than comparing discrete pleasurable states. This shifts the utilitarian calculus significantly toward something resembling a conception of human flourishing.
