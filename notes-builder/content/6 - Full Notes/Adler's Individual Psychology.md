# Adler's Individual Psychology
**Details**
Alfred Adler, another early colleague of Freud, also broke away due to a fundamental disagreement over the role of sexuality in personality. Adler proposed **Individual Psychology**, a theory that viewed each person as an indivisible whole, driven by a primary, conscious goal.

The central force in Adler's theory is the **striving for superiority**. This is not necessarily a desire to be better than others, but an innate drive to overcome challenges, improve oneself, and reach a state of personal excellence and completeness.

Adler believed this striving is shaped by our early experiences of perceived weakness or inadequacy. As children, we naturally compare ourselves to others and become aware of things we are not good at. This creates what he famously termed an **inferiority complex**. Our personality, according to Adler, is largely a product of how we choose to compensate for these feelings of inferiority.

- **Compensation:** We tend to focus on and develop the skills we are good at to accentuate our positives.
    
- **Justification:** We simultaneously de-emphasize or devalue the areas where we feel weak. For example, a person who is not athletic might decide that sports are "stupid anyway."
    

This continuous process of assessing our strengths and weaknesses, and striving to overcome our perceived deficits, shapes our values, interests, and behaviors, ultimately forming our unique personality.
