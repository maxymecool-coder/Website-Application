# Indifference curves and tradeoffs
**Details**
Indifference curves are the graphical maps of consumer preferences. They show combinations of two goods that give the consumer the same level of satisfaction, meaning they will he indifferent between all points on the same curve. In Gruber’s example, the two goods are slices of pizza and cookies. A consumer may be indifferent between two slices of pizza and one cookie, and one slice of pizza and two cookies, while preferring two slices of pizza and two cookies to both. The first two bundles lie on the same indifference curve, while the third lies on a higher indifference curve. This graphical device turns subjective rankings into something that can be analyzed with geometry.

These curves have four main properties: the higher are preferred; they slope downward because they need to have to give up to have some more; they cannot cross because crossing violate transitivity and non-satiation; and lastly, there can only be one curve across a number of bundles.

The job market example makes the concept more intuitive. A graduate student choosing between Princeton and Santa Cruz might care about two dimensions: academic quality and location or weather. Princeton has higher academic quality but worse weather; Santa Cruz has better weather but lower academic prestige. If the student is indifferent between them, both options lie on the same indifference curve. If the IMF in Washington, DC offers a middle combination of somewhat lower academic quality than Princeton but better weather, and the student prefers it, then the IMF lies on a higher indifference curve for that person. There is no objective sense in which the preference is “right” or “wrong.” It is just a representation of that person’s trade-off between two valued dimensions.

Of course this would then depend on the person’s preferences for their curves to be different.
