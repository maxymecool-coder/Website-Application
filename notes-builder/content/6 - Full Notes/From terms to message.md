# From terms to message
**Details**
In the book, a proposition is also a declaration, but of the author’s judgement about something. He affirms something he thinks is true, or denies which is not, and so it is a declaration of knowledge, not intentions. He may tell us intentions at the beginning in a preface, and the rest is throughout the book. 

This leads into the sixth and seventh rule, which I am including both because they are closely connected. As the author declares himself on his own knowledge, we usually trusts him or maybe even parrot his opinions, but we should not be satisfied by this. These propositions are merely personal opinions unless they are supported by scientifically reasons, or logical ones. And if we are also interested in both the author and the book, we should also ask why and how did he come to be persuaded by these ideas. 

And the seventh rule, therefore, deals with arguments of all sorts. Every sort of argument consists a number of statements in any way, and we can choose to argue that is it true; but sometimes only a probability can be defended. 

The presence of these arguments are by words that relate statements, such as: if this is so, then that; or since this,… and we must observe this, because there is no one to guide us completely through the book, it is an absent teacher. The parts of these arguments conclude a series of statements which provide the grounds on what is to be concluded. And therefore, a paragraph, is necessary to express an argument. It may not be stated first, but it is the source, and if it is valid, the conclusion will follow so. This does not mean that it is true, as the supporting anchors can be false.
