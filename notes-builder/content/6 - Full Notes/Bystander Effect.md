# Bystander Effect
**Details**
The Kitty Genovese catalyzed research on helping behavior, but the phenomenon is not an anomaly; similar cases recur across decades and cultures. Latane and Darley's experimental replication had subjects hear a confederate (undergraduate Dick Nisbett) suffer an epileptic seizure over headphones. With only one other person present, most subjects helped quickly. With five others present, helping rates dropped dramatically, and even those who helped took significantly longer. There were basically three gates to the bystander effect. The first was noticing: When alone, 70% noticed smoke entering a room within 5 seconds; with others present, detection slowed because social norms make us keep eyes down and earphones in around strangers. The second is interpretation as emergency: Alone, subjects interpreted smoke as potential fire; with passive confederates, subjects generated absurd alternative explanations ("steam from the ventilator") because others' calm demeanor defined the situation as safe—pluralistic ignorance in action. Last is the acceptance of personal responsibility: A subway study showed subjects corrected wrong directions only when the question had been directed specifically to them; when asked generally or to the confederate beside them, they stayed silent despite knowing the answer. Each gate requires passing the previous one, and the presence of others creates friction at every stage.
