# The Attitude Recall Error
**Details**
The brain does not just account for the change in attitude, it also convinces and reconstructs memories so that we would misremember and support that change in attitude. One of the most striking findings in dissonance research comes from Bem and McConnell's counter-attitudinal essay study, where attitude change was measured not minutes but a _week_ after the essay was written. Subjects who freely chose to write a pro-tuition-increase essay showed significant attitude shift toward favoring tuition increases a week later — demonstrating that dissonance effects persist beyond the laboratory session.

But the truly revealing measure came next. Subjects were asked to _remember_ what their attitude had been a week earlier, before writing the essay. Their recall errors were almost perfectly proportional to their attitude change. If someone's attitude shifted 10 points on a 30-point scale, they misremembered their original attitude by approximately 10 points — in the direction of their current attitude. They believed they had _always_ held the position they now held. Subjects who wrote no essay and showed no attitude change were accurate in recalling their earlier attitudes.

The implications that this study shows are quite uncomfortable, it shows that our rationalizations and such are quite unreliable and fakeable, by our own mind no less.
