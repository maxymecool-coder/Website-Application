# The cycle of suppression
**Details**
Children are born with a natural spark of creativity, imagination, and deep emotional curiosity. However, many parents — instead of nurturing this spark — feel discomfort, irritation, or even anger when their children express these qualities freely. This reaction is not primarily because the child is wrong or problematic. Rather, it reflects an _unhealed wound_ in the parent’s own history.

Many adults were once curious and imaginative children themselves. Yet at some point, through critical parenting, rigid schooling, social pressures, or emotional neglect, they were taught—often painfully—that their wonder, sensitivity, and playfulness were unwelcome. To survive emotionally, they internalized the belief that these vital parts of themselves were shameful, useless, or dangerous. They learned to suppress them.

When these adults become parents, they are confronted with their child's wide-eyed questioning and open emotional expression. But instead of responding with joy, they often experience an unconscious pain. The child’s aliveness mirrors the parts of themselves they were once forced to abandon. Without awareness, they feel threatened and react defensively—through anger, annoyance, withdrawal, or even ridicule. Not because they hate their child, but because seeing that freedom reminds them of what they lost and never had the chance to reclaim.

Thus, the cycle continues:

- The child’s creativity triggers the parent's buried grief.
    
- The parent, unable to tolerate that discomfort, suppresses the child's creativity.
    
- The child grows up, internalizes the suppression, and eventually repeats the same pattern.
    

This intergenerational chain can seem inescapable, but it is not. Recognition is the first step to breaking it. When a parent becomes aware of their emotional inheritance—realizing that their discomfort with their child's wonder is a reflection of their own denied self—they can choose a different path. They can grieve their losses, begin reclaiming their suppressed vitality, and crucially, nurture the child’s curiosity instead of punishing it.
