# Modernity as a Splitting of Factors
**Details**
A precise anthropological definition of modernity requires looking beyond surface-level indicators like advanced technology, industrialization, or urbanization, recognizing that a nation can possess modern technology without being fundamentally modern in its social structure. The core of true modernity lies in the radical separation of human society's four primary institutional spheres: economics, religion or ideology, politics, and kinship or society. In pre-modern tribal societies, these spheres are entirely unified, meaning the tribe simultaneously functions as the family, the economy, the religious body, and the government. As early civilizations developed, a partial separation occurred, typically leaving the economy bound to the family unit and politics tethered to religion. Western modernity, however, functions like a sociological nuclear fission. It violently splits all four of these spheres entirely apart from one another. In a truly modern framework, economic life is divorced from familial obligations, and political governance is strictly separated from religious authority. This total institutional separation is highly unnatural in the broader context of human history, yet it is the very engine that generates the immense energy, freedom, and structural openness characteristic of modern societies.
