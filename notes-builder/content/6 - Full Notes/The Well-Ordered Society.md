# The Well-Ordered Society
**Details**
This is John Rawls's idea of a society where it is not only designed to advance the good of its members but is effective at regulating a publicly recognized conception of political justice. To be frank, it is not necessarily a novel or difficult definition of an ideal society, it has two defining features: that every citizen accepts and knows that the other accepts the same principles that they do. And that the society's main political and social institutions actually satisfy, or are with good reason believed to satisfy, those principles.

The consequence is that a well-ordered society possesses a shared point of view from which citizens' competing claims on institutions can be adjudicated. Unlike the sixteenth- and seventeenth-century religious conflicts — where no common basis for discussion existed — citizens in a well-ordered society can argue about justice in a principled, non-futile way. Rawls is careful to note that no set of principles resolves every question; what they provide is a framework within which discussion is possible and answers are reachable enough of the time.

Of course, there has not actually been a society that satisfies all of the conditions that Rawls stated above, and perhaps none can ever be in a precise sense. Nonetheless, it is usable as a tool and a comparison against other societies to see if one has dumped every principles in the trash and therefore cannot achieve this ideal or how close one is to it.
