# Different kinds of attention
**Details**
As discussed before, writing is never a single act of “focusing.” It is a dance among several kinds of attention, each suited to a different step in the process. _Sustained_ attention lets us sit with a draft long enough to finish a thought, while _selective_ attention helps us tune out the buzz of email pings and stray ideas so we can polish a single sentence. When we jump from outlining to fact‑checking we rely on _alternating_ attention; when we keep an eye on reference material while shaping an argument we call on _divided_ attention; and over all of this hovers _executive_ attention, the internal stage‑manager that decides which spotlight to turn on next.​

Experts are able to slip between these modes without friction because they have practiced “variable attention.” Oshin Vartanian’s 2009 study on creative problem solving showed that high performers don’t stay broadly defocused or narrowly locked in; instead, they tighten or loosen their focus as the task morphs in front of them. In writing, that means knowing when to drill into the cadence of a paragraph and when to lean back to see if the story arc still holds. Such flexibility is an embodied skill, not a set of rules; it grows from countless cycles of noticing a mismatch between the attention being used and the attention the moment actually needs.​

Structures that expose us to those cycles—daily drafting sessions, slip‑box note taking, timed micro‑edits—become training grounds for the attentional repertoire. Finished pages are not the only reward; each conscious switch from one attentional gear to another thickens our intuition. Over time the writer’s inner stage‑manager learns to fade lights up or down without conscious orders, and what once felt like juggling becomes a single, fluid performance.
