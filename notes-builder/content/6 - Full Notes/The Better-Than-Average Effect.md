# The Better-Than-Average Effect
**Details**
Across virtually every measurable dimension, roughly 75%-80% of people rate themselves as above average. Of course this is statistically impossible and represents one of psychology's perhaps...easiest findings or replicable findings: positive illusions. Coined by Shelley Taylor, these are illusions of people that makes them believe in the extent of their abilities to be much farther than it actually is. She also argues that it carries mental and physical health benefits. The only group, that can reliably produce accurate self-assessments is clinically depressed individuals. This creates a disturbing implication: psychological health may depend partly on self-deception, and accuracy about oneself may be a symptom of dysfunction rather than clarity. The debate over whether positive illusions are genuinely adaptive or merely feel good remains unresolved.

To explain why this exists, we have to look into two of the strongest arguments: motivational - as it protects our self-esteem (from an evolutionary standpoint) or that it is cognitive - as biased outputs can emerge from unbiased processing applied to unequal information. Ross and Sicoly's household chores study illustrates the cognitive side: each partner claimed responsibility for 16 of 20 tasks, not from vanity but because each had fuller memory access to their own contributions than their partner's. The database itself is asymmetric (this proves the latter).

Dunning's work shows a deeper mechanism, the mechanism in which people emphasize and value traits that they themselves are expressive of. So a loyal person would value loyalty in friends and tell that to people who ask them questions relative to...friends, and so on. And so their comparisons will also shift to serve their selves.
