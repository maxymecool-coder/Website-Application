# Reflective Equilibrium
**Details**
This is Rawls’s method and ideal endpoint of justification. It begins from the assumption that every citizen carries a capacity for a sense of justice. Evidence of this developed capacity is that adults routinely make justice judgments across an enormous range, "extending from basic institutions... to the law of nations to all the way down to particular standards, principles, practices, the actions of individuals and their characters."

Rawls then select it in two stages. First, we isolate political judgements of justice concerning the basic structure. Then we identify considered judgements which are ones made depending on adequate information, impartiality, freedom from threat… these occur at all levels of generality, from particular assessments of individual actions to abstract constitutional principles.

Among these, some serve as "fixed points" — convictions so firm they are effectively non-negotiable. Rawls's examples include the wrongness of slavery, religious persecution, and inflicting unnecessary suffering. Other judgments — such as views about income distribution — are far less settled, involving "a variety of criteria" applied inconsistently. Rawls observes that internal inconsistency among one's considered judgments is the normal human condition: "if you're not internally inconsistent, you're either probably unreflective or insensitive or maybe mad" — meaning in the grip of a doctrine that enforces artificial consistency "at the cost of ordinary human feeling."

None of these principles are exempt from being scrutinized, any and all of them can realistically be seen and viewed so unlike other frameworks that has a set of first principles.
