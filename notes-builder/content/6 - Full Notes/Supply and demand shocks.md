# Supply and demand shocks
**Details**
For the most part, we'’'ve been discussing about positive economics (if you don’t remember its how things are). And for now we’d have to show how the market may be and the interactions between consumers and producers.

Gruber's example is the gasoline market (Figure 9-1), with gallons on the x-axis and dollars per gallon on the y-axis:

- **Demand** is consumers' marginal willingness to pay: each point is what someone would pay for the next gallon. It slopes down because at higher prices, fewer people find the next gallon worth it.
- **Supply** is producers' marginal willingness to supply, which is their marginal cost. It slopes up because higher prices make more output worth producing.
- **Equilibrium** (e1: P1, Q1) is where the two cross. At P1, consumers want Q1 gallons and producers want to sell Q1.

**A demand shock** (Figure 9-2). Suppose tastes shift toward big cars like SUVs and minivans, which feel safer and handle bad weather better. Big cars and gas are _complements_, since big cars burn more gas. So demand for gas shifts out, from D1 to D2.

- If the price stayed at P1, consumers would want Q1′ while producers still supplied Q1. The gap is _excess demand_.
- Sellers respond by raising the price. As it rises, consumers cut back a little along the new demand curve, and producers expand along the supply curve.
- The market slides up the supply curve to e2, with a higher price and a higher quantity.

**A supply shock** (Figure 9-3). Now suppose a war in the Middle East raises the cost of producing gas: shipping gets more expensive, or supply has to come from higher-cost sources like the US. Supply is marginal cost, so the supply curve shifts up and in; at any price, producers offer less.

- At P1, producers now offer only Q1′ while consumers still want Q1. That's excess demand again, this time caused by costs rather than tastes.
- The price rises, and the market slides up the demand curve to e2: a higher price but a lower quantity.

Gruber catches himself calling the new equilibrium "happy." Equilibrium only means both sides are on their curves at that price; it says nothing about whether anyone is better or worse off. Consumers are clearly worse off in the second situation due to having to pay more and we’d then have to see how that is measured.

We were just demonstrated basically situations where the same thing happened yet for different reasons. So what do we learn from that? We need to see both demand and prices to make a conclusion about the market.
