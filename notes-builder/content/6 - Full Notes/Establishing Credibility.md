# Establishing Credibility
**Details**
To resolve the problem of trust, the therapy must establish some common ground, find some point of agreement, and then extend the area of consensus from there. They may start with something like empathizing with the patient, not necessarily agreeing or disagreeing, but encouraging the patient to express his distorted ideas to understand and really hear the patient. After that, he may send some signs, or trials to determine if the patient is ready to examine the distortions in his thoughts.

These delusions, though thought to be impermeable by psychotherapy, can still eventually become amenable to modification if the therapist is sensitive and patient (Beck, 1952; Davidson, 1966; Salzman, 1960; Schwartz, 1963). Studies have even shown that dogmatism tends to widen the gap between persons with different opinions, making them more extreme in their views of the subject to simply distance themselves from the other. The therapist must then be vigilant to any clues that indicates the patient's disagreement, to not be deceived by their inexpressiveness as agreement.

Many patients appear to agree with the therapist due to their fears of challenging the person of authority and trying to please them instead. And so when in therapy, one must have an admixture of their own humility to trial-and-error and lead the patient to find the best approaches or formulations for his own illness.

Delusional thinking is a way that the confidence between the two is tested. It is generally wise for us not to attack it directly, but rather help the patient cope with it. For example, an elderly man believes his wife is cheating on him, his wife is subsequently annoyed and may really leave him. This is when the therapist steps in to make the man realize that if he continues to berate his wife, he might not have someone to take care of him anymore, which makes him stop.
