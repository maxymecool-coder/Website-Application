# Depressed people are wiser...
**Details**
Depressive realism is the paradoxical phenomenon where individuals with depression, despite their cognitive distortions, sometimes have a more accurate perception of reality than non-depressed individuals. Sapolsky frames this with the phrase "sadder but wiser," highlighting a key nuance in how depression affects cognition.

The paradox is resolved by understanding the _context_ of the judgment being made:

1. **When evaluating personal, emotionally charged events,** a negative cognitive distortion dominates. A person with depression will overgeneralize a single failure into a narrative of lifelong incompetence. They will interpret neutral social cues as negative and see their future as inevitably bleak. In this personal realm, their thinking is distorted.
    
2. **When evaluating external, emotionally detached situations,** they often lose the "optimism bias" that characterizes most healthy individuals. The average person tends to be mildly delusional in their optimism, underestimating risks and overestimating positive outcomes. People with depression are often stripped of this bias. As a result, they can be more accurate in assessing risks, probabilities, and the state of the world at large.
    

This doesn't mean depression is a "better" way of seeing the world. The loss of a healthy optimism bias comes at the cost of hope and well-being. However, it demonstrates that the cognitive effects of depression are complex. The illness doesn't simply make a person think negatively; it fundamentally alters their relationship with reality, removing a protective layer of optimism that, while not strictly "accurate," is crucial for psychological health.
