# The Myth of Moral Coherence
**Details**
Nietzsche also challenged the 19th century notion that society's definition of morality is a single, seamless, and logical whole. Philosophers and historians often speak of "The Elizabethan Worldview" or "Christian Morality" as if these were monolithic structures where every intuition fits neatly with every other. Nietzsche argue that this presumption is false, suggesting that even the Christian Morality is a combination of antagonistic and disparate historical remnants.

To exemplify this, we can take a look at the 19th century as a conglomerate of chaotic Christian values, feudal aristocratic remnants, and modern utilitarian calculus. These values obviously do not fit together-feudal honor views the Roman concept of "glory" as supreme, while Christianity views it as sinful pride. Yet, they coexist in a state of historical tension.

Nietzsche illustrated this in his book Beyond Good and Evil with a striking metaphor. He posited that philosophers viewed Morality as a star that we rotate around, but in fact, it should be multiple stars that we are moving around without a certain direction or orbits. And so Morality at any time could be different due to the shade or point of view that we are standing from.

Furthermore, Nietzsche would also define the concept of "Good" as being univocal, multi-meaning, extending from his conceptualization as there is not a single unified worldview. For example, when viewed nobly, the word "good" is a self-description coined by the masters to refer to themselves. It implies vitality, power, wealth, and happiness. Its opposite, "Bad," is an afterthought—a dismissive word for the "losers," the commoners, or the weak. It carries no moral condemnation, only a recognition of low status. For the slave, "Evil" is the primary term. It is a projection of intense hatred toward the masters. The slave looks at the master’s power and violence and labels it "Evil." "Good" is then created as a back-formation: "I am good _because_ I am not like that evil master."
