# On Being A Father
**Details**
_“To beget is not to be a father, a father is he who begets and proves himself worthy of it.” — Fetyukovich, The Brothers Karamazov_

During the reading of Brothers Karamazov, this was one of the most interesting points that I encountered perhaps because of how it was used. Fetyukovich wields this idea not as an admission of guilt — he maintains his client's innocence throughout — but as a philosophical argument to mentally reclassify the alleged crime from a horrific parricide to a mere homicide. This argument echoes a fundamental question about the definition of "family": can we accept a definition of parenthood based purely on biology when the functional, visceral reality of that bond is entirely absent? In other words, if a parent abandons their child from birth and practically refuses to acknowledge their existence, can and should they still be considered a "father" — not just in the court of law, but in any morally serious sense of the word?

# I. The Societal Meaning of A Father

But to contextualize this and justify its weight in Fetyukovich's closing argument, we must understand what fatherhood _meant_ in 19th century Russia. Russia was a deeply religious country at the time, and the father was treated as an institution - God ruled the universe, the Tsar ruled the empire, and the father ruled the family. As they were all linked together, parricide was essentially viewed as an unnatural abomination - a spiritual treason, so to say.

And the prosecutor Ippolit Kirillovich, by all means, epitomizes what this version of Russia represents. His troika metaphor — Russia galloping out of control, Europe stepping aside in horror — frames Mitya's alleged crime not as an individual act but as a symptom of national moral collapse. Fetyukovich rejects this view, he puts the case of Mitya’s supposed murder of his father as evidence to question the opinion on parricides at the time and in of itself - the very structure of Russia at the time: _“The prosecutor was pleased to terrify us with the Russian troika, at the galloping flight of which ‘all the nations stand aside in disgust and horror.’ Well, let them stand aside, gentlemen of the jury... but I submit to you that a chariot is not a troika. To drive a chariot, one needs a firm foundation, a reliable road.”_

Dostoevsky is careful to undercut Fetyukovich here, as if reminding us that this is a rhetorician doing what rhetoricians do, working the jury's emotions toward acquittal. But that is precisely what makes the argument worth rescuing. The rhetorician was right, by accident or not. And so the purpose of this essay is to take Fetyukovich’s point seriously on its own terms and grounds to substantiate why it holds.

# II. The Moral Nature of A Father

Prior to addressing the argument proposed above, it is necessary to define the term "father" in the classical sense, so that the argument is able to meet the concept at its strongest, rather than a diminished version of it.

As the religious dimensions of 19th century Russia may not translate directly to contemporary society, I will refrain from anchoring the definition there. We will instead attempt to define the classical conception of fatherhood on its own terms. In this sense, we must understand that it is not simply a biological designation. Across the major traditions of the West and the East alike, fatherhood has always been understood as a tripartite structure: origin, authority, and formation.

The first layer — origin — is the biological fact of fatherhood. But, naturally, this would be too bare a foundation, and the ancients did not stop there. Aristotle, in the _Nicomachean Ethics_, treats the father-child relationship as one of natural inequality ordered toward the child's good. The father is not merely the other half of the child’s birth, but also a person whose role is fundamentally defined by what follows from that cause. The relationship is not symmetrical, and it is not optional. It is somewhat similar to partiality, in the sense that the origin generates the role and does not only precede it.

The second layer is authority. In the classical sense, this does not necessarily mean domination but rather stewardship. Across virtually every major cultural tradition, the father has been the breadwinner of the household: providing protection, structure, and an orientation toward society and the world. As such, he is the child’s first encounter with the fact that power can be directed towards care rather than arbitrary imposition. In Confucianism, it is among the highest of virtues because of the role of the father as the primary moral educator; for he is the person who the child essentially learns the obligations of social life from. In the Abrahamic traditions, the father is involved in a chain of authority that runs down from God — which is why, in the context of 19th century Russia, parricide carried a meaning that exceeded the merely murderer.

And lastly, the third layer, which I personally consider the deepest one as it practically justifies the other two: formation. A father, in the fullest classical sense, is the first experience that a child has of that of authority, but also love. Psychology can attest to this as the father is the child’s first model of how to be somebody in relation to others. Philosophy would say that the father is the ground of the earliest of moral formation, of how to inhibit the world alongside other people. Dostoevsky himself understood this, specifically through Zosima. Zosima's entire spiritual philosophy rests on the idea that the experience of being loved unconditionally is what makes the expansion of love outward possible at all. Such personal relation is the very foundation for the universal version of it.

The entirety of the argument above was to justify for why the charge of parricide was considered to never merely be a legal category. To kill your father was not merely to kill a man. It was, in the deepest sense the culture could articulate, to destroy the very axis of your own formation — perhaps even your own soul.

And, only after coming to this point, after understanding what the word was designed to mean, can we truly feel Fetyukovich’s question: _What if the axis was never there?_

# III. The Word Without the Substance

It is clear from the former section that the inherent structure of the word “father” is quite understandable, and perhaps even defensible on its own terms. But this is precisely where the relationship between Fyodor Karamazov and Dmitri Karamazov becomes relevant.

Fyodor Karamazov did not raise Dmitri. He did not protect him, know him, or want him in any meaningful sense. He outright forgot about Dmitri’s existence — and had it not been for Grigory, his butler, Dmitri would have died in neglect. Could we, in any meaningful sense, call that fatherhood? I do not think so. What Fyodor gave Dmitri was not fatherhood but its hollow precondition — a biological fact barely sufficient to install the title permanently, regardless of what followed from it.

In this particular case, we can see clearly how the word “father” is truly being used: normatively, not descriptively. And yet it carries within it the entire tripartite structure outlined above. When we call someone a father, we are not simply naming a genetic link, rather, we are invoking a role, and with it, the accumulated weight of every expectation that role entails. Parricide is uniquely horrifying for precisely this reason: it is understood as a violation of something sacred, something foundational to human development, committed by the very person that relationship was supposed to form. But the case of the Karamazovs exemplifies what actually happens when that foundation was never laid, being that the sacredness was never there, the authority was never exercised, the formation never occurred.

And yet the horror does not recalibrate accordingly. The charge does not diminish. Society still reaches for the full weight of the word, as the word has become self-justifying. It does not require the father to prove his fatherhood to activate its consequences for the son. The title alone is sufficient. The substance is simply assumed.

This is, I would argue, a profound moral error.

# IV. The Cathedral Around a Word

From the argument above, I believe we can come to the conclusion that society has built a cathedral around the world — completely forgot to check whether there was actually anything inside.

The word “father” in its classical/philosophical fullness is genuinely one of the most morally loaded terms in the human language. As demonstrated above, in its fullest form, it is arguably the most formative force in the human life. But that is not where the problem belies. The problem does not lie in whether the meaning of a “father” is extensive enough or not; it lies in the child that is abused by his dad but then demanded by relatives and society to care for the same person; it lies in the son that has never had a father for a singular moment yet suffers a punishment as if he had one. The problem is the assumption of equivalence between the biological fact of a father and the relational reality that goes with it when the history of human experience has offered us endless evidence that they may not correlate.

What Fetyukovich gestures toward, and what this essay has attempted to make explicit, is that this assumption is not merely philosophically sloppy. It is harmful in a specific and predictable way: it extends the protections of the full concept to those who hold only its biological shell, while simultaneously placing the full burden of the concept onto those who never received its substance. And so perhaps it is not just a matter concerning common knowledge or nature, but rather a concern of morality as well. For if we may look upon a person who has not ever in their life received care and love from their father and yet demand them to reciprocate feelings that they were never given access to in the first place then what are we ?

# V. Additional Remarks

I have a certain feeling that one may suggest a common counterargument to that proposed above that the framework allows the absent father to be able to argue that he does not have obligations to his child as he never acted alike one.

This argument basically commits the exact same moral error that we have spent the essay discussing about, merely in reverse. The societal "cathedral" assumes that the biological fact automatically generates the moral reality. The absent father, in this sense, assumes that the absence of the moral reality somehow also erases the biological fact. Both of these perspectives sever the fundamental connection between origin and relation. Both of them reduce fatherhood to a mere convenience for one to treat accordingly to their own whims. To recognize that biology alone is insufficient to make a father is not to say that the biological act carries no inherent obligation; rather, it is to say that abandoning that obligation does not free a man from the title, but rightfully condemns him for leaving the cathedral empty.
