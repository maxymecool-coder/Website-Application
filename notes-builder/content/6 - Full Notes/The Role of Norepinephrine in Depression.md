# The Role of Norepinephrine in Depression
**Details**
Norepinephrine (also known as noradrenaline) is a key neurotransmitter implicated in depression, primarily associated with the body's energy levels, arousal, and vigilance. It was one of the first neurotransmitters targeted by antidepressants, long before the modern focus on serotonin.

The "norepinephrine hypothesis" of depression, which emerged in the 1960s, proposed that a deficit of this chemical was a root cause of the illness. This theory was based on early medications that worked by either blocking norepinephrine's re-uptake or inhibiting the enzymes that break it down, both of which increase its availability in the synapse.

The most direct symptom linked to a norepinephrine deficit is **psychomotor retardation**. This is the profound physical and mental slowing, the overwhelming sense of exhaustion, and the inability to mobilize for action that characterizes many cases of major depression. When norepinephrine levels are low, the brain and body lack the necessary signals for alertness and energy, leading to a state of near-paralysis. This connection highlights how a specific chemical deficit can produce one of the most physically debilitating symptoms of the disease.
