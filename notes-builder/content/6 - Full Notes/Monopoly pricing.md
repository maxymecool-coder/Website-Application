# Monopoly pricing
**Details**
Perfect competition was an extreme; no real market is perfectly competitive. The next few lectures relax it for two reasons. Other structures are more realistic, and they introduce market failures—cases where a market left alone doesn't reach the efficient outcome. That's where government stops being only an agent of chaos and can sometimes improve things.

The opposite extreme is monopoly: one firm being the whole market's output. The key change is that a monopolist is a price maker, not a price taker (it doesn't take from the market; it decides the market). For now, Gruber adds one assumption: no price discrimination. The monopolist sets a single price for every unit it sells.

Marginal revenue: A competitive firm faces a flat demand curve at the market price, so one more unit always adds exactly P to revenue. A monopolist's demand is the whole market's demand, which slopes down. To sell one more unit, it must cut the price—and with a single price, it cuts it on every unit, not just the extra one. Going from Q units at P1 to Q + 1 units at the lower P2:

MR = P2 − (P1 − P2) × Q

The first term is what the new unit sells for. The second is what the price cut costs on the Q units it was already selling. Gruber calls this the **poisoning effect**: each extra sale poisons the earlier ones. In calculus, with revenue R = P(Q) × Q:

MR = dR/dQ = P + (dP/dQ) × Q

For a competitive firm dP/dQ = 0, so MR = P. For a monopolist dP/dQ is negative, so MR < P. The marginal revenue curve lies below the demand curve everywhere.

**Gruber's example.** Demand is Q = 24 − P, so P = 24 − Q and revenue is R = 24Q − Q², which gives MR = 24 − 2Q. For a straight-line demand curve, MR starts at the same intercept and falls twice as fast. (The shared intercept is special to straight lines; MR lying below demand is general.) Cost is C = 12 + Q², so MC = 2Q.

Profit maximization is MR = MC in every market structure. Here 24 − 2Q = 2Q, so **Q* = 6**.

The price is not 12, where MR and MC cross; that's the classic mistake. The monopolist sets the price but has to stay on the demand curve, because buyers won't pay more than it allows. So **P* = 24 − 6 = 18**. Average cost at 6 units is 12/6 + 6 = 8, so profit is (18 − 8) × 6 = 60.

|Units|Price|Revenue|Extra revenue|Cost|Extra cost|Profit|
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
|5|19|95||37||58|
|6|18|108|+13|48|+11|**60**|
|7|17|119|+11|61|+13|58|
|8|16|128|+9|76|+15|52|

Look at the 7th unit. Its buyer would pay 17, and it costs only about 13 to make. Yet the monopolist won't sell it. Selling 7 means charging 17 to everyone, which raises revenue by only 11 while cost rises 13, so profit falls from 60 to 58. The gain on the extra unit doesn't cover the price cut on the first six.
