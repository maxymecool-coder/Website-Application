# Using encyclopedias
**Details**
The intended role of an encyclopedia in the reading process is to fill gaps in background knowledge, clarify allusions, or explain concepts that a reader might not understand from context . Used judiciously, reference books provide essential context that makes complex or technical texts more intelligible , thereby supporting the reader’s understanding without doing the thinking for them. 

Adler and Van Doren caution, however, against misusing or over-relying on encyclopedias. They recount Adler’s early mistake of relying on “secondary sources, encyclopedias, commentaries, all sorts of books about books” to feign mastery of literature . This shortcut backfired, revealing that superficial knowledge from summaries cannot substitute for true comprehension of a work’s content. Likewise, the authors warn it is “just as vain to try to read an encyclopedia through as a dictionary,” noting that someone who memorized an encyclopedia verbatim would be a “learned fool” (an idiot savant) rather than a truly well-read person . In short, being widely read in reference materials is not the same as actively reading and understanding primary texts. 

Instead, encyclopedias should complement and enhance active reading. Adler and Van Doren describe dictionaries and encyclopedias as “educational as well as informational tools” —valuable adjuncts to reading that can illuminate a challenging book’s meaning. The key is not to “enslave yourself” to such aids . An active reader might pause to resolve a confusion by quickly consulting an encyclopedia, but should then return to the book’s argument or narrative with greater insight. In their view, a good reader uses encyclopedias to answer specific factual questions or to grasp necessary background, thereby removing obstacles to comprehension, but always resumes the primary task of engaging with the author’s ideas directly. 

This approach fits into Adler and Van Doren’s broader philosophy of active reading as learning by discovery. Reading is an active endeavor requiring the reader’s mental effort. Even reference tools should be used in that spirit. For example, they note that when such aids are used prematurely, they “only impede our reading, instead of helping it” . In practice, “reading a book with a dictionary in the other hand is a bad idea,” since constantly interrupting the flow makes it hard to grasp the whole . Instead, one should read as far as possible by one’s own lights and consult encyclopedias afterwards (or sparingly) to aid understanding, rather than let them replace the effort of reading. Ultimately, encyclopedias are there to support the reader’s engagement with difficult texts—providing context and clarification—while the real work of reading remains an active dialogue between reader and author.
