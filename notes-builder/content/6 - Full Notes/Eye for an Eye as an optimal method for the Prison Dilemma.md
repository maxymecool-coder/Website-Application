# Eye for an Eye as an optimal method for the Prison Dilemma
**Details**
In computer tournaments run by Robert Axelrod, the strategy that consistently won the Prisoner's Dilemma game was **Tit-for-Tat**. Its rules are simple:

1. **Be Nice:** Cooperate on the first move.
    
2. **Be Retaliatory:** If your opponent cheats, you cheat on the next move.
    
3. **Be Forgiving:** If your opponent returns to cooperation, you do too.
    
4. **Be Clear:** Your strategy is simple for your opponent to figure out.
    

This strategy "may lose the battles, but it wins the war." It is not exploitable and fosters long-term cooperation. Remarkably, this is not just a theoretical model. Biologists have found real-world examples in species from vampire bats (who refuse to share blood with bats that have "cheated" in the past) to stickleback fish (who refuse to "cooperate" with their own reflection if it appears to be lagging behind in a territorial defense).

**Supporting Content**
Thanks to the Axelrod's tournament, we can understand thoroughly why it is the optimal strategy. As the TfT users are able to help each other quadratically gain points whilst the naive nasty (instantly just manipulating) initially wins in one interaction, they would lose in the long run due to the both betraying situation giving less points than the both sharing. And this would be even worse when two "nasties" are pitted against each other.
