# What We Don't Know We Have
**Details**
Back in 2020 while most of the world was frozen in the uncertainty of a new pandemic, a seventeen-year-old from Seattle built a website. No one asked him to do this, it wasn’t for grades, least of all for pride. Avi Schiffman created ncov2019.live — a real-time COVID-19 tracking dashboard that, at its peak, was visited by tens of millions of people across the world. Later, when war broke out in Ukraine, he built Ukraine Take Shelter, a platform connecting refugees with available housing. He was still a teenager.

The natural instinct here is admiration. And that’s fair. But some of the most popular philosophical consensus would actually classify this as something natural and

## **What We Owe Each Other**

The philosopher Peter Singer offers one of the most well-known arguments in modern ethics. It goes roughly like this: if you can prevent something bad from happening, without sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, you ought to do it. It sounds simple. If you walk past a drowning child in a shallow pond, you should wade in and help — even if it ruins your shoes. Most people believes that is the fair and right thing to do. Singer then extends the logic outward. If you _can_ help people suffering on the other side of the world, and the cost to you is relatively small, then you _should_. Suffering doesn’t become less urgent or means less because of distance.

It’s a powerful argument. But I believe that Avi’s story complicates it.

Singer’s framework assumes that we already know what someone is capable of. The word “can” is doing enormous work in his argument. _If you can help, you should._ But what happens when nobody — not the world, not the people around you, possibly not even you yourself — knows that you _can_?

Before the pandemic, Avi Schiffman was a teenager who knew how to code. That’s it. No one could have looked at him and said, “You have an obligation to build a global health information platform that would help millions upon millions of people.” The capability didn’t exist as something visible, something that could be pointed at and turned into a duty. It only became real _in the act of doing it_.

This is where Singer’s framework fails. You simply cannot demand someone towards doing something they themselves do not realize yet.

## **Beyond Duty**

Ethics has a word for actions that are morally good but exceed what could reasonably be demanded of someone. The word is **supererogation**, which are acts beyond the call of duty. These are rare acts but we have seen examples of them nonetheless: Donating a kidney to a stranger; Running into a burning building when you’re not a firefighter. These are things we praise, but don’t blame people for not doing.

Avi’s case fits here in a strange way. What he did was clearly good. It clearly mattered. But it would be absurd to say he was _required_ to do it. No moral framework could have realistically demanded a teenager to build a website that would affect millions of people. The moral landscape before he acted simply didn’t contain that expectation.

Then, if it was not the demand or suggestion from his own capabilities and his own moral responsibility, then what is it ?

## **Noticing**

But this is where the gap comes. I believe that the current landscape of moral philosophy may attempt to explain this as the inherent abilities and knowledge that Avi has always had in of himself and only was precipitated due to the situation demanding said abilities.

Nonetheless, I do not think this actually solves the predicament that is Avi at all. These moral frameworks demand a person would have to understand and know what their abilities are and have enough knowledge about themselves to reliably make moral decisions (the epistemic condition). Avi never had that, he could have never known he was capable of having this kind of idea that worked better than even governments and non-governmental organizations.

So, it must have been something that came before said framework, or, in other words, his perception. He saw that people were confused and frightened, that reliable information was scattered, that the absence of something clear and centralized was itself causing harm. And crucially, he couldn't look away from it. Not because duty told him not to. Before any calculation, before any assessment of his own abilities, there was simply: this is wrong, and this gap is real.

## **Becoming Through Doing**

Aristotle argued, over two thousand years ago, that you don’t become virtuous by thinking about virtue. You become brave by doing brave things. You become generous by practicing generosity. Character isn’t something you have and then act from — it’s something you build _through_ action.

Avi’s story is a striking example of this. Before he built ncov2019.live, he was not yet the person who had done that. The experience of responding — of building something under pressure, of realizing millions of people were relying on what he made — shaped him into someone capable of doing it again when Ukraine needed help. The moral responsibility didn’t just demand something from him. It _developed_ him. It expanded what he was capable of. Is it in this situation that Singer’s framework would actually compel and demand that Avi do what he did.

Which is perhaps the largest point we can gain from examining his story. That moral responsibility might not be fixed, it might grow with our ability to do things, even if our ability depended on unfinished knowledge we have about our own selves.

## **The Uncomfortable Implication**

It would be simpler, easier to come out of this essay with a neat lesson: _you are capable of more than you think, so go out and do great things_. And perhaps that is partly true. But the honest conclusion is less comfortable than that.

What Avi’s story really tells us is that **we cannot know what we are capable of until we test our own capabilities**, but of course most of us do not exactly arrange our lives to encounter these tests or accept to act upon them in our daily lives. We wait for obligation. We wait for permission. We wait to feel ready. And in the waiting, the moment passes.

Singer’s framework tells us that we should act because we can, but the story above has already suggested something else: that we may not know what we can before we do it. And so the moral capability and responsibility that one endures is not merely a prerequisite for moral actions in certain situations but rather a consequence of it.

And so the implication that materializes from this is that we may have an ability to perform something moral perhaps beyond the scope of what we believe we can actually do, but said thing will only be realized if we ever attempt to do so.
