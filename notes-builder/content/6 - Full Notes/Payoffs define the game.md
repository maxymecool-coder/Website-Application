# Payoffs define the game
**Details**
Choices and outcomes alone don't make a game. The game's outcome matrix says who can choose what and which grades follow. It cannot be analyzed and strategized until you know the payoffs: what each player is trying to achieve, selfish or not. Once the payoffs are fixed, then the questions can change to what people should do. Then game theory is purely a tool to tell you how to get it.

Keep the same outcomes, change the payoffs, and you get a different game. Polak's "indignant angels" still like good grades, but two things cost them:
- An A won at a partner's expense costs them guilt, so 3 becomes −1.
- A C caused by a partner's defection costs them indignation, so −1 becomes −3.

Now nothing dominates. α is the better reply to α, and β is the better reply to β. This is a _coordination problem_. Both (α, α) and (β, β) are stable, since at either one neither player gains by switching alone, and both players prefer (β, β). The class split along two sensible rules. α protects the worst case (−1 rather than −3), while β goes for the best case (1 rather than 0). So moral feeling doesn't guarantee cooperation. It removes defection's dominance, which makes cooperation depend on what you expect the other person to do.

And so, in this situation, you would have to take into account the other person's perspective and work out what they will do. And so we will be able to see why:
- A purely self-interested player (an "evil git") facing an angel still plays α. α still dominates for them, so the partner's payoffs don't matter.
- An angel who knows they face a git has no dominant strategy. But they can see that the git's α dominates, so they expect α and reply with α.
