# Why We Stereotype
**Details**
The first part of this has already been explained through [[Heuristics as Rules of Thumb]], further tested by McCrae (1994), that we save mental effort through using stereotypes. McCrae's elegant study had subjects learn traits about "Julian" while simultaneously listening to a geography lesson. Half were told Julian was an artist. Those given the stereotype label remembered more stereotype-consistent traits about Julian AND performed better on the geography test—the opposite of the typical cognitive load tradeoff. The stereotype organized Julian's information so efficiently that it freed attentional resources for the secondary task. Study 2 replicated this with subliminal priming: subjects who couldn't consciously report Julian's occupation still showed enhanced memory organization.

On the other hand, it is also the motivation. Human beings are prone to social comparison, as mentioned before [[Social Comparison]]. Stereotypes allow us to enhance ourselves through it. Kunda's ambiguity study showed stereotypes shape construal: "hit someone who annoyed him" was rated more aggressive when performed by a construction worker than a housewife, but only for ambiguous descriptions—unambiguous acts (decking a neighbor, spanking a child) were rated equivalently regardless of actor. A separate ego-threat study demonstrated that after failing an anagram test, participants primed with Asian stereotypes produced more stereotype-consistent word completions than non-threatened participants—suggesting the brain automatically increases negative stereotyping of outgroups to repair damaged self-image, without conscious awareness or endorsement.
