# Discrepancy Model and Bias
**Details**
The traditional method for diagnosing learning disorders relies on the "discrepancy model," which requires a significant gap between a child's intelligence (IQ) and their academic achievement. Under this model, a child with a high IQ (e.g., 150) who reads at an average level (120) qualifies as having a disorder because they are underperforming relative to their potential. Conversely, a child with a slightly below-average IQ (e.g., 85) who reads at a correspondingly low level (80) does not qualify, as there is no discrepancy; their poor reading is attributed to their lower cognitive ability. This model was historically supported by the Isle of Wight studies by Rutter and Yule, which suggested that high-IQ poor readers ("Specific Reading Retardation") were distinct from low-IQ poor readers ("General Reading Backwardness"). However, modern research argues that the underlying phonological deficit is identical in both groups, challenging the validity of the discrepancy requirement.

This reliance though, may precipitate a bias in who gets treated. Imagine middle-high class kids who have a higher-than average IQ due to them having environmental advantages, they are viable for the diagnosis as their IQ is much higher than their reading score. Children of lower socioeconomic backgrounds may score lower due to the systematic factors, denying them of a diagnosis and not letting them getting the help that they need due to their reading score corresponding with their "IQ".
