# The Economic Model
**Details**
Usually, models in sciences explain through having exact universal constants or laws that apply perfectly everywhere. As it is a social science, it takes the characteristics of how models in other social sciences work, in the sense that it is not completely accurate. The aim is not to build a model that is literally complete, but one that is useful enough to explain something important while remaining simple enough to understand, teach, and solve. George Box’s line that “all models are wrong, but some are useful” captures the spirit of this method.

A major trade-off inside modeling is between explanatory richness and parsimony. A richer model may include many goods, many kinds of agents, uncertainty, institutions, habits, power, and imperfect information, but it can become impossible to solve or too complicated to see the core mechanism. A parsimonious model may reduce the world to two goods, two variables, or a simple linear relationship, which is obviously incomplete but can reveal the logic of the broader case. For example, introductory economics often models choices between two goods even though people actually choose among thousands of goods and activities. The defense is not that the two-good model is literally true, but that the intuition gained from two goods can often generalize to many goods with harder math and uglier graphs.
