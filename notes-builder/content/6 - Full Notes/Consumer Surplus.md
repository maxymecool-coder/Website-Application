# Consumer Surplus
**Details**
Positive economics describes how things are; normative economics asks how they should be. Until now, the course has been entirely positive. Take Lecture 8's result that long-run competitive profits are zero:

- For consumers, that's great: prices are as low as they can go, because firms produce at minimum average cost.
- For producers, it's bad, and maybe we care about firms too, since they're the ones who hire us.

To measure if this is good or not, we have to use “welfare economics,” which measures the wellness of society in its entirety.

The obvious move would be to use utility, but it is only ordinal. Only the ranking means anything, so utils can't be added up or compared. Economists for around this with something called “compensating variation.”Instead of asking how happy something makes you, ask how much you'd pay to get it, or how much you'd need to be paid to give it up. This is _revealed preference_: people show what they value through what they'd pay.

Consumer surplus is the benefit a customer gains from a good beyond what they paid for it. Gruber’s example is his favorite band’s tours:

If he'd pay only $90, he doesn't go.

- If he'd pay exactly $100, he's indifferent. He might go, but he gets no surplus.
- If he'd pay $300–400, which is more realistic for a first show, going leaves him $200–300 better off than if the concert had never happened.

The willingness to pay can be drawn as the demand curve here, the surplus would be the area below it and above the price.

Willingness to pay is just the demand curve, so consumer surplus is the area below the demand curve and above the price (Figure 9-6). Gruber's demand starts at about $500 for the first concert and falls to $100 by the fifth. Suppose it falls by $100 per concert. Then at a $100 ticket his surplus is 400 + 300 + 200 + 100 + 0 = $1,000, and the fifth concert adds nothing.

A student asked what surplus actually does for you. Nothing is stored up. It's simply a money measure of how much better off the purchase makes you. Anyone who has thought "what a deal, I'd have paid twice that" has felt it.

Market consumer surplus works the same way for the whole gas market (Figure 9-7). To keep it simple, suppose each person buys one gallon, so the quantity axis counts people, lined up from the most eager buyer to the least. At $3 a gallon, the 1,000th person (A) is indifferent. Everyone to their right doesn't buy, and everyone to their left gains surplus. The total is the triangle under demand and above $3.

Because demand curves can be written down and that area computed, there's now a measurable stand-in for utility. With it, you can make normative statements.

**A price rise** from $3 to $3.50 (Figure 9-8) costs consumers in two ways:

- The first 900 people still buy, but each pays 50 cents more. That's a rectangle: 900 × $0.50 = $450.
- People 901 to 1,000 stop buying and lose the surplus they had. That's a small triangle; if demand is a straight line there, it's ½ × 100 × $0.50 = $25.

The total loss is a trapezoid, $475 here. The lecture only draws the shapes; the dollar figures assume a straight-line demand curve between the two points.
