# The Synthesis of Experience
**Details**
At the very core of Immanuel Kant’s epistemology is the famous maxim: "Thoughts without content are empty, intuitions without concepts are blind." This captures his foundational argument that human knowledge requires an inextricably linked partnership between _sensibility_ (our capacity to receive sensory data) and _understanding_ (our capacity to deploy concepts). Without sensibility, no object is ever given to us; without understanding, no object can ever be thought.

And so Kant starts with the breakdown. First, he begins with raw sensation, the biological triggering of sensory organs cause of external stimuli. Through the a priori pure intuitions of space and time, these are then converted into appearances. But this is not where it stops. Kant believes that the information received from external stimuli is a chaotic, unorganized mess of sensory input. For it to become a cohesive reality, it must undergo the "original synthetic unity of apperception." This is basically the step where the mind goes through the concepts and what not (the understanding) to organize something cohesive.

Consider the everyday experience of a cup of coffee. The external world provides a disconnected manifold of sensations: a black color, a high temperature, a liquid viscosity, and a pungent aroma. The sensory organs receive these, but the synthesis of these elements into the unified object we recognize as "coffee" is an active imposition of the mind's conceptual framework. The unification is ours, the material of it is the world's.

**Supporting Content**
Additionally, Kant also argues that this is the "anthropology of knowledge," in the sense that the rules governing the conceptual synthesis are universal and necessary for all rational human beings.
