# Target being inertia, avoidance, fatigability
**Details**
The signs in depression that has been regarded with passivity and apparent enervation are a form of neuropsychological inhibition: psychomotor retardation. But research has shown that they can get out of this state if their motivation is aroused by the experimenter or therapist. And so we will make a personalized schedule for them to do so.

There are many positive advantages from a well-developed program. To list some of them are: (a) The patient's self-concept is changed as he will view himself to be more able and less inept. Concomitantly, with this improvement, he will become more optimistic. (b) He is distracted from his thoughts because he is focusing his attention on the activity instead. (c) Other people's responses to the patient become more positive; their significant others will reinforce their development in a beneficial way. (d) There may be a change in the patient's response, they may enjoy the activities and thus feel better.

Of course we will also have to be vigilant of the motivation necessary for the activity, and so we must stimulate it. The therapist doing this must be careful and meticulous in his methods to do so. There are some recommendations: Firstly, the therapist must elicit the patient's reasons for his inactivity, by giving them something that is well in their ability to do and find the "reasons" that they state for not doing it. Then we will figure out an activity for the patient to do to contradict his reasons.

And now to nudge the patient, we must gently present the rationale for the particular procedure, and conveying to the patient that it is a less painful alternative to feeling as bad as he does by cooperating with us. Then we must make the patient realize how his thoughts are making him more vulnerable and miserable, and that by questioning these ideas, he is likely to feel better.

It is crucial that the therapist pose his questions and assertions regarding the patient’s self-defeating ideas and wishes in a nonjudgmental, reflective way. He should avoid the appearance of scolding the patient, and making him defensive. Because depressed patients generally respond to “criticism” with further self-criticisms and self-immobilization, the therapist should elicit the patient’s reactions to his statements; he should determine whether the patient is using the therapist’s comments “against himself.” 

The therapist, furthermore, should be vigilant lest his statements be construed simply as a summons to invoke “the power of positive thinking.” He should indicate clearly that he and the patient are attempting to pinpoint a problem and provide a remedy for it. Global exhortations to “think positively” rarely help to correct unreasonable negative thinking and thus lead the patient to a further sense of failure. 

After outlining his pragmatic approach, the therapist should encourage the patient to state his points of agreement and disagreement. This approach may seem fairly obvious and superficial, and one might reasonably wonder how it could be effective in counteracting the patient’s powerful regressive wishes and negative ideas. But this is not the purpose of this procedure yet, it is only here to help the patient recognize his thoughts without the therapist's help.

When the therapist is able to penetrate the patient’s resistances and engage his interest, he then proceeds to the next stage in the therapeutic program. The therapist should not be content with simply producing cognitive dissonance, but must insure that whatever advantage is gained through discussion is followed immediately by action. 

The therapeutic program may be formulated in terms of the following steps: (a) proposing a specific project to the patient; (b) eliciting his reasons for opposing the proposal; (c) asking the patient to weigh the validity of his “reasons” (or negative attitudes); (d) indicating to the patient why these reasons (or attitudes) are self-defeating and invalid; (e) stimulating the patient’s interest in attempting to perform the proposed assignment; (f) setting up the project in such a way that the patient’s performance will test the validity of his ideas. Thus, successful completion of the task will contradict the patient’s hypothesis that he is incapable of doing it; (g) after the successful experience, indicating to the patient how the favorable outcome contradicts his self-defeating predictions; (h) reviewing with the patient the “constructive” attitudes proposed previously by the therapist. These formulations may be used later by the patient to combat his negativity; (i) training the patient to give reasonable answers to these thoughts. This technique is implemented through home work assignments.

The homework is a three column worksheet where the patient writes down the situation that elicited an unpleasant feeling, the negative automatic thought, and then the correction to that thought.
