# Reproductive Effects and Warnings of Tobacco
**Details**
The toxicity of tobacco extends beyond the user to the developing fetus, leading to specific medical classifications. "Fetal Tobacco Syndrome" is one of the few medically diagnosed syndromes caused by drug consumption during pregnancy (alongside Fetal Alcohol Syndrome). Tobacco use during pregnancy correlates with a 50% decrease in fertility, a 50% increase in spontaneous abortion (miscarriage), and a higher incidence of premature birth and perinatal mortality.

The mechanism is largely attributed to the hypoxic effects of carbon monoxide and the vasoconstrictive effects of nicotine, which starve the developing fetus of oxygen and nutrients. Consequently, infants are often born with significantly lower birth weights. Furthermore, there is a strong statistical association between environmental/maternal tobacco smoke and Sudden Infant Death Syndrome (SIDS).

Public policy attempts to mitigate these risks through warning labels, though the efficacy and implementation vary globally. In the United States, warning labels have historically been small, text-based, and placed on the side of packaging (e.g., warning of CO or low birth weight). In contrast, European and other international regulations often mandate large, graphic warnings that cover a significant portion of the package (e.g., "Smoking Kills"). This disparity highlights the ongoing tension between public health advocacy and the entrenched political and economic power of the tobacco industry, a legacy stretching back to the American colonies.
