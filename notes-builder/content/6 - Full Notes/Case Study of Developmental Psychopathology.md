# Case Study of Developmental Psychopathology
**Details**
The story of Stephen Hinshaw's father, Virgil Hinshaw Jr., serves as a poignant case study in developmental psychopathology. Virgil experienced a significant traumatic experience when he lost his mother at the early age of three. Developmental psychopathology posits that the loss of a parent before age five is a risk factor for future mood disorders; however, this is usually modulated by protective factors. Unfortunately, Virgil lacked these protections: his father remarried a missionary woman who subjected him to severe physical abuse. This created a specific "non-shared environment" where he suffered differently than his brothers, allowing the vulnerability for illness to take root.

The culmination of these risk factors occurred in 1936 when he was 16. Amidst a family atmosphere charged with his father's work in the **Prohibition movement**, and rising political anxiety regarding fascism in Europe, Virgil experienced his first manic episode. Triggered by sleep deprivation and the late-summer seasonality often associated with the onset of mania, he developed a grandiose delusion that he could stop the fascists by flying. This culminated in a psychotic break where he jumped off a porch roof, resulting in physical injury and immediate institutionalization. Because the distinction between bipolar disorder and schizophrenia was not well recognized at the time, he was misdiagnosed with the latter.

As discussed in [[Development of Asylums]], institutional care during this era was primitive, and this case was no different. Virgil was restrained to his bed nightly and received no medical intervention when he stopped eating due to a delusion that the food was poisoned. His family was only contacted long after his condition had deteriorated. Eventually, Virgil was released due to "improving" spontaneously. However, the family never discussed the event, adhering to a strict code of silence driven by [[Stigma]].

Virgil went on to excel academically, completing his PhD at Princeton. However, the stress of his doctoral dissertation combined with relationship troubles triggered a second severe psychotic episode in 1945. He was admitted to Philadelphia State Hospital (Bybury), infamous for its deplorable conditions and immortalized in _The Snake Pit_. During a visit, Virgil refused to leave, insisting the road signs were in German and that he was in a concentration camp. At the time, this was viewed as a total break from reality.

Years later, a historical re-examination of the hospital suggests this delusion had a grain of truth. Photographs of the men's dormitory reveal conditions shockingly similar to the concentration camps of Europe; his psychosis was, in part, a reaction to his dehumanizing environment.

Despite these struggles, Virgil was able to marry and secure tenure as a philosophy professor. He possessed protective factors (intelligence, resilience) that allowed him to function where others might not have. However, the "rule of silence" persisted. His wife was only informed of his history after he became manic during her pregnancy. Furthermore, the genetic risk was evident in the family tree: other brothers struggled with substance abuse, and several nephews succumbed to severe mental illness or suicide.

Despite being a loving father, the cycle of silence affected the next generation. Stephen Hinshaw was kept in the dark about the true nature of his father's absences because psychiatrists of the era believed discussing mental illness would "ruin" the children. Consequently, the children were left to internalize the confusion, only learning the truth after they had left for college.

**Supporting Content**
And so what conclusion can we get from this ? Telling your children about your conditions and problems can significantly impact the risks of them developing mental disorders down the line. Proven by a multitude of case studies, the rate at which people suffer from mental illnesses later in their adult lives actually halves if their parents educated them about how they suffered mentally.
