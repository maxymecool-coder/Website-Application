# Medications in PTSD
**Details**
Drugs are usually used to deal with traumatic stress, but replacement for those when they can’t legally obtain drugs like gin, vodka and such are much more popular. It makes sense, people are desperate to get anything to feel calmer and more in control.

The thing is, these cannot “cure” trauma; they can only dampen the expressions of a disturbed physiology. They do not teach patients how do deal with the effects that trauma has the body and the mind. They can help to control feelings and behavior, but at a price, they work by blocking the chemical systems that regulate engagement, motivation, pain, and pleasure. 

Practically every group of medications have been used to treat some aspect of PTSD. 

SSRIs are the one that have been studied the most, they make feelings less intense and life more manageable. But some report that it blunts them, that they feel like they’re “losing their edge”. If one SSRI does not work, it is advised to try another as they normally have different effects. Van der Kolk has experimented with this in comparison with EMDR, the result turned out that EMDR was more effective than the SSRIs.

Continuing with the theme, medicines that target the autonomic nervous system, can help decrease hyperarousal and reactivity to stress. This family of drugs helps block the physical effects of adrenaline, the fuel of arousal, and thus reduces nightmares, insomnia, and reactivity to trauma triggers. 

Next up, tranquilizing drugs, benzodiazepines. They work like alcohol somewhat, in which they make people feel calm and keeps them from worrying. But also, they work like alcohol in the negative sense, making people less careful, say more hateful things even to their love ones. It’s generally ill advised to prescribe these drugs, as they have high addiction potential and interfere with trauma processing. Patients who use the drugs also report to have withdrawal reactions after stop using them.

Lastly, anticonvulsants and mood stabilizers, can have mildly positive effects, taking the edge off hyperarousal and panic. The most controversial medications are the second-generation antipsychotic agents. These can calm down combat veterans and women with PTSD related to childhood abuse. It is important to keep in mind that these medications block the dopamine system, so no pleasure, motivation. But moreover, these antipsychotic medications can dampen the emotional brain, which may interfere with being able to appreciate subtle signals of pleasure, danger, or satisfaction.
