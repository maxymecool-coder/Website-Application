# Structured Interview
**Details**
Assessments are what helps the diagnostician collect evidence and data to lead to a diagnosis. Firstly though, before getting too invasive and cold when talking about the symptoms and what they are looking for, a well-trained diagnostician would first try to build a relationship with the patient as it will ensure trust in the space and in the interviewer so that the patient will be more truthful and open to them about their background later on. But there are differences between a normal talk and this, which is called a clinical interview. In these interviews, the interviewer will generally avoid sensitive topics that can evoke something in the patient and only talking about it when they’ve strengthened their relationship enough (or rapport). These interviews can vary in the aspect of how much structuring they have.

At times, mental health professionals may need to collect standardized information so that they can make diagnostic judgements based on the DSM. To meet that criteria, they will use a structured interview, in which the questions are prescribed fashionably for the interviewer. One commonly used form of this is the Structured Clinical Interview (SCID) for Axis I of the DSM-IV (the DSM-5 will revise these structures).

Another important assessment is stress, which is related to psychosocial and environmental aspects of the DSM. There are of course self-report checklists that people can do which is not as good as the other methods. Some other methods is interviewing (LEDS: Life and Events Difficulties Schedule), where it is basically a slow talk about the patient’s live events in the last few periods of time in their life.
