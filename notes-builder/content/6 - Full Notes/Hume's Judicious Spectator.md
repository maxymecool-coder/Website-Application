# Hume's Judicious Spectator
**Details**
The first to ground moral philosophy in social sciences and society in general rather than by natural law or through God. Hume wasn't interested in why property ought to exist morally but why it came to serve social purposes. To do this, he introduced the concept of the "judicious spectator." This is an imaginative, reasoning perspective we learn to adopt through socialization, where we evaluate actions based purely on a shared principle of humanity. It is an explanation of the psychological mechanics of morality rather than a judgment of it.

**Supporting Content**
Decades later, John Stuart Mill inherited this empirical, utilitarian lineage, but his approach to it was deeply colored by his unique, traumatic upbringing. Raised as an intellectual prodigy by his father (James Mill) and Jeremy Bentham, Mill was deprived of a normal childhood, serving instead as a tutor to his siblings. This bred a deep, albeit repressed, resentment. Interestingly, Mill's chosen vocation was not to be a pure, isolated scholar, but rather an "educator of advanced and enlightened opinion." Influenced by Saint-Simon, Mill believed society was entering a secular, democratic, and industrial "organic age," and he wanted to provide the foundational principles for this new era. However, because of his complicated psychological relationship with his father, Mill could never bring himself to openly break from classical utilitarianism. Instead, he constantly presented his own highly original—and often contradictory—revisions of moral philosophy under the guise of simply updating and defending the utilitarian tradition.
