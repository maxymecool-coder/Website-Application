# The two faces of Amnesia
**Details**
Amnesia, the clinical term for memory loss, is not a single condition but manifests in two primary forms, defined by their relationship to the time of a brain injury or event.

1. **Retrograde Amnesia:** This is the loss of memories formed _before_ the event. It's a failure to retrieve from the past. This is the type of amnesia most commonly depicted in fiction—the character who wakes up with no knowledge of their identity or past life. In reality, it is often "graded," meaning that more recent memories are lost while distant, older memories (like from childhood) remain intact. This suggests that over time, memories become less dependent on the hippocampus and more firmly stored in the cortex.
    
2. **Anterograde Amnesia:** This is the inability to form _new_ long-term memories _after_ the event. It is a failure to encode the present for the future. Patient H.M. and Clive Wearing are classic examples. They could remember their distant pasts but were trapped in a perpetual present, unable to convert new experiences into lasting memories.
    

While Hollywood favors retrograde amnesia for its dramatic potential, anterograde amnesia is the more common and often more devastating clinical reality following significant damage to the hippocampus.
