# Price ceilings and rationing
**Details**
[[The market is always right]] has shown us how a competitive market maximizes total surplus. Now we come to the interventions part of it. In a world of perfectly competitive markets, the market knows best, and government can only make things worse (as Gruber says). Later lectures show when government isn't the bad guy.

First, a shock that isn't an intervention (Figure 10-1). A war in the Middle East (he's speaking in October 2023) raises the cost of getting oil. The supply curve for gas shifts up, and the market moves from E1 to E2 at a higher price. Measured against E1:

- Consumer surplus falls from A + B + D + E to just A.
- Producers gain B from the higher price but lose F + G on the gallons no longer sold, so producer surplus goes from C + F + G to B + C.
- Total surplus falls by D + E + F + G.

That loss is real, because oil has genuinely become harder to get. But it isn't a market failure. Once the war has happened, E2 is the best the market can do.

Now politics. Voters' sense of inflation is shaped heavily by gas prices, and President Biden faced re-election in about a year. Suppose the government caps the price at the old level, P1 — in Gruber's numbers, $3.60 a gallon instead of letting it rise toward $4 or $4.50 (Figure 10-2).

At P1, consumers want Q1 gallons, but producers will only supply Q3, the amount on their supply curve at that price. Since the price can't adjust, the quantity will be set by whichever side wants to trade less. Which means here the suppliers decide, and so the market sits at E3 with excess demand. The right comparison is E3 against E2, not E1. The war has happened; E1 is gone. Gruber's areas:

|   |   |   |
|---|---|---|
||E2 (no cap)|E3 (cap at P1)|
|Consumer surplus|V + Y|V + W|
|Producer surplus|W + Z + X|X|

- **W is a transfer** from producers to consumers: the lower price on the gallons still sold.
- **Y + Z is deadweight loss**: the gallons between Q3 and Q2 that buyers and sellers would both gladly trade but aren't allowed to. Gruber's analogy is two kids who each want the other's playing card while a parent forbids the swap. Both are worse off.

That's the first cost. The second is new: allocative inefficiency from rationing. In equilibrium, the price decides who gets the good. Everyone who values a gallon above the price buys it, and nobody else does. Under the cap, Q1 is wanted but only Q3 exists, so something other than price has to decide who gets it. In the 1970s, after the OPEC cartel cut oil supplies (cartels come later in the course), the US capped gas prices. The result was long lines at gas stations, with waits of 15–20 minutes or more. That waiting is pure waste: time that could have gone to work or leisure. There was a smaller second waste too: cars that got about 8 miles to the gallon burned gas idling in line for gas. So what is it? [[Utility in Economics]]

The supposed benefit is the transfer W to consumers, whether you call it equity or political calculation. But with welfare defined as consumer surplus plus producer surplus, transfers count for nothing, so the cap is pure loss. In reality, we might accept some inefficiency to get goods to people we think deserve them more. That's the equity–efficiency trade-off, which the course returns to later.

**Supporting Content**
Prices without money: Feeding America distributes donated food to food banks across the US. Allocating it without prices, it ended up doing things like sending potatoes to Idaho, the country's potato capital. So it built a virtual market. Each food bank got a budget of points and bid for the loads it wanted; a bank that badly needed potatoes could spend more of its points on them. No real money changes hands, and the food stays free, but the bidding reveals who values what most. That's the job prices do in a market.
