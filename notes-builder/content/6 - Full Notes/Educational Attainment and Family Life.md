# Educational Attainment and Family Life
**Details**
Statistics have shown interesting correlations between these two values, specifically about how stable a family is with the level of education a person receive. The divergence is no longer subtle; it is a chasm. Data on adults nearing the end of their primary child-rearing years (ages 46-54) shows that 61% of those with a bachelor's degree or higher are still in their first marriage. This figure plummets to just 30% for those with less than a high school diploma, with other educational groups falling in between. The stability of marriage, once a more broadly shared norm, is now increasingly correlated with higher education.

This pattern is further reflected in the experiences of children. Since the 1980s, the rate of children living with a single parent has remained low and relatively stable for college-educated families. For every other group, it has risen dramatically. For white families with only a high school education, the rate has more than doubled to around 20%, and for non-white families without a high school degree, it approaches 40%. This fracturing of family life is not a result of differing moral values but is deeply tied to the economic shifts that have eroded the foundation of the working class. The disappearance of stable, well-paying manufacturing and industrial jobs—the kind that could support a family with only a high school diploma—has left less-educated individuals, particularly men, with precarious employment, making the long-term commitment of marriage a far riskier proposition. Consequently, the stable, two-parent household is becoming a marker of class privilege, accessible primarily to those with the educational credentials to secure a place in the modern economy.
