# Reid, Common Sense, and Why Kant Dismissed It
**Details**
Thomas Reid was a person of first principles, he was a person that above all, cared about how something, how everything has a reason, or reasons. You can't prove the law of contradiction because all proof presupposes it. Likewise, in physics, you also assume that it always apply, if your car doesn't start tomorrow, then its the car not working, not that the laws of combustion has been suspended.

Reid turned Hume against himself. Hume argued causation is just a mental habit from repeated experience — "anything may be the cause of anything" — then admitted he thinks like an ordinary person once he leaves his study. Reid's response: a philosophy that contradicts everything required for functioning life will have a very brief shelf life. People will smile at the clever philosopher and get on with things.

Even though this closely resembles Kant's transcendalism, Kant still criticized Reid nonetheless as consulting the wisdom of the herd, of the masses rather than something that he figured out himself. The lecturer argues, following Manfred Kuehn, that this dismissal is strategic rather than philosophical: Kant was fighting a war _within German philosophy_ between serious systematic philosophy (_Schulphilosophie_) and accessible popular philosophy (_Popularphilosophie_). Reid's common sense approach looked too much like the popular side, so Kant needed distance from it — even though the actual arguments have significant overlap.

In this sense, Kant was blind in the sense that these were not exactly arguments, but rather presuppositions that rational people would have to believe, which corresponded more strongly to his transcendalism than he admits.
