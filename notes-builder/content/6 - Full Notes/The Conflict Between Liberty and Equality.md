# The Conflict Between Liberty and Equality
**Details**
Within the democratic thought there resides a conflict between the competing claims of liberty and equality. Rawls frames this using Benjamin Constant's famous distinction (Constant lived roughly 1770–1830). The Lockean tradition emphasizes what Constant called "the liberties of the moderns" - freedom of speech, thought, conscience, and various civil rights of equal citizenship. The Rousseauian tradition emphasizes "the liberties of the ancients" — the political liberties of participation, the values of public life, and active self-governance, modeled on the adult male citizens of Athenian democracy.

Both traditions recognize these values but they treat them very differently. This discrepancy is basically from how differently the people in these traditions view and think about these concepts and how it actually should be treated.

Rawls then maps two great philosophical traditions onto this divide: the social contract tradition (Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau, Kant) and the utilitarian tradition (Hume, Bentham, J.S. Mill, Sidgwick). The latter is quite formidable due to its dominance over the English economists theorists from 1750 to 1900, making it not only a moral-political doctrine but a comprehensive social-scientific framework. It remains, in some form, the dominant view. And many philosophers have come to challenge this viewpoint, John Rawls included.
